# Knowledge Objects

## 1.5.1 Consciousness

## 1. Purpose
Establish consciousness as the broad psychological phenomenon of being aware and having subjective experience, while separating it from specific contents, processes, and states.

## 2. Definition
Consciousness is the condition or set of processes associated with subjective experience and awareness of oneself, the environment, or ongoing mental activity. It is studied through what people report, what they can access or control, and observable behavioral and neural correlates. **What it is not:** consciousness is not identical to attention, wakefulness, self-awareness, or any single proposed neural mechanism.

## 3. Core Understanding
Consciousness is multidimensional rather than a single simple switch. Questions about its contents, level, access, and neural correlates can be separated. A person may be awake yet have limited awareness of particular information, and conscious experience can vary in clarity and content. Different scientific theories emphasize different mechanisms, including global availability, recurrent processing, higher-order representations, or other proposed architectures; these remain theoretical frameworks rather than interchangeable facts. A useful analysis separates phenomenal experience, information that becomes globally available for cognition, and behavioral evidence used to infer either. These dimensions can covary without being identical, so a finding about reportability should not automatically be generalized to the full nature of consciousness. This distinction also prevents a proposed neural correlate from being mistaken for a complete explanation of the phenomenon.


A deeper analysis separates whether experience exists, what its contents are, and what information is available for flexible cognition. These dimensions can dissociate, so behavioral report is evidence about a conscious state rather than a definition of consciousness. Consciousness is also temporally structured: experience has changing contents, transitions, continuity, and interruptions rather than existing as a static object. Scientific theories differ over whether recurrent interaction, global availability, higher-order representation, or other architectures are necessary or sufficient. None should be presented as an established identity between a mechanism and experience. Sleep, anesthesia, neurological, developmental, and altered-state evidence can constrain theories, but each paradigm measures selected aspects. Precise claims therefore identify the operational level being explained and avoid moving directly from a neural correlate to a complete account of subjective experience.
## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Organizes the chapter's distinctions among experience, awareness, access, processing modes, sleep-related consciousness, and altered states.
## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Owns the general psychological construct of consciousness. Defers specific states, processes, measurement methods, and neural mechanisms to their specialized Topics and to Chapter 1.3 where biological mechanisms are primary.

### Defers
Defers awareness, subjective experience, self-awareness, access, processing modes, and measurement to dedicated Topics; biological implementation to Chapter 1.3.
## 6. Key Principles
- Consciousness concerns subjective experience and awareness but is not reducible to one proposed mechanism.
- Conscious contents, access, level, and state should be distinguished.
- Scientific explanations of consciousness remain theory-dependent.

## 1.5.2 Subjective experience

## 1. Purpose
Clarify the first-person aspect of psychological life that makes consciousness a phenomenon of experience rather than only observable behavior.

## 2. Definition
Subjective experience is the first-person character of what a mental or bodily event is like for the individual experiencing it. It includes qualities of perception, thought, emotion, bodily feeling, and other conscious states. **What it is not:** it is not simply a report, behavior, or neural measurement; those are ways of studying or expressing experience.

## 3. Core Understanding
Subjective experience is inherently experienced from a particular perspective. Two people can receive similar stimulation yet report different experiences, and the same person can experience the same stimulus differently across contexts or states. Scientific psychology cannot directly observe another person's experience; it infers it from reports, behavior, physiological measures, and other correlates. First-person reports are therefore important data but can be incomplete, biased, or difficult to interpret. The existence of subjective experience is distinct from any particular theory about how the brain produces it. Its scientific treatment therefore requires triangulation rather than assuming that verbal report is a transparent window into experience. Report accuracy can depend on memory, language, response criteria, and the timing of measurement. Comparing reports with behavior and physiological or neural measures can constrain interpretation, but none of these measures should be treated as a perfect substitute for first-person experience.


Subjective experience has qualitative and structural properties: sensory quality, intensity, affective tone, temporal organization, bodily location, intentional content, and sense of self can vary. These properties can change independently. Reports are temporally fragile because describing an experience after it occurs introduces memory and reconstruction. Immediate reports, confidence measures, behavioral discrimination, and physiological indicators therefore answer different questions. The scientific challenge is not to choose between first-person and third-person evidence, but to determine how different measures constrain one another. Individual and cultural differences can affect how experiences are described without necessarily implying identical differences in the underlying experience. A rigorous account keeps phenomenology, report, and measurement analytically separate.
## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Provides the first-person phenomenological layer needed to interpret consciousness, dreams, hypnosis, meditation, and altered states without confusing experience with its measurement.
## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Owns the general concept of first-person psychological experience. Defers conscious access, awareness, self-awareness, and measurement to their specialized Topics.

### Defers
Defers consciousness to 1.5.1 and self-related structures to Chapter 1.14; does not equate phenomenology with report.
## 6. Key Principles
- Subjective experience is first-person and cannot be equated with its measurement.
- Reports provide evidence about experience but are not identical to experience itself.
- The scientific explanation of why experience occurs remains theoretically contested.

## 1.5.3 Awareness

## 1. Purpose
Define awareness as a person's availability of information to conscious experience or report without equating it with all consciousness.

## 2. Definition
Awareness is the condition in which information, events, internal states, or aspects of the environment are available to conscious experience or report. **What it is not:** awareness is not identical to attention; attention can influence what becomes available, but awareness and attention are conceptually distinct.

## 3. Core Understanding
Awareness can concern external events, internal bodily states, thoughts, emotions, memories, or aspects of one's own behavior. It varies in degree and can be selective. Information can influence behavior without becoming consciously reportable, which is one reason awareness must be distinguished from processing in general. Operational definitions vary by task: researchers may infer awareness through verbal report, discrimination, confidence, or other measures. No single measure captures every form of awareness. Awareness can be operationalized at different granularities, from detection of a stimulus to awareness of a complex internal state. A person may detect something without identifying it precisely, or may have a vague experience without being able to report its source. These distinctions matter because apparently conflicting studies may use different criteria for what counts as awareness.


Awareness is better treated as selective availability than as a single global property. A person can be aware that something happened without knowing its exact identity, cause, or implications. Awareness can be focal: information may be consciously represented while concurrent information remains outside awareness. Experimental paradigms manipulate stimulus visibility, masking, competition, task relevance, and timing while measuring objective discrimination and subjective awareness. Dissociations are informative but difficult to interpret because no awareness measure is perfect. Confidence, forced-choice performance, free report, and metacognitive judgments can produce different thresholds. Awareness also has a temporal dimension: information can become conscious after an initial nonconscious stage and later fade while leaving residual effects.
## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Bridges subjective experience with experimentally testable availability of information and contrasts directly with unconscious processing.
## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Owns the general construct of awareness. Defers self-awareness, conscious access, unconscious processing, and consciousness measurement to their specialized Topics.

### Defers
Defers attention to Chapter 1.6 and conscious access to 1.5.6; owns awareness rather than all processing.
## 6. Key Principles
- Awareness concerns availability to conscious experience or report.
- Processing can occur without awareness.
- Awareness is task- and definition-dependent in scientific measurement.

## 1.5.4 Consciousness-specific self-awareness

## 1. Purpose
Define self-awareness specifically as awareness of one's own ongoing experience or mental state within consciousness, without taking ownership from the broader self construct.

## 2. Definition
Consciousness-specific self-awareness is conscious awareness directed toward one's own ongoing experiences, thoughts, feelings, bodily states, or mental activity. **What it is not:** it is not the entire psychological construct of the self or self-concept.

## 3. Core Understanding
Self-awareness can involve noticing one's own thoughts, emotions, bodily sensations, actions, or current mental state. It can be momentary and situational rather than a stable personality characteristic. Attention, context, emotion, and social evaluation can affect how strongly self-related information enters awareness. The broader psychological self includes representations and structures that are not necessarily conscious at any given moment. Scientific measures therefore distinguish momentary self-awareness from broader self-knowledge and self-related constructs. Momentary self-awareness can also change with attention, social context, emotional salience, and task demands. Increased self-focus does not necessarily produce more accurate self-knowledge; monitoring itself can be selective and interpretive. Keeping this construct narrow protects the chapter from absorbing the broader literature on identity, self-concept, or self-esteem.


Momentary self-awareness can be directed toward internal events or toward the self as an object of observation. It can include noticing an emotional state, recognizing that one is thinking, monitoring bodily sensations, or recognizing one's own action. Self-focus can increase access to some information while narrowing attention to other information, and heightened self-monitoring does not guarantee accuracy. It is temporally variable, increasing during evaluation, conflict, novelty, or emotionally salient events and receding during absorption. Self-awareness is not identical to self-knowledge, self-conscious emotion, identity, or self-esteem. Its canonical role here is specifically the conscious registration of one's own ongoing mental activity.

## 1.5.5 Levels of consciousness

## 1. Purpose
Explain how consciousness can vary in degree, organization, or availability rather than existing as one all-or-none state.

## 2. Definition
Levels of consciousness are differences in the degree or organization of conscious responsiveness, awareness, and experience. **What it is not:** a level is not necessarily a single universal numerical scale of consciousness.

## 3. Core Understanding
Conscious functioning can vary across wakefulness, sleep, sedation, anesthesia, severe brain dysfunction, and other conditions. Different clinical and experimental frameworks operationalize these differences using responsiveness, behavioral signs, reports, or physiological measures. A change in one dimension does not always mean an identical change in all others: responsiveness, awareness, and specific conscious contents can dissociate. Consequently, claims about a person's “level” of consciousness must be tied to the measure and context used. Because different dimensions can dissociate, apparent changes in responsiveness should be interpreted with the specific assessment procedure in view. Behavioral responsiveness can be reduced for reasons that do not map neatly onto subjective experience, while preserved behavior does not by itself prove a particular conscious state. The construct is therefore most useful as a dimensional description tied to explicit criteria.


“Level” is a shorthand for a multidimensional profile. Responsiveness, arousal, awareness of environment, awareness of self, complexity of experience, and purposeful behavior can vary independently enough that a single ladder conceals important distinctions. Sleep illustrates this: dreaming can contain rich experience despite reduced responsiveness to the external environment, while transitional states can contain mixtures of wake-like and sleep-like features. Anesthesia and severe neurological conditions further show why behavior alone can be an imperfect proxy. Measurement must specify whether the target is behavioral responsiveness, subjective report, physiological arousal, or another dimension. The construct is most useful as a descriptive coordinate system rather than a claim that consciousness has one universally agreed biological scale.
## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Provides the dimensional bridge among waking, sleep, sedation, and altered states rather than a single universal ladder.
## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Owns the general dimensional idea of variation in consciousness. Defers sleep stages, altered states, clinical disorders, and measurement instruments to their specialized owners.

### Defers
Defers sleep stages to 1.5.20–1.5.21, altered states to 1.5.34, and clinical consciousness conditions to Chapter 1.19.
## 6. Key Principles
- Consciousness can vary along multiple dimensions.
- Responsiveness and subjective awareness are related but not identical.
- Any claimed level depends on the operational definition used.

## 1.5.6 Conscious access

## 1. Purpose
Explain how information becomes available to conscious report, reasoning, or flexible control.

## 2. Definition
Conscious access is the availability of information to conscious awareness and potentially to deliberate report or flexible cognitive use. **What it is not:** it is not synonymous with the initial processing of a stimulus.

## 3. Core Understanding
Information can be processed at multiple stages. Some information influences behavior without broad conscious access, whereas information that gains access can often be reported, maintained, combined with other information, or used flexibly. The conditions producing access depend on stimulus strength, attention, competing information, task demands, and the architecture proposed by the relevant theory. Measures of reportability are useful but do not by themselves settle what consciousness fundamentally is. Access is often studied through contrasts between information that can be flexibly reported or used and information that produces narrower effects. This makes access a functional construct rather than a synonym for vividness. Experimental conclusions depend strongly on timing, masking, task demands, and the criterion used to decide whether information was consciously available.


Conscious access is often studied as a transition from locally available information to information that can be flexibly used across tasks. Access can be limited by masking, competition, insufficient evidence, or attentional selection, but attention and access should not be assumed to have identical mechanisms. A useful distinction is between information that merely changes a narrow response and information that can be intentionally reported, maintained, compared, or integrated with unrelated information. Timing is critical because early stimulus effects and later access can occur at different stages. Theories disagree about which neural interactions constitute access, so findings should be described at the functional level unless evidence directly supports a mechanism. Access is not equivalent to vividness.
## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Connects awareness with information that can be flexibly used, forming a functional boundary between conscious and nonconscious processing.

### Defers

## 6. Key Principles
- Conscious access concerns availability, not mere processing.
- Information can influence behavior without broad conscious access.
- The mechanisms of access are theory-dependent.

## 1.5.7 Conscious processing

## 1. Purpose
Define processing that occurs with conscious availability and distinguish it from unconscious processing.

## 2. Definition
Conscious processing is information processing that is accompanied by conscious awareness or is available to conscious cognition. **What it is not:** it is not simply any complex or effortful processing; unconscious processes can also be complex.

## 3. Core Understanding
Conscious processing can support deliberate reasoning, explicit report, intentional regulation, and flexible integration of information. Its speed and capacity may be limited relative to some automatic processes, although the exact limits depend on task and theory. Conscious processing can interact continuously with nonconscious processing rather than operating as a completely separate system. Whether a process is conscious should therefore be determined by evidence about awareness, not by whether the task appears difficult or sophisticated. Conscious processing is better treated as a family of operations than as one uniform cognitive mode. Some consciously accessible operations can become highly practiced, while difficult tasks can sometimes recruit processes that are not consciously experienced. Evidence should therefore identify the specific conscious component rather than infer it from subjective effort alone.


Conscious processing is often capacity-limited and flexible, but neither property is absolute. A person can consciously process several related elements as a structured unit, and practiced skills can become less dependent on deliberate monitoring. Conscious processing is therefore better understood as a changing relation between information and the current workspace of cognition than as one uniform mode. It can include explicit comparison, deliberate rule application, metacognitive evaluation, intentional planning, and controlled updating, but these functions are not synonymous. Experimental tasks can separate subjective awareness from behavioral consequences, helping determine whether a result reflects conscious cognition or post-perceptual decision processes.
## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Describes cognition occurring with conscious availability and complements controlled processing without becoming a synonym for it.
## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Owns the consciousness-specific construct of processing accompanied by conscious awareness. Defers general psychological processing to Chapter 1.1 and attentional control to Chapter 1.6.

### Defers
Defers automatic and controlled processing to 1.5.11–1.5.12 and general cognition to relevant chapters.
## 6. Key Principles
- Conscious processing involves conscious availability or awareness.
- Complexity and effort do not by themselves prove consciousness.
- Conscious and unconscious processing can interact.

## 1.5.8 Unconscious processing

## 1. Purpose
Establish that information can be processed and influence behavior without entering conscious awareness.

## 2. Definition
Unconscious processing is processing that occurs without the relevant information or operation becoming consciously experienced or reportable. **What it is not:** it does not mean a process is mysterious, inaccessible to all measurement, or necessarily completely independent of consciousness.

## 3. Core Understanding
Research demonstrates that information can affect perception, memory, action, emotion, or decision-related behavior under conditions in which conscious awareness is limited or absent by the study's operational measure. The magnitude and reliability of such effects vary by task and method. Unconscious does not mean causally unlimited: processing remains constrained by the information available, the person's capacities, and the experimental context. Different paradigms also differ in how convincingly they establish lack of awareness. A central methodological issue is separating absence of measured awareness from proof of complete absence of awareness. Weak or indirect awareness can remain undetected by a coarse measure. Strong claims about unconscious causation therefore require converging awareness measures and controls for alternative explanations such as guessing, response bias, and residual stimulus information.


Evidence for unconscious processing is strongest when awareness is independently assessed and alternative explanations are controlled. A stimulus can influence a response through residual sensory information, motor preparation, learned associations, or response tendencies without establishing rich unconscious understanding. Claims should specify what was processed: a feature, category, affective cue, action tendency, or more complex representation. Unconscious processing can coexist with partial awareness, making binary classifications an approximation. Masking, inattentional conditions, implicit learning, and automatic skills answer different questions and should not be collapsed into one mechanism. The construct demonstrates that psychological processing extends beyond reportable experience, but it does not imply a hidden second mind with unlimited access to goals or meanings.
## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Provides the broad counterpart to conscious processing and the conceptual background for subliminal processing and automaticity.
## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Owns the consciousness-specific distinction between processing with and without conscious awareness. Defers general psychological processing and specific unconscious mechanisms to their canonical Topics.

### Defers
Defers subliminal processing to 1.5.9 and automaticity to 1.5.10; does not imply one unconscious mechanism.
## 6. Key Principles
- Processing can occur without conscious awareness.
- Unconscious does not mean unlimited or unmeasurable.
- Evidence for unconscious processing depends on how awareness is assessed.

## 1.5.9 Subliminal processing

## 1. Purpose
Explain processing of stimuli presented below or outside a person's consciously reportable threshold under defined conditions.

## 2. Definition
Subliminal processing refers to processing of information presented under conditions intended to prevent conscious identification or awareness of that information. **What it is not:** subliminal does not mean that a stimulus has no neural or behavioral effect, nor does it imply powerful hidden control.

## 3. Core Understanding
Subliminal effects are typically studied by presenting stimuli briefly, weakly, or under masking conditions and then measuring behavior or physiological responses. Some studies find effects on subsequent responses, but effects are generally task-, context-, and method-dependent. Demonstrating that participants did not consciously perceive a stimulus is itself a measurement problem, because awareness can be partial and sensitive to the measure used. Claims that subliminal information can reliably bypass conscious control and determine complex behavior are not established by ordinary subliminal priming findings. The strongest interpretation of a subliminal finding is usually local and task-specific: a stimulus may influence a particular response under particular presentation and masking conditions. This does not establish that complex goals, preferences, or behavior can be covertly rewritten. Awareness checks, objective discrimination, and control conditions are essential because “not reported” and “not perceived” are not automatically equivalent.


Subliminality is an experimental property of stimulus presentation and awareness measurement, not a mysterious psychological force. A stimulus may be discriminable under one task yet fail to reach awareness under another, which is why researchers distinguish objective and subjective awareness measures. Masking can alter visibility without eliminating every trace of processing. Effects can be small, short-lived, and dependent on stimulus relevance, baseline activation, and task conditions. Subliminal priming therefore supports constrained processing under limited awareness rather than the popular idea that hidden messages reliably dictate complex choices. Awareness checks, objective discrimination, and control conditions are essential because “not reported” and “not perceived” are not equivalent.
## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Operationalizes one experimentally defined form of limited-awareness processing.
## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Owns the psychological construct of processing under experimentally defined subliminal conditions. Defers general unconscious processing to its Topic and attention to Chapter 1.6.

### Defers

## 6. Key Principles
- Subliminal processing concerns experimentally limited conscious awareness.
- Effects are usually constrained by task and context.
- Subliminal exposure does not establish covert control of complex behavior.

## 1.5.10 Automaticity

## 1. Purpose
Define automaticity as characteristics of processes that operate with reduced conscious control or monitoring, while avoiding an absolute automatic/manual split.

## 2. Definition
Automaticity is the degree to which a process can operate with limited conscious control, monitoring, or effort under appropriate conditions. **What it is not:** automaticity does not necessarily mean completely unconscious, effortless, uncontrollable, or immune to context.

## 3. Core Understanding
Repeated practice can make some operations faster, more efficient, and less dependent on deliberate control. Automaticity is often multidimensional: a process may become fast while remaining interruptible, or require little attention while still reaching awareness. Automatic and controlled processing can coexist and interact. The degree of automaticity depends on task demands, practice, competing goals, and environmental conditions rather than being a permanent label attached to a behavior. Automaticity can be decomposed into features such as speed, efficiency, lack of intention, reduced attentional demand, and resistance to interference. A process may be automatic on one dimension and controlled on another. This multidimensional view explains why familiar skills can remain sensitive to goals and context even after extensive practice.


Automaticity is best treated as a profile rather than a binary category. Researchers can ask whether a process is fast, efficient, unintentional, difficult to suppress, minimally dependent on attention, or acquired through practice. These dimensions can diverge. Reading a familiar word can be rapid and difficult to suppress while still becoming consciously experienced; a practiced motor routine can be efficient while remaining goal-sensitive. Automaticity can weaken when the environment changes or competing responses are introduced. Practice may shift processing demands rather than simply remove consciousness. The construct describes how control is distributed between learned procedures and deliberate regulation and should not be a catch-all explanation for behavior that feels easy.
## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Provides the multidimensional property underlying automatic versus controlled processing.

- Practice can change processing demands without eliminating all control.

## 1.5.11 Automatic processing

## 1. Purpose
Explain processing that can proceed with relatively little deliberate control under appropriate conditions.

## 2. Definition
Automatic processing is processing that, under specified conditions, proceeds with relatively limited conscious control, monitoring, or attentional demand. **What it is not:** it is not necessarily completely unconscious or impossible to modify.

## 3. Core Understanding
Automatic processes can emerge through learning, repeated practice, or well-established system properties. They can be efficient, but their efficiency can depend on familiar conditions and may decrease when circumstances change or competing demands appear. Some automatic processes remain accessible to consciousness even though they do not require continuous deliberate control. The distinction from controlled processing is therefore functional rather than an absolute division between two separate minds. Automatic processing can be triggered by environmental cues or learned associations without requiring a fully articulated intention. However, automatic activation does not guarantee automatic completion: goals, competing inputs, and executive control can alter what happens next. The scientifically useful question is which component became relatively automatic under which conditions.


Automatic processing can operate with reduced deliberate initiation and monitoring, but its content remains constrained by learned associations, stimulus conditions, and current goals. Automatic processes can be triggered by familiar inputs, practiced mappings, or learned routines, and their output can subsequently enter awareness. Automaticity does not imply infallibility: processes efficient in regular environments can produce systematic errors when context changes. Some automatic effects are also sensitive to attention, expectancy, or competing goals. Measurement should identify what is automatic—speed, initiation, resource demand, or resistance to interruption—because these dimensions can yield different conclusions.

Expresses automaticity at the process level when a task proceeds with reduced deliberate control.

- Automatic processing can require little deliberate control.
- It can remain conscious in some circumstances.
- Automaticity depends on conditions and task demands.

## 1.5.12 Controlled processing

## 1. Purpose
Define processing that depends more strongly on deliberate, conscious regulation and distinguish it from effort alone.

## 2. Definition
Controlled processing is processing that requires relatively deliberate regulation, monitoring, or goal-directed control. **What it is not:** controlled processing is not simply any difficult task, and conscious involvement does not guarantee perfect control.

## 3. Core Understanding
Controlled processing is commonly recruited when tasks are novel, conflicting, weakly learned, or require flexible adjustment. It can involve maintaining goals, selecting among responses, monitoring performance, and overriding competing tendencies. Control is limited by available attention, working memory, fatigue, motivation, and task complexity. Repeated practice can reduce the amount of control required for some operations, although context can restore control demands. Controlled processing generally becomes more important when tasks are novel, conflicting, weakly practiced, or require active goal maintenance. It can still be constrained by working-memory capacity, fatigue, distraction, and competing habits. The distinction is therefore functional and context-sensitive rather than a claim that one mental system always operates consciously.


Controlled processing becomes especially important when rules are novel, conflicting, weakly learned, or require deliberate updating. It often involves maintaining a goal, selecting among alternatives, suppressing a dominant response, monitoring outcomes, or adapting when a familiar routine fails. Controlled processing is not necessarily slow in every circumstance; practice can compress previously deliberate operations into faster routines. Conversely, a familiar action can require renewed control when circumstances change. Control is dynamic and context-sensitive. Measurement can use interference, switching, inhibition, error monitoring, and strategy reports, but no single task isolates control perfectly.

Expresses deliberate regulation and complements automatic processing while depending on attention and other resources.

## 6. Key Principles

- Control is limited and context-sensitive.
- Practice can reduce control demands for some operations.

## 1.5.13 Consciousness measurement

## 1. Purpose
Explain how consciousness is scientifically measured and why no single measure captures the whole construct.

## 2. Definition
Consciousness measurement is the assessment of conscious awareness, experience, responsiveness, or related indicators using behavioral, subjective, physiological, or neural measures. **What it is not:** no single measure should be treated as a direct, complete readout of consciousness itself.

## 3. Core Understanding
Researchers use verbal reports, forced-choice discrimination, confidence judgments, behavioral responsiveness, physiological responses, and neural measures, often in combination. Each measure captures a different aspect and has limitations. A person may have experience without being able to report it, or may show behavior that is difficult to interpret as evidence of conscious experience. Experimental designs therefore need to separate performance, awareness, and response ability where possible. Measurement choices can affect conclusions about whether processing was conscious. No single measurement method provides a complete readout of consciousness. Self-report has privileged access to reported experience but can be limited by language and response requirements; behavioral measures can reveal discriminability but can also be affected by strategy; physiological and neural measures provide correlates without automatically establishing subjective experience. Converging methods strengthen inference.


Consciousness measurement is an inference problem because the target is partly subjective and cannot be directly observed from another person. Measures include report, forced-choice discrimination, confidence, metacognitive sensitivity, behavioral responsiveness, physiological signals, neural measures, and experimental state manipulations. Each has distinct failure modes. Report can be limited by language, memory, and response criteria; behavior can occur without awareness; physiological or neural correlates can indicate state changes without uniquely identifying experience. Converging measures are therefore valuable. Timing matters because a post-event report can reflect memory rather than the exact moment of experience. A good measurement claim specifies the operational definition, task, population, contrast, and inferential limit.
## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Operationalizes the preceding consciousness constructs and establishes the inferential limits of measurement.
## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Owns the general measurement problem for consciousness. Defers specific research methods and measurement validity to Chapter 1.2 and biological measurement techniques to Chapter 1.3.

### Defers
Defers biological measurement mechanisms to Chapter 1.3 and specific instruments to methodological contexts.
## 6. Key Principles
- Consciousness cannot be reduced to one measurement method.

## 1.5.29 Lucid dreaming

## 1. Purpose
Define lucid dreaming as dreaming accompanied by awareness that one is dreaming.

## 2. Definition
Lucid dreaming is a dream state in which the dreamer has some awareness that the current experience is a dream, sometimes accompanied by deliberate influence over dream content or actions. **What it is not:** lucidity does not guarantee complete control over the dream.

## 3. Core Understanding
Lucid dreams can contain ordinary dream imagery alongside reflective awareness. Reports and laboratory studies suggest that lucid dreaming is most often associated with REM sleep, although the exact neural and cognitive conditions supporting lucidity remain under investigation. Techniques intended to increase lucid-dream frequency vary in effectiveness, and individual differences are substantial. Awareness and control should be treated as separate dimensions. Lucid dreaming involves awareness that one is dreaming while the dream is ongoing, sometimes accompanied by deliberate influence over dream content. Lucidity and dream control are related but distinct dimensions. Evidence is constrained by the difficulty of verifying subjective dream states, although some laboratory paradigms use prearranged eye-movement signals during REM sleep as behavioral markers.


Lucid dreaming is dreaming in which the dreamer recognizes during the dream that the current experience is a dream. Lucidity and control are distinct: a person may know they are dreaming without being able to deliberately alter the dream. Lucidity can vary in clarity, stability, metacognitive awareness, and voluntary influence. Experimental verification is difficult because the defining event is subjective, but trained lucid dreamers can sometimes signal during sleep with prearranged eye movements while physiological criteria identify the sleep state. Lucid dreams often occur during REM. Claims about universal benefits or therapeutic effects require separate evidence; the core phenomenon is dreaming combined with awareness of the dream state.

## 1.5.30 Sleep paralysis

## 1. Purpose
Define sleep paralysis as temporary inability to move during transitions into or out of sleep, often with preserved awareness.

## 2. Definition
Sleep paralysis is a temporary state in which voluntary movement is inhibited during sleep–wake transitions while awareness may be partly or fully preserved. **What it is not:** it is not evidence by itself of a supernatural event or a permanent loss of motor function.

## 3. Core Understanding
Sleep paralysis is associated with the muscle atonia characteristic of REM sleep persisting into a state of waking awareness or occurring during sleep onset. Episodes can include vivid perceptual experiences, pressure sensations, or a sense of presence. Episodes are usually brief and self-limited, although they can be frightening. Irregular sleep schedules, sleep deprivation, and other factors are associated with increased occurrence in some people. Sleep paralysis involves temporary inability to move or speak around sleep onset or awakening while aspects of awareness are preserved. It can include vivid perceptual or sensed-presence experiences that are often interpreted through cultural and personal frameworks. Episodes can be frightening without implying psychosis, and frequency can be influenced by sleep disruption, stress, and other factors.


Sleep paralysis is a temporary state in which voluntary movement is unavailable during transitions into or out of sleep, often with preserved or unusual awareness. It is commonly associated with REM-related muscle atonia persisting into a wake-like state, although phenomenology varies. Episodes may include vivid perceptual experiences, chest pressure, sensed presence, or fear, but these are not necessary for the core phenomenon. Sleep deprivation, irregular schedules, stress, and other factors can increase susceptibility in some people. Cultural interpretation can shape meaning because unusual bodily and perceptual experiences invite different explanations. Sleep paralysis should be distinguished from persistent neurological motor impairment and from hallucination as a general perceptual phenomenon.

## 1.5.31 Hypnosis

## 1. Purpose
Define hypnosis as a social and cognitive procedure involving focused attention and heightened responsiveness to suggestions, without assuming a single mechanism.

## 2. Definition
Hypnosis is a procedure or state involving focused attention, reduced peripheral attention, and increased responsiveness to suggestions within a social and cognitive context. **What it is not:** hypnosis is not simply unconsciousness, sleep, or proof that a person has lost all control.

## 3. Core Understanding
People vary in hypnotic responsiveness, and suggestions can affect experience, attention, memory, perception, and behavior under appropriate conditions. Theories differ about whether hypnosis is best explained through altered states, social-cognitive processes, executive control, expectancy, or combinations of these. Hypnotic effects are therefore real phenomena to study without requiring one settled account of their mechanism. People generally retain meaningful agency, although suggestions can influence experience and behavior. Hypnosis is commonly studied as a context involving focused attention, suggestion, altered expectations, and changes in subjective experience or behavior. Responsiveness to suggestions varies substantially among people and across suggestions. Findings do not support treating hypnosis as a universal loss of control; interpretation depends on the specific induction, suggestion, task, and outcome measure.


Hypnosis is a social and psychological procedure involving focused attention, reduced peripheral awareness, and increased responsiveness to suggestions under particular conditions. Responses vary with expectations, motivation, context, hypnotic suggestibility, and the suggestion itself. Hypnosis does not require loss of consciousness, and people can remain aware while experiencing altered perception or voluntary control. Research on analgesia, memory, attention, and behavior shows that suggestions can change experience and performance, but mechanisms remain debated. Effects should not be interpreted as proof of a wholly separate state or removal of ordinary agency. Measurement should distinguish subjective reports, behavioral response, physiological correlates, and post-hypnotic effects.

Shows how suggestion, attention, expectation, and context can modify experience without requiring unconsciousness.

## 1.5.32 Meditation

Define meditation as a family of practices that train or cultivate attention, awareness, or other mental processes.

Effects reported in research vary by practice, population, duration, comparison condition, and outcome measure. Practices differ in attentional target, monitoring style, effort, instruction, duration, and intended outcome. Meditation is a heterogeneous family of practices that deliberately train attention, monitoring, emotion regulation, bodily awareness, or other aspects of experience. There is no single meditation state or mechanism: practices differ in attentional target, instruction, posture, duration, cultural tradition, and intended outcome. Research findings therefore depend heavily on the specific practice and comparison condition. Acute state changes should be separated from longer-term changes associated with repeated practice. Claims of universal benefits are not justified by the existence of a broad practice category.

## 1.5.33 Mindfulness

## 1. Purpose
Define mindfulness as a mode of attending to present experience with awareness and reduced automatic entanglement with evaluative reactions.

## 2. Definition
Mindfulness is a mode of attending to present-moment experience with awareness, often emphasizing nonjudgmental observation and reduced automatic reactivity. **What it is not:** mindfulness is not simply relaxation, positive thinking, or the absence of thoughts.

## 3. Core Understanding
Mindfulness can refer to a trait-like tendency, a state, or practices designed to cultivate it. Attention may be directed toward sensations, thoughts, emotions, or other present experiences while noticing them without immediately reacting to them. Research on mindfulness-based practices reports effects across several psychological and health outcomes, but findings vary by intervention, comparator, measurement quality, and population. Mindfulness should therefore not be treated as a single mechanism or universal treatment effect. Mindfulness commonly involves intentional attention to present-moment experience with an attitude of observation or reduced reactivity, but definitions and operationalizations vary. It can be studied as a transient state, repeated practice, or broader dispositional characteristic. Effects on cognition or emotion depend on training, context, measurement, and comparison condition rather than following automatically from the label.


Mindfulness is commonly described as intentional present-oriented awareness characterized by sustained attention to experience with reduced automatic judgment or elaboration. It can be practiced formally or in ordinary activities. Mindfulness is related to meditation but is not identical to every meditation practice, and trait self-reports are not equivalent to demonstrated skill. Proposed components include attentional monitoring, decentering, reduced habitual elaboration, and emotion-regulation changes, but evidence varies by measure and intervention. Mindfulness can involve observing thoughts without treating them as commands or facts. It should not be represented as guaranteed relaxation or emotional suppression. Measurement should distinguish instructed state, self-reported tendency, and trained behavioral capacity.

## 1.5.34 Altered states of consciousness

Define altered states broadly as systematic changes in conscious experience or organization relative to ordinary waking consciousness.

## 3. Core Understanding
Altered states can arise during sleep, meditation, hypnosis, intense absorption, psychoactive substance use, sensory changes, and other conditions. Dimensions can include attention, perception, self-experience, time perception, emotional intensity, memory, and responsiveness. Different altered states may share some features while differing in causes and mechanisms. Classification therefore depends on the dimensions being measured rather than on a single universal definition. Altered states can differ in content, intensity, arousal, self-experience, perception of time, and degree of control. They can arise through sleep, meditation, hypnosis, psychoactive substances, sensory conditions, or other circumstances. “Altered” is descriptive rather than inherently pathological, and the construct is most informative when the specific dimensions of change are identified.


Altered states of consciousness are departures from an individual's ordinary pattern of conscious experience, awareness, arousal, self-representation, perception, cognition, or temporal organization. They include naturally occurring states such as dreaming and states associated with hypnosis, meditation, sleep transitions, substances, sensory conditions, and other contexts. “Altered” is descriptive rather than inherently pathological or beneficial. A state can change one dimension while preserving others, so the category should not be treated as a single mechanism. Classification requires comparison with an appropriate baseline and specification of which features changed. Duration, reversibility, voluntary control, external responsiveness, and phenomenology are relevant dimensions. Different altered states can arise from different mechanisms.

## 1.5.35 Dissociation

Define dissociation as a disruption or separation in normally integrated aspects of psychological functioning or experience.

## 2. Definition
Dissociation is a disruption, separation, or altered integration among normally connected aspects of consciousness, memory, identity, perception, or experience. **What it is not:** dissociation is not synonymous with every unusual mental state and does not automatically indicate a disorder.

## 3. Core Understanding
Dissociative experiences vary in intensity and form, including detachment, altered sense of self or surroundings, gaps in access to information, or compartmentalization of experience. They can occur in ordinary and stressful contexts as well as in clinical conditions. The causes and mechanisms are heterogeneous, and different theories emphasize attention, memory, defensive processes, trauma-related mechanisms, or other factors. Clinical significance depends on severity, distress, impairment, and context rather than on the mere presence of a dissociative experience. Dissociation can refer to altered relationships among normally integrated aspects of experience, memory, identity, perception, or awareness. It is therefore broader than any one symptom and does not by itself identify a disorder. Measurement varies across self-report, experimental tasks, and clinical assessment, so apparent differences between studies can reflect both phenomenology and operational definition.


Dissociation refers to altered relationships among normally integrated aspects of experience, cognition, memory, identity, perception, or agency. The construct is heterogeneous: detachment or depersonalization differs from gaps in autobiographical memory or discontinuity in identity-related experience. Dissociation can occur transiently in ordinary or stressful contexts and can also be prominent in clinical conditions. Measurement ranges from brief state reports to trait questionnaires and clinical interviews, and instruments capture different components. The term should not be a synonym for distraction, absorption, or ordinary forgetfulness. Mechanistic accounts include altered attention, predictive processing, stress-related changes, memory processes, and other models; evidence does not justify reducing all dissociation to one mechanism.

## 1.5.36 Dissociative states

## 1. Purpose
Describe temporary configurations of experience characterized by dissociative features without equating them with dissociative disorders.

## 2. Definition
Dissociative states are episodes or conditions in which dissociative features become prominent, such as altered self-experience, detachment, unusual access to memory, or changes in perceived reality. **What it is not:** a dissociative state is not automatically a psychiatric disorder.

## 3. Core Understanding
States can vary in duration, intensity, triggers, and functional consequences. They may occur in contexts such as acute stress, trauma-related reactions, absorption, or other altered conditions, but no single cause applies to all episodes. The boundary between ordinary, transient, and clinically significant dissociation depends on context, distress, impairment, persistence, and associated symptoms. A state-level analysis asks what changed during a particular episode rather than assuming a stable trait or diagnosis. Features can include detachment, altered ownership of experience, narrowed access to memory, or discontinuity in awareness, but these features do not always co-occur. Context and temporal course are therefore essential for distinguishing transient dissociative experiences from persistent clinical syndromes.


Dissociative states are best analyzed temporally. An episode may involve altered bodily ownership, detachment from self or surroundings, narrowed or discontinuous access to information, or changes in agency. Different features can appear separately, and the same person can experience different forms under different circumstances. Triggers and correlates are heterogeneous, including acute stress, trauma-related contexts, absorption, fatigue, and other altered conditions. A transient state does not establish a persistent disorder. State measures are particularly important because retrospective trait questionnaires can blur frequency, intensity, and context. The scientific task is to describe what changed, when it changed, and what evidence supports the interpretation rather than infer a diagnosis from an isolated unusual experience.

## 1.5.37 Psychoactive substances

## 1. Purpose
Define psychoactive substances as substances that alter psychological or nervous-system functioning and distinguish this broad category from harmful use.

## 2. Definition
Psychoactive substances are chemical substances that alter brain function and thereby can change perception, mood, cognition, arousal, behavior, or consciousness. **What it is not:** psychoactive does not mean inherently harmful, addictive, or illegal.

## 3. Core Understanding
Substances differ in their primary effects, dose-response relationships, routes of administration, duration, interactions, and risks. The same substance can produce different effects depending on dose, person, tolerance, context, and co-use with other substances. Pharmacological effects can include changes in arousal, perception, pain, mood, memory, and motor function. Classification by broad effect is useful but cannot capture every mechanism or individual response. Psychological effects are produced through interactions among pharmacology, dose, route, timing, tolerance, expectations, setting, and individual differences. The same substance can therefore have different subjective and behavioral effects across circumstances. This variability is central to interpretation and prevents broad substance categories from being treated as deterministic predictors of experience.


Psychoactive substances can alter consciousness through interacting routes: pharmacological action, downstream physiology, learned expectations, context, and interactions with other substances. Dose-response relationships can be nonlinear, and route changes onset, peak effects, duration, and risk. Acute subjective effects should be separated from repeated-exposure adaptations such as tolerance and dependence, and from clinical substance-use disorders. Substance categories overlap pharmacologically; one drug can have stimulant-like, sedating, analgesic, perceptual, or other effects depending on dose and systems affected. Individual variation is substantial because metabolism, prior exposure, genetics, health, sleep, and concurrent substances affect response.

## 1.5.38 Stimulants

## 1. Purpose
Define stimulants as psychoactive substances that generally increase central nervous-system activation or alertness.

## 2. Definition
Stimulants are psychoactive substances that generally increase central nervous-system activation, alertness, or arousal. **What it is not:** stimulant does not mean that every substance produces the same subjective or physiological effects.

## 3. Core Understanding
Stimulant effects can include increased alertness, energy, heart rate, and reduced subjective fatigue, but effects vary by substance and dose. Examples include caffeine and several prescription or illicit drugs, which differ substantially in mechanism, potency, duration, and risk. High doses or repeated use can produce adverse effects, and tolerance or dependence can develop with some substances. Classification should therefore remain pharmacological rather than moral or legal. Stimulant effects often depend on the balance between desired increases in alertness and unwanted increases in arousal or physiological activation. Tolerance can change some effects over repeated exposure, while abrupt changes in use can produce other effects depending on the substance. The class therefore describes a pharmacological pattern, not a uniform subjective experience.

Repeated use can produce tolerance to some effects without eliminating all physiological risks. Stimulants can also alter circadian timing by delaying sleep or changing perceived fatigue, creating a cycle in which subjective alertness masks accumulated sleep loss. ## 1.5.42 Drug tolerance

## 1. Purpose
Define tolerance as reduced response to a drug after repeated exposure, requiring careful distinction from dependence.

## 2. Definition
Drug tolerance is a reduction in response to a drug after repeated exposure such that a greater amount may be required to produce a similar effect. **What it is not:** tolerance is not the same as dependence or addiction.

## 3. Core Understanding
Tolerance can develop through pharmacological adaptations and, for some effects, through learned or context-dependent processes. It is not uniform across all effects of a substance: tolerance to one effect may develop more strongly than to another. Tolerance can also change after periods without exposure, which can increase overdose risk if a previously tolerated dose is resumed. The mechanisms differ across substances and patterns of use. Tolerance can be pharmacodynamic, pharmacokinetic, learned, or context-dependent, and these forms need not develop at the same rate. Cross-tolerance can also occur among substances with overlapping mechanisms. A person's apparent response therefore reflects both the drug and the history and context of exposure.


Tolerance can be pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, behavioral, or context-dependent. Pharmacokinetic tolerance changes exposure at relevant sites; pharmacodynamic tolerance reflects adaptation at target systems; learned tolerance can arise when environmental cues predict exposure and prepare compensatory responses. Different effects of the same substance can develop tolerance at different rates. Cross-tolerance can occur among substances with overlapping mechanisms. Tolerance can diminish after abstinence, making a previously tolerated dose dangerous after a period without use. Measuring tolerance requires specifying the effect tested, baseline response, repeated exposure pattern, and interval since last exposure.

## 1.5.43 Dependence

## 1. Purpose
Define dependence as adaptation in which stopping or substantially reducing a substance can produce withdrawal, and distinguish it from addiction.

## 2. Definition
Dependence is a physiological or psychological adaptation to repeated substance exposure in which reducing or stopping the substance can produce withdrawal symptoms or other rebound effects. **What it is not:** dependence is not synonymous with addiction or substance-use disorder.

## 3. Core Understanding
Dependence can develop with some prescribed medications as well as substances used nonmedically. Physical dependence concerns bodily adaptation and withdrawal; psychological dependence can involve perceived need or difficulty functioning without a substance, though terminology varies by context. Dependence can occur without compulsive harmful use, so it should not be used as a moral label or automatic diagnosis. Dependence describes adaptation, whereas addiction or substance-use disorder concerns patterns of use and clinically significant impairment or loss of control. Physical dependence can arise during appropriate medical treatment, which is why withdrawal after dose reduction cannot by itself establish a disorder. Psychological and physiological adaptations can also differ in timing and expression.


Dependence is an adaptation state and can be physiological, psychological, or both. Physical dependence is inferred from characteristic withdrawal after reduction or cessation; psychological dependence can refer to perceived need or difficulty functioning without a substance, but terminology varies. Dependence can arise during medically supervised treatment, so it should not be equated with addiction. Dependence can interact with tolerance but neither requires the other in identical form. The key temporal structure is repeated exposure followed by adaptation, with withdrawal revealing the altered regulatory state. Interpretation depends on substance, dose, duration, route, tapering pattern, and individual physiology.

## 1.5.44 Withdrawal

## 1. Purpose
Define withdrawal as a characteristic set of symptoms or effects following reduction or cessation of a substance after adaptation.

## 2. Definition
Withdrawal is a set of physical, psychological, or behavioral symptoms that can occur when a substance is reduced or stopped after physiological adaptation has developed. **What it is not:** withdrawal is not evidence by itself of addiction or a substance-use disorder.

## 3. Core Understanding
Symptoms vary by substance, duration and intensity of exposure, individual factors, and rate of reduction. They can include changes in mood, sleep, autonomic function, pain, gastrointestinal function, arousal, or other systems. Some forms of withdrawal can be medically dangerous, making abrupt cessation inappropriate for certain substances. Withdrawal is best understood as a pharmacological and psychological adaptation phenomenon rather than a moral consequence of drug use. Withdrawal is often temporally patterned and substance-specific, with some syndromes involving substantial medical risk. Symptoms can reflect rebound of systems that were chronically altered during exposure, but individual experiences vary widely. Clinical interpretation therefore considers substance, dose, duration, co-use, medical context, and timing rather than treating every post-cessation symptom as equivalent.


Withdrawal is temporally organized and substance-specific. Symptoms can begin after reduction or cessation, change in intensity over time, and resolve at different rates depending on substance and exposure history. Some syndromes are primarily uncomfortable, while others can involve seizures, delirium, severe autonomic instability, or other medical risks. Withdrawal can include physiological rebound and psychological symptoms, and expectations can influence subjective distress without explaining away physiological adaptation. Withdrawal demonstrates dependence but does not by itself establish compulsive use or a substance-use disorder. Clinical interpretation requires substance, dose, duration, co-use, medical context, and timing.
