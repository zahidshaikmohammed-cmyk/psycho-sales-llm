## 1.9.1 Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Thinking** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Thinking is the coordinated psychological activity of manipulating, evaluating, transforming, and using information in ways that support representation, reasoning, problem solving, planning, and goal-directed action.

## 3. Core Understanding

Thinking should be analyzed at the level of operations rather than treated as a vague synonym for mental activity. A person can retrieve a fact, transform it, compare it with another representation, simulate an outcome, infer an unstated relation, or plan an action; these operations can occur within the same episode but have different mechanisms. Thinking is also constrained by representation: if relevant relations are absent from the active representation, additional effort may not solve the problem. Conversely, re-representation can make a previously difficult task easy without adding new factual information. Thinking can be deliberate, automatic, verbal, spatial, numerical, embodied, or distributed across external artifacts. Its products are therefore insufficient to identify its process. Measurement should examine intermediate steps, error patterns, transfer, strategy choice, and sensitivity to task changes. Individual differences may arise from knowledge, working memory, attention, control, motivation, or strategy rather than a single thinking capacity. Thinking also changes developmentally as representations and control become more sophisticated. It is consequently a process family that coordinates other cognitive resources while remaining distinct from the specialized systems that provide its content.

A useful process decomposition separates representation maintenance, transformation, inference, evaluation, and response selection. Different tasks emphasize different combinations, so a single performance score cannot identify a unitary thinking mechanism. Thinking can also operate through external representations such as notes, diagrams, formulas, or tools; these supports alter the computational burden without necessarily changing the person's underlying knowledge. Development and expertise change which operations are automatic and which require deliberate control. Individual differences can therefore reflect accumulated knowledge, strategy, working memory, attentional control, and metacognitive regulation. Errors should be classified by stage: an incorrect representation can generate a wrong answer even when the later inference is valid, while a correct representation can still fail because the wrong operation was selected. This stage-sensitive approach is important because thinking is often inferred from final products. Strong evidence instead combines process measures, transfer, strategy reports, response patterns, and experimental manipulations.

## 1.9.2 Cognition

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Cognition** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Cognition is the broad family of mental processes involved in acquiring, representing, transforming, storing, retrieving, and using information.

## 3. Core Understanding

Cognition is best treated as an organizational level rather than a single mechanism. Perception supplies processed information, attention selects it, memory retains and retrieves it, language can structure it, and reasoning or problem solving transforms it. The fact that all are cognitive does not make them interchangeable. Cognition can also be externally scaffolded: writing, diagrams, calculators, tools, and social interaction can change what an individual must maintain internally. This means cognitive performance depends partly on the coupling between person and environment. Cognition should therefore be studied with careful specification of task demands, representation, time scale, and output. Claims about a general cognitive ability require converging evidence across tasks because any one task contains multiple component demands. Cognition also extends beyond conscious thought; many cognitive operations occur without deliberate report. Conversely, conscious awareness does not guarantee accurate cognition. The construct is thus broad enough to organize information-processing science but too heterogeneous to serve as a standalone causal explanation. A strong account always resolves the umbrella term into more specific operations and levels of explanation.

The breadth of cognition creates a measurement problem. A task labeled “cognitive” can simultaneously require perception, attention, memory, language, executive control, and motivation. Researchers therefore need converging tasks and manipulations if they want to attribute an effect to a specific process. Cognition also occurs in interaction with the environment. External memory aids can reduce internal maintenance, diagrams can expose relations that are difficult to hold mentally, and tools can perform computations that would otherwise consume working memory. This does not mean the cognitive process disappears; it changes its allocation across person and environment. Cognition is also temporally layered: some operations occur in milliseconds, while knowledge acquisition and belief revision unfold over days or years. The umbrella term is therefore useful for organizing a scientific domain but weak as a standalone explanation. Its explanatory value increases as the analyst specifies the information being processed, the operation performed, the constraints imposed, and the evidence distinguishing competing mechanisms.

## 1.9.3 Mental representation

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Mental representation** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
A mental representation is an internal state or structure that stands for, models, or carries information about an object, event, relation, rule, or possibility.

## 3. Core Understanding

Mental representations can differ in format, content, stability, and accessibility. A representation may preserve perceptual similarity, encode symbolic relations, summarize category regularities, model causal structure, or represent a possible future state. It can be transiently active or embedded in long-term knowledge, and it can be altered when new information is integrated. Representations are inferred rather than directly observed, so claims about their format require converging evidence from behavior, transfer, errors, neuropsychology, and sometimes neural measures. A successful task does not establish that the hypothesized representation literally exists in the proposed form; it shows that the model can explain the observed pattern. Competing representational theories can therefore produce similar behavioral predictions. Representations are also task-relative: a useful representation preserves the relations required for the operation being performed. Compression can help generalization while damaging exact recall; detail can support discrimination while increasing processing demands. This makes representational adequacy a central concept for reasoning and problem solving. It also explains why changing notation, perspective, or organization can change performance without changing the external information.

Representations should be distinguished from the things they represent and from the processes that operate on them. A memory of an event is not the event itself; a mental model of a machine is not the machine; a concept of an animal is not an animal. Representations can also be transformed. The same underlying information can be encoded as words, a diagram, a spatial arrangement, a numerical expression, or a causal model, and each format can support some operations more effectively than others. Representational format is therefore partly functional. Evidence for a representation comes from systematic transfer and error patterns: if performance remains stable when surface features change but relations remain constant, a relational representation is plausible. If performance changes when the same relations are reformatted, representational accessibility may be involved. Competing theories should be evaluated by prediction rather than by assuming that introspective experience reveals the format directly. This distinction protects the encyclopedia from treating metaphors such as “mental picture” or “mental file” as literal mechanisms.

## 1.9.4 Concepts

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Concepts** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Concepts are mental structures that organize knowledge about entities, properties, events, or relations and support classification, inference, prediction, and communication.

## 3. Core Understanding

Concepts support generalization beyond particular experiences by organizing information around meaning, properties, relations, or functions. They can operate at different abstraction levels and can contain graded membership, exceptions, and context-sensitive boundaries. Concept learning is therefore not equivalent to acquiring a dictionary definition. A person may know that an instance belongs to a concept while remaining uncertain about its less typical properties, or may possess conceptual knowledge without a precise verbal label. Concepts can be learned through experience, instruction, language, analogy, causal explanation, and social transmission. Existing concepts influence new learning by directing attention and interpretation, creating both efficiency and potential bias. Theories differ over whether concepts are primarily prototypes, exemplars, theory-like structures, or hybrid combinations. Evidence must therefore include typicality, exception handling, category learning, and transfer to novel instances. A concept is especially informative when it supports predictions or inferences about cases that were not directly encountered. Conceptual knowledge also changes as exceptions accumulate or causal understanding improves. Thus concepts are dynamic representational structures rather than static lists of defining features.

Concepts also support compositional thought. A concept can participate in relations with other concepts, allowing a person to infer properties, form categories, generate explanations, and construct novel combinations. Conceptual knowledge therefore has both content and relational organization. A concept can be broad while containing specialized substructures, and different concepts can overlap without having identical boundaries. Learning can change conceptual structure through differentiation, integration, and restructuring. Differentiation occurs when a previously broad concept is divided into more precise distinctions; integration occurs when separate pieces of knowledge become connected under a common structure. These changes can improve reasoning while also creating new expectations that later influence perception and memory. Measurement should therefore examine not only whether a person can define a concept but whether they can apply it to unfamiliar cases, explain its relations, identify exceptions, and use it to make predictions. Concept knowledge is strongest when it supports flexible inference rather than rote verbal description.

## 1.9.5 Categories

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Categories** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Categories are groupings of entities or events treated as equivalent in some respect for purposes such as prediction, memory, reasoning, or action.

## 3. Core Understanding

Categories impose functional equivalence on otherwise diverse instances. The relevant equivalence depends on purpose: objects can be grouped by appearance, biological kind, function, ownership, causal role, or social convention. Category membership is therefore not always an intrinsic property of the stimulus. Boundaries can be sharp, graded, overlapping, hierarchical, or context-dependent. Categories support efficient prediction by allowing knowledge associated with a group to generalize to new cases, but generalization can fail when members are heterogeneous or the environment changes. Category knowledge also interacts with decision criteria. A person can possess the same category representation while changing the threshold for assigning an ambiguous item because the consequences of errors changed. Research should therefore distinguish category structure from categorization policy. Development and culture can alter which dimensions become diagnostic, while expertise can make causal or functional features more salient than surface appearance. Measurement through classification, generalization, typicality, and transfer can reveal different aspects of category organization. A category is consequently a structured way of organizing information, not merely a label attached to a collection of objects.

Category structure is partly induced by the environment and partly shaped by the purposes of the learner. Natural categories can have uncertain boundaries because members vary continuously, whereas artificial categories may be defined by explicit rules. Categories can also be hierarchical, allowing a single instance to belong simultaneously to a broad class and several subordinate classes. This hierarchy supports different levels of prediction and explanation. Context can change which level is most useful: a person may identify an object as a tool in one situation and as a specific instrument in another. Category errors can therefore reveal not only what information was represented but which level and criterion were selected. Culture and learning history also influence category structure, especially for socially meaningful categories. Scientific analysis should distinguish descriptive category membership from normative or socially imposed classifications. The psychological category is the organization used by the person, which may or may not correspond to an external taxonomy.

## 1.9.6 Prototypes

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Prototypes** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
A prototype is a graded or summary representation of characteristic features associated with members of a category.

## 3. Core Understanding

Prototype representations summarize central or characteristic properties of category members. They are useful when a category has a meaningful central tendency because similarity to the prototype can support rapid generalization and graded typicality. A prototype need not correspond to any actual member and may instead reflect an abstraction across experiences. The approach becomes less sufficient when atypical members contain diagnostic information or when category membership depends on relational or causal structure that averaging obscures. Prototype representations can also change with the distribution of experience: repeated exposure to unusual members can shift what appears typical. This means prototype effects depend on learning history and attention, not just on objective stimulus structure. Evidence should compare prototype-based predictions with exemplar-based and theory-based alternatives. Typicality ratings alone cannot establish a prototype mechanism because many representational models predict graded typicality. Stronger evidence comes from transfer to novel items, category learning, exception behavior, and sensitivity to distributional changes. Prototype knowledge can coexist with specific exemplar memories, and the relative contribution may depend on task demands and expertise.

Prototype representations can be multidimensional rather than a single average point. Different features may contribute unequally, and the weighting can change with goals and expertise. A prototype may therefore be better understood as a structured summary than as a literal mean of all encountered examples. Prototype effects can also arise from repeated exposure and frequency distributions without requiring an explicit prototype computation. This is why typicality alone cannot establish the mechanism. Stronger tests manipulate the distribution of category members and examine whether classification shifts toward the new central tendency. Prototype-based knowledge can coexist with exceptions represented separately, allowing people to recognize an atypical member despite low prototype similarity. This hybrid organization helps explain why categories can show both graded typicality and sharp responses to diagnostic cases. Prototype theory is therefore a model of how category regularities might be represented, not a claim that all category decisions are calculated from a single central image.

## 1.9.7 Exemplars

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Exemplars** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Exemplars are representations of specific encountered instances that can contribute to category learning and later classification.

## 3. Core Understanding

Exemplar representations preserve information about individual encountered instances and allow new cases to be evaluated through similarity to stored experiences. This can explain sensitivity to unusual examples, recency, frequency, and distinctive category members that a central tendency would obscure. Exemplar approaches connect categorization closely to memory but do not require conscious retrieval of each example. Many stored instances can contribute implicitly to a response through their similarity structure. The main challenge is explaining how large numbers of exemplars can be used efficiently and how abstraction emerges when exact instances vary widely. Hybrid theories therefore allow exemplar information, prototype-like summaries, and causal knowledge to contribute simultaneously. Experimental tests examine whether behavior is better predicted by individual-case similarity, category averages, or structured rules. Expertise may shift the balance because experts possess more diagnostic exemplars and richer abstractions. Exemplar representations are especially valuable for explaining variability within categories, but they should not be treated as the only possible representation. Their scientific status is that of a model whose predictions can be compared against alternatives.

Exemplar representation is especially useful for explaining category variability and sensitivity to unusual cases. A learner can preserve information about individual examples and use the distribution of those memories to determine similarity to a new instance. The psychological system need not consciously retrieve each example; activation can operate over a network of stored instances. Frequency, recency, distinctiveness, and contextual associations can therefore influence classification. The challenge is computational: if every instance were compared equally, categorization could become inefficient. Attention and learned organization can reduce this burden, and hybrid theories allow summary representations to emerge from repeated exemplar experience. Exemplar models are also closely connected to memory because category knowledge can depend on what examples were encountered and retained. Measurement should therefore manipulate exposure distributions, atypical exemplars, and transfer to novel cases. The goal is to determine whether behavior is better predicted by individual-instance similarity, category summaries, explicit rules, or structured causal knowledge.

## 1.9.8 Categorization

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Categorization** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Categorization is the process of assigning or organizing instances into categories using available information, goals, and learned knowledge.

## 3. Core Understanding

Categorization requires selecting relevant information, comparing an instance with category knowledge, resolving ambiguity, and applying a criterion. These operations can be performed rapidly when category evidence is strong or slowly when multiple categories compete. Goals change which features are treated as diagnostic, so the same stimulus can be categorized differently under different tasks without any change in sensory input. Learning can also alter feature weighting, category boundaries, and the speed with which relevant dimensions are accessed. Errors may reflect poor perception, incomplete knowledge, inappropriate generalization, competing categories, or a decision criterion rather than one global categorization deficit. Classification accuracy and reaction time measure different aspects of the process, while transfer to unfamiliar instances tests whether the underlying structure has been learned. Categorization can rely on prototypes, exemplars, rules, causal theories, or combinations. It should therefore be distinguished from category representation: the category is an organized knowledge structure, while categorization is the act of using information to assign or organize a current case.

Categorization can be viewed as evidence accumulation followed by a category decision, but the evidence itself depends on attention and representation. A person may notice color, shape, function, causal properties, or social cues depending on task goals and learned relevance. Ambiguous cases can produce competition among categories, and decision criteria can shift when the consequences of errors change. This means categorization performance contains both representational and decision components. Learning can alter category boundaries, feature weighting, and response speed, while context can activate different category systems. Transfer is crucial because accurate classification of trained examples can be achieved through memorization without a general category representation. Good measurement therefore includes novel cases, exceptions, ambiguous cases, and changes in context. Categorization is also temporally dynamic: early learning may depend on explicit rules, while later performance can become more automatic. The process is thus an interaction among category knowledge, current evidence, goals, and control.

## 1.9.9 Concept formation

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Concept formation** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Concept formation is the acquisition or construction of a mental concept through detecting regularities, relations, or defining features across experience.

## 3. Core Understanding

Concept formation involves discovering or constructing abstractions that remain useful beyond the examples that produced them. Learners may detect shared features, relations, causal dependencies, or predictive regularities and use them to organize future cases. Concept formation can be guided by explicit instruction or discovered through feedback and statistical structure. Surface similarity may support early learning but can become a poor guide when deeper relational structure matters. A learner can also memorize examples or labels without forming a transferable concept. The strongest evidence for concept formation is therefore generalization to novel cases, especially cases that differ in surface form while preserving the underlying relation. Exception handling is equally informative because it shows whether the representation can accommodate boundary conditions. Prior knowledge changes concept formation by determining which dimensions are noticed and which interpretations are plausible. Concept formation is consequently an interaction between new evidence and existing knowledge rather than a blank-slate extraction process.

Concept formation can be treated as a search for structure in experience. Learners must determine which dimensions vary systematically, which relations predict outcomes, and which features are incidental. This can involve statistical learning, hypothesis testing, analogical comparison, causal explanation, or feedback. The learner's prior knowledge determines what patterns are noticed, so concept formation is not purely bottom-up. A misleading training distribution can produce a concept that works locally but fails under transfer. Conversely, sparse examples can sometimes produce powerful abstractions when the underlying relation is clear. Measurement should therefore include generalization, exception handling, explanation, and prediction under altered surface conditions. Concept formation also interacts with language because labels can draw attention to distinctions, but labels do not guarantee that the corresponding conceptual structure has been acquired. A robust concept is demonstrated when the learner can use the abstraction flexibly rather than merely reproduce the examples or definition used during training.

## 1.9.10 Schemas

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Schemas** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Schemas are organized knowledge structures that represent expected relations among concepts, events, roles, or situations.

## 3. Core Understanding

Schemas organize expectations about relations among elements of objects, events, roles, or situations. They support efficient comprehension because predictable information can be processed against an existing structure rather than constructed from scratch. Schemas also guide attention and memory, increasing the probability that schema-relevant information is encoded while plausible but absent details may later be supplied during reconstruction. A schema can therefore increase both efficiency and error depending on environmental regularity. Schema change can occur through accumulation of exceptions, contradiction, new evidence, or restructuring of the knowledge system. Not every expectation is a schema, and schemas should not be equated with simple memories of individual episodes. They are more general structures that organize multiple experiences. Measurement can examine prediction, recall intrusions, transfer, and sensitivity to violations. Schemas also vary across development and culture because regularities differ across environments. Their scientific value lies in explaining how prior knowledge shapes processing without requiring that every expectation be consciously represented.

Schemas can be understood as probabilistic expectations about relations rather than rigid templates. They contain likely elements and relationships but allow variation. When information is incomplete, schemas help fill gaps; when information conflicts strongly with the schema, the person may revise expectations or reinterpret the evidence. This creates a tension between efficiency and accuracy. Schema-driven processing can reduce cognitive load in familiar situations while increasing false recognition of expected but absent details. Schemas also guide what receives attention, meaning that they influence encoding before they influence later memory. Development and culture shape schemas because repeated experience teaches which relations are regular. Measurement should therefore examine both facilitation and intrusion, and should compare familiar and schema-inconsistent situations. A schema is not merely a memory structure and should not be treated as a single stored script. It is an organized expectation system that influences interpretation, prediction, memory, and action.

## 1.9.11 Scripts

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Scripts** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Scripts are event-knowledge structures representing typical sequences, roles, actions, and expectations within recurring situations.

## 3. Core Understanding

Scripts are schemas organized around familiar event sequences, roles, and expected actions. They help people understand incomplete descriptions and predict what is likely to happen next because the sequence provides a structured prior. Scripts can include obligatory, typical, and optional elements rather than a single rigid order. Their usefulness depends on contextual fit: a restaurant script can guide expectations in an ordinary setting but mislead when the event is unusual or intentionally structured differently. Script knowledge is culturally learned, so different communities can develop different expectations for the same nominal event. Scripts can also produce memory intrusions when people later remember expected actions that were not actually observed. They therefore connect event comprehension with reconstructive memory. Measurement should distinguish knowledge of the sequence from memory for a particular episode. Script flexibility is important because real situations often contain branches and interruptions. A script is consequently a structured expectation about event organization, not a literal recording of a repeated routine.

Scripts can contain roles, temporal order, causal dependencies, and optional branches. Their structure is learned through repeated experience and social instruction, so scripts vary across contexts and cultures. A script can support rapid comprehension because the person does not need to infer every expected action independently. It can also generate errors when an expected step is inserted into memory even though it did not occur. This makes scripts relevant to both comprehension and reconstructive remembering. Script knowledge can become highly automatized but remains revisable when unusual events repeatedly violate expectations. Measurement should distinguish knowledge of a canonical sequence from memory for an actual episode and should test whether people recognize deviations. Scripts can also support planning because they provide default action sequences, but a script is not a plan: a script describes what usually happens, whereas a plan represents what the person intends to make happen. This distinction is important for keeping event knowledge separate from goal-directed control.

## 3. Core Understanding

Mental models represent systems or situations in terms of relations that can support prediction, explanation, and simulation. A model can include components, causal links, constraints, and possible states. People often use simplified models because representing every variable is computationally expensive. Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Symbolic thought** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition

Symbolic thought uses signs whose meaning depends on learned relations or conventions. Symbolic processing can also interact with imagery and concrete representations; diagrams can combine symbolic and spatial structure, while numerical notation can compress quantities into manipulable forms. ## 2. Definition

## 3. Core Understanding

Abstract thinking operates on relations, properties, classes, rules, or possibilities that extend beyond the immediate concrete instance. Abstraction reduces complexity by preserving what is invariant across cases while discarding irrelevant detail. The challenge is selecting the correct invariant: excessive abstraction can remove information that is causally or diagnostically important. Abstract thinking therefore requires both generalization and constraint. Abstract thinking can be tested by requiring a relation to survive changes in surface content. If a person recognizes the same causal, logical, or categorical structure across different examples, the evidence supports abstraction. Each step discards some detail while preserving structure. The danger is over-abstraction, in which exceptions or boundary conditions are ignored. Education and expertise often increase access to useful abstractions because they provide organized examples from which invariants can be learned. Abstract thinking can also be strategic: concrete inspection may be reintroduced to test whether an abstract model actually fits a particular case. ## 1.9.22 Inductive reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Inductive reasoning** within the reasoning architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Inductive reasoning generalizes, predicts, or infers broader patterns from particular observations or examples.

## 3. Core Understanding

Inductive reasoning extends observed evidence toward generalizations, predictions, or classifications that are not logically guaranteed. Its quality depends on the representativeness and independence of observations, sample size, measurement reliability, base rates, and the stability of the environment. Domain knowledge also changes induction because people can recognize which features are predictive and which are accidental. A large biased sample can support poor inference, while a smaller but representative sample can support stronger generalization. ## 1.9.25 Counterfactual thinking

## 1.9.67 Expertise and thinking

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Expertise and thinking** within the executive-control architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Expertise and thinking concerns how extensive, organized, domain-specific knowledge and experience alter perception, representation, reasoning, problem solving, and strategic control.

## 3. Core Understanding

Expertise changes the starting conditions for thinking. Experts often recognize meaningful patterns rapidly, organize information into larger units, retrieve domain-specific procedures efficiently, and attend to diagnostic features that novices overlook. These advantages arise from accumulated knowledge and practice rather than from a single general mechanism. Expertise can also produce overgeneralization or fixation when familiar patterns are applied outside their valid conditions. Adaptive expertise therefore includes both routine efficiency and the ability to recognize novelty, revise representations, and transfer principles. Mere years of experience do not guarantee expertise if practice lacks useful feedback or repeatedly reinforces ineffective strategies. Measurement should separate factual knowledge, structural organization, automatic pattern recognition, problem solving, transfer, and adaptability. Expertise is consequently domain-specific and dynamic. Its importance for thinking lies in how richly organized knowledge changes the computational demands of perception, memory, reasoning, and problem solving.
