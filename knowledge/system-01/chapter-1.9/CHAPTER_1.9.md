## 1.9.1 Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Thinking** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Thinking is the coordinated psychological activity of manipulating, evaluating, transforming, and using information in ways that support representation, reasoning, problem solving, planning, and goal-directed action.

## 3. Core Understanding

Thinking should be analyzed at the level of operations rather than treated as a vague synonym for mental activity. A person can retrieve a fact, transform it, compare it with another representation, simulate an outcome, infer an unstated relation, or plan an action; these operations can occur within the same episode but have different mechanisms. Thinking is also constrained by representation: if relevant relations are absent from the active representation, additional effort may not solve the problem. Conversely, re-representation can make a previously difficult task easy without adding new factual information. Thinking can be deliberate, automatic, verbal, spatial, numerical, embodied, or distributed across external artifacts. Its products are therefore insufficient to identify its process. Measurement should examine intermediate steps, error patterns, transfer, strategy choice, and sensitivity to task changes. Individual differences may arise from knowledge, working memory, attention, control, motivation, or strategy rather than a single thinking capacity. Thinking also changes developmentally as representations and control become more sophisticated. It is consequently a process family that coordinates other cognitive resources while remaining distinct from the specialized systems that provide its content.

A useful process decomposition separates representation maintenance, transformation, inference, evaluation, and response selection. Different tasks emphasize different combinations, so a single performance score cannot identify a unitary thinking mechanism. Thinking can also operate through external representations such as notes, diagrams, formulas, or tools; these supports alter the computational burden without necessarily changing the person's underlying knowledge. Development and expertise change which operations are automatic and which require deliberate control. Individual differences can therefore reflect accumulated knowledge, strategy, working memory, attentional control, and metacognitive regulation. Errors should be classified by stage: an incorrect representation can generate a wrong answer even when the later inference is valid, while a correct representation can still fail because the wrong operation was selected. This stage-sensitive approach is important because thinking is often inferred from final products. Strong evidence instead combines process measures, transfer, strategy reports, response patterns, and experimental manipulations.

A further safeguard is to separate thinking from its outputs. Two people can produce the same answer through different representations and strategies, while the same person can produce different answers from the same knowledge when goals or representations change. Process tracing, transfer, and controlled manipulation are therefore more informative than final accuracy alone. This also prevents overgeneralizing from a narrow task to a global cognitive trait.

A final interpretive boundary is that thinking is not necessarily conscious. Deliberate report can reveal some operations, but other transformations may be inferred only from behavior and timing. This makes process-level evidence essential when subjective explanation is incomplete.

Process tracing should therefore accompany outcome scoring where possible. The same answer can result from retrieval, analogy, heuristic search, or explicit derivation, and those routes have different transfer properties. This makes mechanism identification essential.

## 1.9.2 Cognition

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Cognition** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Cognition is the broad family of mental processes involved in acquiring, representing, transforming, storing, retrieving, and using information.

## 3. Core Understanding

Cognition is best treated as an organizational level rather than a single mechanism. Perception supplies processed information, attention selects it, memory retains and retrieves it, language can structure it, and reasoning or problem solving transforms it. The fact that all are cognitive does not make them interchangeable. Cognition can also be externally scaffolded: writing, diagrams, calculators, tools, and social interaction can change what an individual must maintain internally. This means cognitive performance depends partly on the coupling between person and environment. Cognition should therefore be studied with careful specification of task demands, representation, time scale, and output. Claims about a general cognitive ability require converging evidence across tasks because any one task contains multiple component demands. Cognition also extends beyond conscious thought; many cognitive operations occur without deliberate report. Conversely, conscious awareness does not guarantee accurate cognition. The construct is thus broad enough to organize information-processing science but too heterogeneous to serve as a standalone causal explanation. A strong account always resolves the umbrella term into more specific operations and levels of explanation.

The breadth of cognition creates a measurement problem. A task labeled “cognitive” can simultaneously require perception, attention, memory, language, executive control, and motivation. Researchers therefore need converging tasks and manipulations if they want to attribute an effect to a specific process. Cognition also occurs in interaction with the environment. External memory aids can reduce internal maintenance, diagrams can expose relations that are difficult to hold mentally, and tools can perform computations that would otherwise consume working memory. This does not mean the cognitive process disappears; it changes its allocation across person and environment. Cognition is also temporally layered: some operations occur in milliseconds, while knowledge acquisition and belief revision unfold over days or years. The umbrella term is therefore useful for organizing a scientific domain but weak as a standalone explanation. Its explanatory value increases as the analyst specifies the information being processed, the operation performed, the constraints imposed, and the evidence distinguishing competing mechanisms.

The umbrella nature of cognition means explanations should resolve the level at which a claim operates. A neural correlate, a cognitive operation, a behavioral pattern, and a social context can all describe the same event without being interchangeable explanations. Strong cognitive science therefore links levels rather than allowing one to substitute for another.

Because cognition is heterogeneous, broad scores should be treated as summaries rather than mechanisms. Component dissociations and task manipulations are needed to establish which process changed and why.

The broad construct should be used only as an organizing layer. Specific explanations should resolve to attention, perception, memory, language, reasoning, control, or other narrower processes when evidence permits.

## 1.9.3 Mental representation

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Mental representation** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
A mental representation is an internal state or structure that stands for, models, or carries information about an object, event, relation, rule, or possibility.

## 3. Core Understanding

Mental representations can differ in format, content, stability, and accessibility. A representation may preserve perceptual similarity, encode symbolic relations, summarize category regularities, model causal structure, or represent a possible future state. It can be transiently active or embedded in long-term knowledge, and it can be altered when new information is integrated. Representations are inferred rather than directly observed, so claims about their format require converging evidence from behavior, transfer, errors, neuropsychology, and sometimes neural measures. A successful task does not establish that the hypothesized representation literally exists in the proposed form; it shows that the model can explain the observed pattern. Competing representational theories can therefore produce similar behavioral predictions. Representations are also task-relative: a useful representation preserves the relations required for the operation being performed. Compression can help generalization while damaging exact recall; detail can support discrimination while increasing processing demands. This makes representational adequacy a central concept for reasoning and problem solving. It also explains why changing notation, perspective, or organization can change performance without changing the external information.

Representations should be distinguished from the things they represent and from the processes that operate on them. A memory of an event is not the event itself; a mental model of a machine is not the machine; a concept of an animal is not an animal. Representations can also be transformed. The same underlying information can be encoded as words, a diagram, a spatial arrangement, a numerical expression, or a causal model, and each format can support some operations more effectively than others. Representational format is therefore partly functional. Evidence for a representation comes from systematic transfer and error patterns: if performance remains stable when surface features change but relations remain constant, a relational representation is plausible. If performance changes when the same relations are reformatted, representational accessibility may be involved. Competing theories should be evaluated by prediction rather than by assuming that introspective experience reveals the format directly. This distinction protects the encyclopedia from treating metaphors such as “mental picture” or “mental file” as literal mechanisms.

Representation also has a temporal dimension. A representation can be constructed, maintained, transformed, compressed, retrieved, and discarded. What matters is not only whether information exists in some form but whether it is available in the format required by the current operation. This explains why the same knowledge can be accessible for one task and inaccessible for another.

Representational claims should remain at the strongest level justified by evidence. Functional evidence can support what information is preserved without proving a unique physical format.

A representation should be defined by the information and relations it preserves, not by an illustrative metaphor. This distinction keeps functional claims separate from stronger claims about implementation.

## 1.9.4 Concepts

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Concepts** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Concepts are mental structures that organize knowledge about entities, properties, events, or relations and support classification, inference, prediction, and communication.

## 3. Core Understanding

Concepts support generalization beyond particular experiences by organizing information around meaning, properties, relations, or functions. They can operate at different abstraction levels and can contain graded membership, exceptions, and context-sensitive boundaries. Concept learning is therefore not equivalent to acquiring a dictionary definition. A person may know that an instance belongs to a concept while remaining uncertain about its less typical properties, or may possess conceptual knowledge without a precise verbal label. Concepts can be learned through experience, instruction, language, analogy, causal explanation, and social transmission. Existing concepts influence new learning by directing attention and interpretation, creating both efficiency and potential bias. Theories differ over whether concepts are primarily prototypes, exemplars, theory-like structures, or hybrid combinations. Evidence must therefore include typicality, exception handling, category learning, and transfer to novel instances. A concept is especially informative when it supports predictions or inferences about cases that were not directly encountered. Conceptual knowledge also changes as exceptions accumulate or causal understanding improves. Thus concepts are dynamic representational structures rather than static lists of defining features.

Concepts also support compositional thought. A concept can participate in relations with other concepts, allowing a person to infer properties, form categories, generate explanations, and construct novel combinations. Conceptual knowledge therefore has both content and relational organization. A concept can be broad while containing specialized substructures, and different concepts can overlap without having identical boundaries. Learning can change conceptual structure through differentiation, integration, and restructuring. Differentiation occurs when a previously broad concept is divided into more precise distinctions; integration occurs when separate pieces of knowledge become connected under a common structure. These changes can improve reasoning while also creating new expectations that later influence perception and memory. Measurement should therefore examine not only whether a person can define a concept but whether they can apply it to unfamiliar cases, explain its relations, identify exceptions, and use it to make predictions. Concept knowledge is strongest when it supports flexible inference rather than rote verbal description.

Concepts also provide a basis for inductive inference. Once an instance is categorized conceptually, knowledge associated with that concept can be projected onto the new case. The accuracy of that projection depends on how homogeneous the category is and whether the relevant property is actually diagnostic. Conceptual knowledge therefore supports efficient inference while creating predictable risks of overgeneralization.

Conceptual structure can be measured through transfer and exception handling. Definition recall alone is weak evidence because verbal familiarity can exist without usable conceptual organization.

Conceptual knowledge is demonstrated most clearly when it supports novel inference. Repeating a definition shows verbal access but not necessarily a structured concept.

## 1.9.5 Categories

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Categories** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Categories are groupings of entities or events treated as equivalent in some respect for purposes such as prediction, memory, reasoning, or action.

## 3. Core Understanding

Categories impose functional equivalence on otherwise diverse instances. The relevant equivalence depends on purpose: objects can be grouped by appearance, biological kind, function, ownership, causal role, or social convention. Category membership is therefore not always an intrinsic property of the stimulus. Boundaries can be sharp, graded, overlapping, hierarchical, or context-dependent. Categories support efficient prediction by allowing knowledge associated with a group to generalize to new cases, but generalization can fail when members are heterogeneous or the environment changes. Category knowledge also interacts with decision criteria. A person can possess the same category representation while changing the threshold for assigning an ambiguous item because the consequences of errors changed. Research should therefore distinguish category structure from categorization policy. Development and culture can alter which dimensions become diagnostic, while expertise can make causal or functional features more salient than surface appearance. Measurement through classification, generalization, typicality, and transfer can reveal different aspects of category organization. A category is consequently a structured way of organizing information, not merely a label attached to a collection of objects.

Category structure is partly induced by the environment and partly shaped by the purposes of the learner. Natural categories can have uncertain boundaries because members vary continuously, whereas artificial categories may be defined by explicit rules. Categories can also be hierarchical, allowing a single instance to belong simultaneously to a broad class and several subordinate classes. This hierarchy supports different levels of prediction and explanation. Context can change which level is most useful: a person may identify an object as a tool in one situation and as a specific instrument in another. Category errors can therefore reveal not only what information was represented but which level and criterion were selected. Culture and learning history also influence category structure, especially for socially meaningful categories. Scientific analysis should distinguish descriptive category membership from normative or socially imposed classifications. The psychological category is the organization used by the person, which may or may not correspond to an external taxonomy.

Category learning is sensitive to feedback and consequences. When classification errors have different costs, people can alter criteria without changing the underlying category representation. Category research should therefore distinguish what people know from how they use that knowledge under particular decision demands. This distinction is important whenever ambiguous instances are common.

Category research should distinguish the category known by the person from the criterion used in a particular decision. The same knowledge can therefore produce different classifications under changed goals.

Category boundaries can change with task demands while the underlying knowledge remains stable. This is why classification and representation must be measured separately.

## 1.9.6 Prototypes

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Prototypes** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
A prototype is a graded or summary representation of characteristic features associated with members of a category.

## 3. Core Understanding

Prototype representations summarize central or characteristic properties of category members. They are useful when a category has a meaningful central tendency because similarity to the prototype can support rapid generalization and graded typicality. A prototype need not correspond to any actual member and may instead reflect an abstraction across experiences. The approach becomes less sufficient when atypical members contain diagnostic information or when category membership depends on relational or causal structure that averaging obscures. Prototype representations can also change with the distribution of experience: repeated exposure to unusual members can shift what appears typical. This means prototype effects depend on learning history and attention, not just on objective stimulus structure. Evidence should compare prototype-based predictions with exemplar-based and theory-based alternatives. Typicality ratings alone cannot establish a prototype mechanism because many representational models predict graded typicality. Stronger evidence comes from transfer to novel items, category learning, exception behavior, and sensitivity to distributional changes. Prototype knowledge can coexist with specific exemplar memories, and the relative contribution may depend on task demands and expertise.

Prototype representations can be multidimensional rather than a single average point. Different features may contribute unequally, and the weighting can change with goals and expertise. A prototype may therefore be better understood as a structured summary than as a literal mean of all encountered examples. Prototype effects can also arise from repeated exposure and frequency distributions without requiring an explicit prototype computation. This is why typicality alone cannot establish the mechanism. Stronger tests manipulate the distribution of category members and examine whether classification shifts toward the new central tendency. Prototype-based knowledge can coexist with exceptions represented separately, allowing people to recognize an atypical member despite low prototype similarity. This hybrid organization helps explain why categories can show both graded typicality and sharp responses to diagnostic cases. Prototype theory is therefore a model of how category regularities might be represented, not a claim that all category decisions are calculated from a single central image.

Prototype representations can also be hierarchical. A person may possess prototypes at several levels of abstraction, allowing rapid movement from broad to specific categories. Which level is activated depends on task demands and context. Prototype effects therefore need not imply one fixed representation; they can emerge from multiple summaries operating at different scales.

Prototype evidence is strongest when exposure distributions are manipulated and classification shifts in the predicted direction. Typicality alone is insufficient because several models can generate it.

Prototype effects can coexist with exemplar sensitivity. A central tendency may guide broad classification while specific exceptions retain independent influence.

## 1.9.7 Exemplars

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Exemplars** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Exemplars are representations of specific encountered instances that can contribute to category learning and later classification.

## 3. Core Understanding

Exemplar representations preserve information about individual encountered instances and allow new cases to be evaluated through similarity to stored experiences. This can explain sensitivity to unusual examples, recency, frequency, and distinctive category members that a central tendency would obscure. Exemplar approaches connect categorization closely to memory but do not require conscious retrieval of each example. Many stored instances can contribute implicitly to a response through their similarity structure. The main challenge is explaining how large numbers of exemplars can be used efficiently and how abstraction emerges when exact instances vary widely. Hybrid theories therefore allow exemplar information, prototype-like summaries, and causal knowledge to contribute simultaneously. Experimental tests examine whether behavior is better predicted by individual-case similarity, category averages, or structured rules. Expertise may shift the balance because experts possess more diagnostic exemplars and richer abstractions. Exemplar representations are especially valuable for explaining variability within categories, but they should not be treated as the only possible representation. Their scientific status is that of a model whose predictions can be compared against alternatives.

Exemplar representation is especially useful for explaining category variability and sensitivity to unusual cases. A learner can preserve information about individual examples and use the distribution of those memories to determine similarity to a new instance. The psychological system need not consciously retrieve each example; activation can operate over a network of stored instances. Frequency, recency, distinctiveness, and contextual associations can therefore influence classification. The challenge is computational: if every instance were compared equally, categorization could become inefficient. Attention and learned organization can reduce this burden, and hybrid theories allow summary representations to emerge from repeated exemplar experience. Exemplar models are also closely connected to memory because category knowledge can depend on what examples were encountered and retained. Measurement should therefore manipulate exposure distributions, atypical exemplars, and transfer to novel cases. The goal is to determine whether behavior is better predicted by individual-instance similarity, category summaries, explicit rules, or structured causal knowledge.

Exemplar information can preserve contextual detail that prototypes necessarily compress. This can be useful when categories contain exceptions or when a particular example is highly diagnostic. It also makes exemplar-based behavior sensitive to memory quality and retrieval conditions. Category learning therefore cannot be separated completely from the memory architecture that preserves examples.

Exemplar evidence is strongest when unusual and recent instances influence classification in ways that a central tendency cannot explain. This makes learning history a crucial part of category theory.

Exemplar influence is especially informative when atypical cases change later classification. Such effects reveal the importance of learning history and memory accessibility.

## 1.9.8 Categorization

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Categorization** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Categorization is the process of assigning or organizing instances into categories using available information, goals, and learned knowledge.

## 3. Core Understanding

Categorization requires selecting relevant information, comparing an instance with category knowledge, resolving ambiguity, and applying a criterion. These operations can be performed rapidly when category evidence is strong or slowly when multiple categories compete. Goals change which features are treated as diagnostic, so the same stimulus can be categorized differently under different tasks without any change in sensory input. Learning can also alter feature weighting, category boundaries, and the speed with which relevant dimensions are accessed. Errors may reflect poor perception, incomplete knowledge, inappropriate generalization, competing categories, or a decision criterion rather than one global categorization deficit. Classification accuracy and reaction time measure different aspects of the process, while transfer to unfamiliar instances tests whether the underlying structure has been learned. Categorization can rely on prototypes, exemplars, rules, causal theories, or combinations. It should therefore be distinguished from category representation: the category is an organized knowledge structure, while categorization is the act of using information to assign or organize a current case.

Categorization can be viewed as evidence accumulation followed by a category decision, but the evidence itself depends on attention and representation. A person may notice color, shape, function, causal properties, or social cues depending on task goals and learned relevance. Ambiguous cases can produce competition among categories, and decision criteria can shift when the consequences of errors change. This means categorization performance contains both representational and decision components. Learning can alter category boundaries, feature weighting, and response speed, while context can activate different category systems. Transfer is crucial because accurate classification of trained examples can be achieved through memorization without a general category representation. Good measurement therefore includes novel cases, exceptions, ambiguous cases, and changes in context. Categorization is also temporally dynamic: early learning may depend on explicit rules, while later performance can become more automatic. The process is thus an interaction among category knowledge, current evidence, goals, and control.

Categorization is also a form of compression. It reduces many possible distinctions to a smaller set of functionally useful classes. The benefit is reduced cognitive complexity; the cost is loss of detail and possible overgeneralization. Effective categories preserve distinctions that matter for the current goal while ignoring distinctions that do not.

Categorization is also sensitive to uncertainty. Ambiguous instances can produce graded evidence before a discrete response is selected, linking category use to decision criteria.

Category decisions can be probabilistic before becoming categorical at response. Evidence accumulation and criterion setting therefore both contribute to observed classification.

## 1.9.9 Concept formation

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Concept formation** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Concept formation is the acquisition or construction of a mental concept through detecting regularities, relations, or defining features across experience.

## 3. Core Understanding

Concept formation involves discovering or constructing abstractions that remain useful beyond the examples that produced them. Learners may detect shared features, relations, causal dependencies, or predictive regularities and use them to organize future cases. Concept formation can be guided by explicit instruction or discovered through feedback and statistical structure. Surface similarity may support early learning but can become a poor guide when deeper relational structure matters. A learner can also memorize examples or labels without forming a transferable concept. The strongest evidence for concept formation is therefore generalization to novel cases, especially cases that differ in surface form while preserving the underlying relation. Exception handling is equally informative because it shows whether the representation can accommodate boundary conditions. Prior knowledge changes concept formation by determining which dimensions are noticed and which interpretations are plausible. Concept formation is consequently an interaction between new evidence and existing knowledge rather than a blank-slate extraction process.

Concept formation can be treated as a search for structure in experience. Learners must determine which dimensions vary systematically, which relations predict outcomes, and which features are incidental. This can involve statistical learning, hypothesis testing, analogical comparison, causal explanation, or feedback. The learner's prior knowledge determines what patterns are noticed, so concept formation is not purely bottom-up. A misleading training distribution can produce a concept that works locally but fails under transfer. Conversely, sparse examples can sometimes produce powerful abstractions when the underlying relation is clear. Measurement should therefore include generalization, exception handling, explanation, and prediction under altered surface conditions. Concept formation also interacts with language because labels can draw attention to distinctions, but labels do not guarantee that the corresponding conceptual structure has been acquired. A robust concept is demonstrated when the learner can use the abstraction flexibly rather than merely reproduce the examples or definition used during training.

Concept formation is often incremental. Early representations may be broad or based on salient surface features, while later evidence can produce differentiation and deeper relational structure. Feedback and comparison accelerate this refinement. A final concept therefore reflects learning history rather than a single moment of discovery.

## 1.9.10 Schemas

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Schemas** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Schemas are organized knowledge structures that represent expected relations among concepts, events, roles, or situations.

## 3. Core Understanding

Schemas organize expectations about relations among elements of objects, events, roles, or situations. They support efficient comprehension because predictable information can be processed against an existing structure rather than constructed from scratch. Schemas also guide attention and memory, increasing the probability that schema-relevant information is encoded while plausible but absent details may later be supplied during reconstruction. A schema can therefore increase both efficiency and error depending on environmental regularity. Schema change can occur through accumulation of exceptions, contradiction, new evidence, or restructuring of the knowledge system. Not every expectation is a schema, and schemas should not be equated with simple memories of individual episodes. They are more general structures that organize multiple experiences. Measurement can examine prediction, recall intrusions, transfer, and sensitivity to violations. Schemas also vary across development and culture because regularities differ across environments. Their scientific value lies in explaining how prior knowledge shapes processing without requiring that every expectation be consciously represented.

Schemas can be understood as probabilistic expectations about relations rather than rigid templates. They contain likely elements and relationships but allow variation. When information is incomplete, schemas help fill gaps; when information conflicts strongly with the schema, the person may revise expectations or reinterpret the evidence. This creates a tension between efficiency and accuracy. Schema-driven processing can reduce cognitive load in familiar situations while increasing false recognition of expected but absent details. Schemas also guide what receives attention, meaning that they influence encoding before they influence later memory. Development and culture shape schemas because repeated experience teaches which relations are regular. Measurement should therefore examine both facilitation and intrusion, and should compare familiar and schema-inconsistent situations. A schema is not merely a memory structure and should not be treated as a single stored script. It is an organized expectation system that influences interpretation, prediction, memory, and action.

Schema activation can be partial rather than all-or-none. A situation may activate several overlapping schemas, with attention and context determining which structure dominates interpretation. This helps explain why the same event can be understood differently by different people without assuming that one person simply lacks the relevant information.

Schema effects should be evaluated against both expected and violated events. This reveals when prior knowledge facilitates processing and when it produces systematic intrusion.

Schemas are predictive structures, so their accuracy depends on the regularity of the environment. Violated schemas provide especially informative tests of flexibility and updating.

## 1.9.11 Scripts

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Scripts** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Scripts are event-knowledge structures representing typical sequences, roles, actions, and expectations within recurring situations.

## 3. Core Understanding

Scripts are schemas organized around familiar event sequences, roles, and expected actions. They help people understand incomplete descriptions and predict what is likely to happen next because the sequence provides a structured prior. Scripts can include obligatory, typical, and optional elements rather than a single rigid order. Their usefulness depends on contextual fit: a restaurant script can guide expectations in an ordinary setting but mislead when the event is unusual or intentionally structured differently. Script knowledge is culturally learned, so different communities can develop different expectations for the same nominal event. Scripts can also produce memory intrusions when people later remember expected actions that were not actually observed. They therefore connect event comprehension with reconstructive memory. Measurement should distinguish knowledge of the sequence from memory for a particular episode. Script flexibility is important because real situations often contain branches and interruptions. A script is consequently a structured expectation about event organization, not a literal recording of a repeated routine.

Scripts can contain roles, temporal order, causal dependencies, and optional branches. Their structure is learned through repeated experience and social instruction, so scripts vary across contexts and cultures. A script can support rapid comprehension because the person does not need to infer every expected action independently. It can also generate errors when an expected step is inserted into memory even though it did not occur. This makes scripts relevant to both comprehension and reconstructive remembering. Script knowledge can become highly automatized but remains revisable when unusual events repeatedly violate expectations. Measurement should distinguish knowledge of a canonical sequence from memory for an actual episode and should test whether people recognize deviations. Scripts can also support planning because they provide default action sequences, but a script is not a plan: a script describes what usually happens, whereas a plan represents what the person intends to make happen. This distinction is important for keeping event knowledge separate from goal-directed control.

Scripts can support prediction because the expected sequence supplies likely next events. They can also influence action by making some responses more accessible than others. When the environment violates the script, successful cognition requires noticing the discrepancy rather than automatically completing the familiar sequence. Script flexibility is therefore part of adaptive functioning.

Script knowledge should be tested across variants of the same event. Flexible scripts preserve structure while allowing optional steps, whereas rigid scripts overpredict canonical sequences.

Scripts can support action as well as comprehension, but intended action should not be confused with knowledge of what usually happens. This boundary separates scripts from plans.

## 1.9.12 Mental models

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Mental models** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Mental models are internal representations of how a system, situation, or process is structured and how changes within it may unfold.

## 3. Core Understanding

Mental models represent systems or situations in terms of relations that can support prediction, explanation, and simulation. A model can include components, causal links, constraints, and possible states. People often use simplified models because representing every variable is computationally expensive. A simplified model can be useful if it preserves the relations needed for the current task, but it can produce systematic errors when hidden variables become important. Mental models are therefore evaluated by the predictions and interventions they support, not by verbal fluency alone. A person may describe a system accurately while failing to predict what happens after a change because the relational structure was not represented. Model revision occurs when contradictory evidence changes subsequent predictions rather than merely being acknowledged verbally. Mental models differ from schemas in emphasis: schemas organize expected knowledge structures, while mental models more directly support reasoning about a particular system or possible state. Both can interact with memory, language, and perception.

Mental models are particularly important when people must reason about systems whose behavior is not directly visible. A model can represent components, causal relations, constraints, and possible state transitions. People can then mentally intervene on the model and infer consequences. Model errors often arise because relevant variables are omitted or because causal direction is represented incorrectly. Simplification is unavoidable, so the question is whether the model preserves the structure required for the current inference. Mental models can be updated through contradiction, feedback, instruction, or comparison with external evidence. Verbal explanation alone is insufficient evidence of model quality because a person may repeat correct descriptions without being able to derive novel consequences. Prediction under intervention and transfer to changed conditions provide stronger tests. Mental models overlap with schemas and knowledge structures but emphasize relational simulation of systems and states. They are therefore central to causal reasoning, planning, and problem solving.

Mental models are especially testable through counterfactual and intervention questions. If changing one component produces the predicted downstream effect, the model captures relevant structure. If prediction fails systematically, the model may omit a variable or represent a relation incorrectly. This makes mental models directly relevant to causal reasoning and problem solving.

Mental-model evidence is strongest when predictions survive interventions and changed surface descriptions. Verbal description alone can be supported by rote knowledge without a usable model.

Model validity is best tested by intervention and transfer. A model that only reproduces familiar descriptions has limited explanatory value.

## 1.9.13 Knowledge structures

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Knowledge structures** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Knowledge structures are organized networks or systems of stored information and relations that support interpretation, retrieval, inference, and learning.

## 3. Core Understanding

Knowledge structures describe how accumulated information is organized into connected relations rather than stored as isolated facts. They can include semantic networks, hierarchies, schemas, scripts, causal models, rules, and domain-specific procedures. Organization affects retrieval, inference, transfer, and learning because connected knowledge provides multiple routes for access and combination. Experts often appear faster partly because their knowledge is organized into meaningful units that reduce search. Knowledge structures can also contain contradictions or misconceptions, so having many facts does not guarantee accurate reasoning. New learning can strengthen existing links, create distinctions, or require restructuring when information conflicts with prior organization. Measurement should therefore examine relational recall, explanation, transfer, and ability to use knowledge in new conditions. The concept is broader than any single schema or concept and should be used to describe organization at the level required by the task.

Knowledge structures are not necessarily accurate; misconceptions can be richly organized and therefore resistant to correction. New learning can be assimilated when compatible, differentiated when partially compatible, or require restructuring when contradictory. Measurement should therefore assess explanation, relational recall, transfer, prediction, and response to contradiction. The construct provides a bridge between memory and active cognition: retained information becomes usable knowledge through organization. Experts often reduce this competition by encoding diagnostic differences explicitly. Knowledge structure therefore affects both facilitation and error.

Knowledge structures are best studied through relations among facts, not only the number of facts remembered. Organization determines what can be inferred and transferred.

## 1.9.14 Mental imagery

## 1. Purpose

## 2. Definition
Mental imagery is the experience or construction of percept-like representations in the absence of corresponding immediate sensory input.

## 3. Core Understanding

Mental imagery represents sensory-like or spatial information without the corresponding immediate stimulus. It can support navigation, mental rotation, motor preparation, planning, creative combination, and anticipation. Imagery can be visual, auditory, spatial, motor, or multimodal, and subjective vividness varies substantially across people. Imagery can be generated deliberately or arise spontaneously, and repeated imagery can change later memory through source-monitoring effects. Experimental approaches include transformation tasks, interference with perception, spatial judgments, subjective ratings, and neuropsychological dissociations. Competing theories differ over depictive versus propositional representation, and evidence may support different formats for different tasks. Imagery is therefore a family of representational processes rather than one universal internal picture.

Symbolic thought uses signs whose meaning depends on learned relations or conventions. This supports mathematics, formal logic, programming, language, maps, and other abstract systems. Symbolic competence depends on learning the conventions of the system and maintaining the distinction between the symbol and its referent. People can manipulate a notation correctly while misunderstanding what it means, which is why formal performance and conceptual understanding should be measured separately. Symbolic processing can also interact with imagery and concrete representations; diagrams can combine symbolic and spatial structure, while numerical notation can compress quantities into manipulable forms. Unfamiliar symbolic systems impose working-memory demands and may produce errors that reflect decoding rather than reasoning. Symbolic thought is therefore powerful but dependent on representational fluency and semantic mapping.

Symbolic thought becomes more powerful when symbols can be composed recursively. This allows cognition to represent nested relations and hypothetical structures that are difficult to express through direct perception. The same compositional power can create errors when a person follows formal syntax while misunderstanding semantic constraints.

Symbolic competence requires both manipulation and interpretation. Correct syntax without correct mapping is not equivalent to conceptual understanding.

## 1.9.18 Rule learning

Transfer to novel cases tests whether the learner acquired the underlying relation rather than memorized specific responses. Rule learning interacts with categories, because rules often classify instances by features or relations, and with problem solving, because rules define legal operators. ## 1.9.20 Reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Reasoning** within the reasoning architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Reasoning is the process of deriving, evaluating, or revising conclusions from information, assumptions, evidence, or representations.

## 3. Core Understanding

Reasoning is a family of inferential operations rather than a single capacity. Its quality can be evaluated against different standards: logical validity, probabilistic calibration, causal adequacy, explanatory power, or practical usefulness. These standards can disagree, so a conclusion can be logically valid yet empirically false because its premises are wrong, or practically useful without being formally optimal. Psychological reasoning also depends on how premises are represented, what knowledge is available, how alternatives are searched, and how confidence is monitored. Some inferences are rapid and automatic while others require deliberate working-memory coordination. Errors can arise from weak representation, incomplete search, inappropriate heuristics, memory limitations, or biased evaluation. Measurement should therefore examine both the structure of the inference and the process that produced it. Reasoning also differs from decision-making because reasoning concerns implications and evidence, while decision-making additionally incorporates preferences and action consequences. A strong account identifies the inferential form, the normative benchmark, the information available to the person, and the conditions under which the conclusion should change.

Reasoning can be decomposed into representation, inference, evaluation, and confidence. A person can fail before inference because the premises were misunderstood, or after inference because the conclusion was evaluated against an inappropriate criterion. Normative standards also differ: logical validity asks whether the conclusion follows, probability asks how strongly evidence supports it, causal reasoning asks whether interventions would change outcomes, and practical reasoning asks whether an action serves the goal. These should not be collapsed into one rationality score. Reasoning is also sensitive to knowledge and context. A domain expert may solve an unfamiliar-looking problem rapidly because relevant structures are already organized, while a novice may need deliberate search. Measurement should therefore vary content, assess transfer, and distinguish final correctness from confidence and process. Reasoning can be automatic or controlled and can be supported by external representations. The chapter's central principle is that good reasoning is conditional on the structure of the inferential problem and the evidence available.

Reasoning is also affected by the representation of uncertainty and alternatives. A conclusion can appear strong when competing hypotheses are not represented at all. Generating alternatives therefore changes the inferential space before evidence is evaluated. Good reasoning includes not only deriving conclusions but structuring the set of possibilities being compared.

Reasoning should be evaluated with the relevant normative standard made explicit. Without that criterion, “good reasoning” can become an undefined global judgment.

Reasoning should specify its evidence standard. Validity, probability, causality, explanation, and usefulness are different criteria and require different measurements.

## 1.9.22 Inductive reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Inductive reasoning** within the reasoning architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Inductive reasoning generalizes, predicts, or infers broader patterns from particular observations or examples.

## 3. Core Understanding

Inductive reasoning extends observed evidence toward generalizations, predictions, or classifications that are not logically guaranteed. Its quality depends on the representativeness and independence of observations, sample size, measurement reliability, base rates, and the stability of the environment. Domain knowledge also changes induction because people can recognize which features are predictive and which are accidental. A large biased sample can support poor inference, while a smaller but representative sample can support stronger generalization. Induction is therefore not simply “reasoning from examples”; it is evidence-weighting under uncertainty. People can also use similarity, causal models, or learned statistical regularities as different routes to an inductive conclusion. Measurement should compare predictions with outcomes and examine calibration rather than only whether a single guess was correct. Inductive reasoning is particularly important when certainty is impossible, making uncertainty representation and confidence adjustment central components.

Inductive reasoning depends on evidence that is incomplete by definition. Generalization is stronger when observations are representative, independent, reliably measured, and sufficiently varied to reveal the relevant structure. Domain knowledge can help identify which features are likely to predict the target, but it can also introduce assumptions that bias interpretation. Induction therefore combines evidence with models of what matters. People can generalize by similarity, frequency, causal understanding, or learned statistical regularity, and these routes need not produce identical conclusions. Measurement should examine prediction on unobserved cases and calibration across repeated outcomes. A correct single prediction does not prove good inductive reasoning. The process is especially sensitive to selection bias and base-rate information, making the sampling history part of the reasoning problem. Inductive reasoning is therefore a disciplined response to uncertainty rather than a weaker version of deduction.

Induction is especially sensitive to environmental change. A regularity learned in one population may fail when the sampling process changes. Effective inductive reasoning therefore requires some representation of where the evidence came from and whether the generating process remains stable.

Inductive reasoning should account for the data-generating process because evidence quality depends on how observations were sampled.

It is also iterative: new evidence can eliminate explanations, alter their relative plausibility, or reveal that the original problem representation was incomplete. Measurement should therefore examine alternative generation and the ability to identify discriminating evidence. Abduction does not establish certainty; it produces a hypothesis whose strength depends on how well it accounts for observations relative to competitors. This makes it a key bridge between reasoning and model construction.

Abduction can be improved by seeking evidence that would make competing explanations diverge. This turns explanation into a testable process rather than a search for the most satisfying story. Causal reasoning concerns whether and how changing one variable would alter another under specified conditions. It can draw on covariation, temporal order, interventions, mechanisms, and counterfactual dependence. Mere association is insufficient because common causes, selection effects, reverse causation, and measurement artifacts can produce correlations without direct causation. A causal mental model specifies relevant variables and relations and can therefore support predictions about interventions. People can reason causally from observation, but formal experimental designs provide stronger leverage when alternative explanations are controlled. Causal reasoning is also conditional: a relation can hold in one context and fail when mechanisms or background conditions change. Measurement should distinguish causal belief, mechanistic explanation, and causal accuracy. Causal reasoning connects representation with action because interventions require imagining altered system states.

Causal reasoning requires representing what would happen under changes, not merely what is associated in observation. People may use covariation, temporal order, interventions, mechanistic knowledge, and counterfactual dependence, with different cues becoming more or less reliable across contexts. Confounding can create persuasive associations that disappear under intervention. Causal reasoning is therefore highly dependent on the mental model of the system. A person may correctly observe that two variables co-vary while holding an incorrect causal explanation. Measurement should distinguish prediction of outcomes from explanation of causal structure and should include intervention or counterfactual tests when possible. Causal beliefs can also be revised when interventions contradict the existing model. This makes causal reasoning dynamic and closely connected to learning and problem solving.

Causal reasoning also requires distinguishing interventions from observations. An observed relationship can remain stable while an intervention produces a different result if the association was mediated by another variable. Causal models therefore support reasoning about changes that have not yet occurred, not merely summarizing past covariation.

## 1.9.25 Counterfactual thinking

Counterfactual thinking represents what might have happened if some feature of the actual situation had been different. Counterfactual thinking creates alternative worlds by modifying selected aspects of actual events while preserving other features. Counterfactuals can support learning by revealing how a different action might have changed an outcome, but they can also generate regret or biased causal attribution. Upward counterfactuals emphasize better possible outcomes; downward counterfactuals emphasize worse ones. The causal usefulness of either depends on whether the altered feature is genuinely connected to the outcome. Counterfactuals also support planning because imagined alternatives can identify contingencies that future action should prepare for. Measurement should assess structural validity, not merely vividness or emotional impact. Counterfactual reasoning is therefore a constrained simulation of alternatives that links causal inference, planning, and evaluation.

Counterfactual thinking can also reveal assumptions about responsibility and controllability. People often alter features they believe they could have changed, which makes counterfactuals useful for learning but potentially biased for causal attribution. The structure of the imagined alternative therefore matters as much as its emotional intensity.

Counterfactual structure reveals which feature the person treats as causally relevant, making it informative for both reasoning and attribution.

## 1.9.28 Analogical reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Analogical reasoning** within the reasoning architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Analogical reasoning uses structural or relational correspondences between a familiar source and a target to support inference.

## 3. Core Understanding

Analogical reasoning maps relational structure from a known case onto a different case. The critical similarity is often structural rather than perceptual: two situations may look different while sharing the same causal or relational pattern. Successful analogy requires retrieving a relevant source, identifying corresponding elements, aligning relations, and adapting the mapped structure to the target. Surface similarity can facilitate retrieval but can also distract from deeper correspondence. Prior knowledge strongly influences which analogies are available and how their structure is interpreted. Measurement should compare transfer across cases with different surface similarity and examine whether the solver preserves the relational pattern. Analogical reasoning can support explanation, learning, scientific model building, and problem solving because it allows knowledge to be reused without literal copying. It is therefore a form of structured transfer rather than simple similarity matching.

Analogical reasoning depends on recognizing relational correspondence. Surface similarity can help retrieve a source but may also mislead if the shared appearance is irrelevant. Structural similarity is more powerful for transfer because it preserves the relation that actually supports the solution. The process can be decomposed into source retrieval, alignment, mapping, adaptation, and evaluation. Prior knowledge influences each stage. People often fail to use a useful analogy because the source is not retrieved spontaneously, not because they cannot understand it once shown. Comparison prompts can therefore improve transfer by making common structure explicit. Analogical reasoning is important for scientific explanation, teaching, troubleshooting, and problem solving because it allows a known relational pattern to be reused in a new context.

Analogical reasoning can fail even when the source is understood because the target representation does not make the relevant relation salient. Effective transfer therefore depends on mapping cues. Teaching comparison can improve performance by making structural correspondences explicit rather than merely adding more examples.

They also interact with expertise because experts often possess domain-specific cues that make simple rules more powerful. Measurement should evaluate heuristic performance against environmental structure rather than judging it against an abstract optimization benchmark alone. A heuristic is therefore best understood as a bounded strategy whose quality depends on task, information, time, and ecological fit.

Heuristics simplify cognition by exploiting selected cues rather than evaluating every possibility. Their quality depends on the environment. In a stable environment, a simple cue may outperform a complex model because the cue is highly diagnostic and computationally cheap. In a changing environment, the same heuristic can fail. Heuristics can therefore be adaptive strategies rather than defects. Research should identify the information the heuristic ignores, the assumption that makes it work, and the conditions that cause failure. This ecological approach is more informative than labeling all heuristics as bias.

Heuristic performance should also be judged against the cost of computation. A mathematically superior strategy may be impractical when information is incomplete or decisions must be made rapidly. Heuristics can therefore be rational relative to constraints even when they depart from abstract optimization.

## 1.9.49 Inhibitory control

## 1. Purpose

Inhibition can be proactive, established before interference occurs, or reactive, recruited after conflict is detected. Performance depends on the strength and timing of the competing response, task instructions, motivation, and speed-accuracy tradeoffs. A failure to stop a response may therefore reflect unusually strong competition rather than a general absence of control. Measurement should identify the specific operation being inhibited and compare conditions that manipulate response strength and preparation. Inhibitory control varies by what is being inhibited, how strong the competitor is, and when control is recruited. Motor stopping, attentional filtering, conflict resolution, and suppression of an unwanted memory can all be called inhibition but may rely on partially different operations. Proactive control prepares against expected conflict; reactive control responds after conflict appears. A failure on one inhibition task therefore does not establish a global inability to inhibit. Measurement should manipulate response strength, preparation, and conflict to determine which control operation is involved. Inhibition can also be adaptive only when applied selectively; suppressing relevant information can be as harmful as failing to suppress irrelevant information.

Inhibition can be selective and context-dependent. Suppressing a response that is irrelevant now may be harmful if the environment changes and that response becomes relevant. Effective inhibition therefore requires monitoring the task context and releasing suppression when conditions change.

## 1.9.60 Cognitive load

Cognitive load concerns the demands that task structure places on limited cognitive resources. ## 2. Definition
Bounded rationality describes decision and reasoning processes constrained by limited information, time, computational capacity, and other cognitive or environmental limits.

## 3. Core Understanding

Bounded rationality describes cognition under limits of time, information, knowledge, and computation. People therefore often use procedures that are feasible and satisfactory rather than evaluating every option and consequence. A bounded strategy is not necessarily irrational: its success depends on how well it matches the structure of the environment. Stable environments can make simple cues highly predictive, while volatile environments may require broader information search. Bounded rationality also separates internal limits from environmental constraints. A person may appear limited because information is unavailable, not because the underlying reasoning capacity is insufficient. The framework is broader than any one heuristic because it explains why optimization may be unrealistic as a psychological model. Measurement should evaluate outcomes relative to the information and computational resources actually available. Bounded rationality therefore links cognition to ecological structure and emphasizes fit between strategy and environment.

Bounded rationality explains why human cognition often uses satisfactory procedures rather than exhaustive optimization. Limits on time, information, computation, and attention make complete search unrealistic. The quality of a strategy depends on ecological structure: a simple rule can be highly effective when environmental cues are stable and predictive, while the same rule can fail under change. Bounded rationality is therefore not a synonym for error. It is a framework for evaluating cognition relative to realistic constraints and available information. It also highlights environmental scaffolding: stable structures can reduce the computation required internally.

Bounded rationality also explains why simple strategies can outperform complex ones under uncertainty. A complex model can overfit limited data, while a simple cue may generalize robustly. The appropriate strategy depends on information quality, environmental stability, and computational cost rather than complexity alone.

## 2. Definition

## 3. Core Understanding

Cognitive reflection concerns the tendency to inspect an initially compelling response and consider whether a more deliberate analysis is warranted. Cognitive reflection is therefore one component of metacognitive control and conflict resolution. Its effectiveness depends on both the willingness to reconsider and the quality of the representations and knowledge available for reconsideration.

Cognitive reflection involves detecting that an intuitive response may be unreliable and engaging additional analysis. It therefore requires both monitoring and control. A person may fail because the intuitive answer produces no conflict signal, because the relevant rule is unknown, or because the alternative reasoning process is itself flawed. Reflection should not be equated with intelligence or with always choosing the slower response. Its value is conditional on whether additional analysis improves the representation and evaluation of the problem. Measurement should separate initial and final answers and assess whether corrections are actually more accurate.

Reflection requires a reason to distrust the first answer. If no conflict is detected, deliberate analysis may not occur. This makes reflection partly dependent on metacognitive sensitivity and partly on available corrective knowledge. It is therefore not equivalent to merely taking more time.

## 2. Definition

## 3. Core Understanding

Belief revision occurs when an existing belief changes in strength, scope, interpretation, or content in response to new information. Revision can be gradual or discontinuous and does not always require complete abandonment. Contradictory evidence can be discounted, reinterpreted, attributed to a different cause, or integrated, depending on source credibility, prior knowledge, and the strength of the existing belief. Strong evidence may change a central proposition and require restructuring of connected assumptions, while weak evidence may be absorbed as an exception. Measurement should examine the pattern of changes across a belief network rather than only a final yes/no response. Revision is successful when the representation becomes better calibrated to the available evidence, not merely when the person verbally agrees with new information.

Belief revision can create cascading changes when beliefs are interdependent. Changing one causal assumption may alter predictions, explanations, and related expectations. Stable revision therefore involves maintaining coherence across the network rather than changing one isolated proposition. This helps explain why deeply organized beliefs can be difficult to revise.

## 1.9.65 Belief updating

Evidence order can also matter because earlier information changes expectations about later observations. Sequential designs are therefore especially useful for studying updating. Measurement should track the trajectory of belief strength across observations and compare changes with the evidential value of each observation. Updating differs from one-time revision mainly in temporal structure: it describes the ongoing state of a belief as information streams through time.

Belief updating is sequential and therefore sensitive to evidence order, uncertainty, and reliability. Normative Bayesian updating provides one formal benchmark for some tasks, but psychological updating also depends on memory, attention, source evaluation, and causal structure. Underreaction preserves outdated beliefs in changing environments, while overreaction makes beliefs unstable in noisy environments. Confirmation tendencies can produce asymmetric encoding and retrieval of evidence. Sequential experiments can therefore reveal whether people weight observations according to diagnosticity and whether they preserve uncertainty. Updating is a dynamic process that changes the current belief state after each new observation rather than a one-time revision.

Updating can be evaluated by how appropriately belief change tracks information value. Diagnostic observations should produce larger changes than weak or redundant observations. Sequential evidence also permits study of recency, order, and confirmation effects. Updating is therefore both a statistical and representational process.
