## 1.9.1 Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Thinking** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Thinking is the coordinated psychological activity of manipulating, evaluating, transforming, and using information in ways that support representation, reasoning, problem solving, planning, and goal-directed action.

## 3. Core Understanding
Thinking has a distinct cognitive function that cannot be reduced to the broader chapter domain. Its operation is expressed through the specific process described in its Definition and is shaped by the representation, information, goals, prior knowledge, task demands, and context available to the person. Performance attributed to Thinking may depend on interacting processes such as attention, perception, memory, language, motivation, or decision-making; therefore the construct should be interpreted by isolating its defining operation rather than treating any observed task performance as a pure measure of it. Empirical conclusions should distinguish the phenomenon from proposed mechanisms and should remain sensitive to measurement and boundary conditions.

Thinking is not a single operation but an umbrella for coordinated transformations of represented information. It can involve maintaining a representation, changing its format, combining relations, comparing alternatives, simulating possibilities, drawing inferences, or selecting operations that serve a goal. The same outward answer can arise from different internal routes, so performance on a thinking task does not by itself identify a mechanism. Thinking also operates over different representational materials: perceptual information, episodic memories, semantic knowledge, language, spatial models, numerical structures, and action possibilities. Its efficiency depends on the quality and accessibility of those representations as well as on control over attention and working memory. A deep account therefore distinguishes content from operation: knowing a fact is different from transforming it, and producing an answer is different from the process that generated it. Thinking can be deliberate or relatively automatic, verbal or nonverbal, concrete or abstract, and individual operations can be distributed across multiple timescales. Measurement consequently requires specifying the task's representational demands, response requirements, and competing strategies. Thinking should not be treated as synonymous with intelligence, consciousness, reasoning, or decision-making; those are overlapping but analytically distinct constructs.

## 1. Purpose

## 2. Definition
Cognition is the broad family of mental processes involved in acquiring, representing, transforming, storing, retrieving, and using information.

## 3. Core Understanding

Its operation is expressed through the specific process described in its Definition and is shaped by the representation, information, goals, prior knowledge, task demands, and context available to the person. Cognition is a broad organizing term for information-related mental processes, but its breadth creates a boundary problem. Perception, attention, memory, language, reasoning, and decision-making can all be cognitive without being interchangeable. Cognition is therefore most useful as a domain-level construct describing processes that acquire, represent, transform, retain, and use information. Its mechanisms are heterogeneous: some cognitive operations depend on learned knowledge, some on temporary activation, some on perceptual computation, and some on control processes. Cognition is also embodied in task context because the same individual can perform differently when information is externally represented, when cues are absent, or when action changes the environment. Scientific measurement should therefore avoid treating one laboratory task as a pure index of “cognition.” The concept organizes families of processes rather than providing one causal mechanism. It is also distinct from consciousness: substantial cognitive processing can occur without reportable awareness, while conscious experience can contain contents that do not produce complex cognitive performance. Cognition is consequently a useful umbrella only when the specific component being explained remains explicit.

## 1.9.3 Mental representation

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Mental representation** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
A mental representation is an internal state or structure that stands for, models, or carries information about an object, event, relation, rule, or possibility.

## 3. Core Understanding
Mental representation has a distinct cognitive function that cannot be reduced to the broader chapter domain. Its operation is expressed through the specific process described in its Definition and is shaped by the representation, information, goals, prior knowledge, task demands, and context available to the person. Performance attributed to Mental representation may depend on interacting processes such as attention, perception, memory, language, motivation, or decision-making; therefore the construct should be interpreted by isolating its defining operation rather than treating any observed task performance as a pure measure of it. Empirical conclusions should distinguish the phenomenon from proposed mechanisms and should remain sensitive to measurement and boundary conditions.

A mental representation is not simply a mental picture. It is a functional state or structure that carries information about something other than the state itself, whether that target is an object, event, relation, rule, category, bodily state, or hypothetical possibility. Representations differ in format and granularity: they may preserve perceptual features, encode symbolic relations, summarize regularities, or model causal dependencies. They can also be partial and task-dependent. A representation useful for recognizing an object need not preserve every detail required to draw it, manipulate it, or explain its causal behavior. Representation therefore involves both content and format, and the same information can sometimes be recoded into different forms. The concept is theoretical because internal representations are inferred from patterns of behavior, error, transfer, reaction time, neuropsychological dissociations, and other evidence rather than directly inspected as ordinary objects. Competing theories disagree about whether representations are best characterized as symbolic structures, distributed patterns, analog-like formats, conceptual networks, or combinations of these. A rigorous account should therefore distinguish the existence of representational information from claims about its exact implementation.

Concepts organize knowledge by grouping information around meaning, properties, relations, or functional relevance. A concept can support classification, prediction, explanation, communication, and inference beyond the particular examples from which it was learned. Concepts are not necessarily defined by a single feature: many natural concepts have graded membership, family resemblance, exceptions, and context-sensitive boundaries. They can also operate at different levels of abstraction, from highly specific categories to broad superordinate structures. Theories of concepts include prototype, exemplar, theory-based, and hybrid approaches, each explaining different patterns of typicality, exception handling, and category learning. Concepts should consequently be treated as structured knowledge rather than dictionary-like definitions stored in isolation.

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Categories** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Categories are groupings of entities or events treated as equivalent in some respect for purposes such as prediction, memory, reasoning, or action.

## 3. Core Understanding

Its operation is expressed through the specific process described in its Definition and is shaped by the representation, information, goals, prior knowledge, task demands, and context available to the person. Categories reduce complexity by treating different instances as equivalent for a particular purpose. Equivalence is functional rather than absolute: two objects can belong to the same category for one task and different categories for another. Category boundaries can be based on perceptual similarity, functional properties, causal relations, social conventions, goals, or combinations of these. Categories support prediction because past regularities associated with the group can be generalized to new members, but this generalization can fail when category membership is heterogeneous or context changes. Categories can be hierarchical, overlapping, fuzzy, or dynamically constructed. Their organization also reflects learning history and cultural knowledge, so category structure is not necessarily identical across people or populations. Experimental evidence comes from classification speed, typicality judgments, generalization gradients, learning curves, and transfer to novel cases. Category behavior should not be equated with the existence of a single fixed mental boundary because categorization can be flexible and goal-sensitive. The construct therefore sits between representation and action: it is a way of organizing information that changes what is inferred, remembered, and expected from an instance.

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Prototypes** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
A prototype is a graded or summary representation of characteristic features associated with members of a category.

## 3. Core Understanding
Prototypes has a distinct cognitive function that cannot be reduced to the broader chapter domain. Its operation is expressed through the specific process described in its Definition and is shaped by the representation, information, goals, prior knowledge, task demands, and context available to the person. Prototype accounts propose that category knowledge can include a summary representation of characteristic or central features. A prototype need not correspond to any single encountered object; it can represent a central tendency abstracted across experiences. Prototype similarity can explain graded typicality, rapid classification of familiar central members, and generalization to novel instances that resemble the category center. However, prototype models face difficulty when atypical members have diagnostic features or when people remember individual examples unusually well. Prototypes are therefore better treated as one representational mechanism than as a universal account of categorization. A prototype can be context-sensitive and can change as the distribution of experience changes. It may also coexist with exemplar memory and theory-like knowledge. Evidence must distinguish genuine abstraction from averaging effects produced by repeated exposure or selective attention. The strongest interpretation asks whether behavior is better predicted by similarity to a summary representation, similarity to stored instances, or a structured theory of the category. Prototype theory is thus a model of representation whose usefulness depends on the category and task.

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Exemplars** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Exemplars are representations of specific encountered instances that can contribute to category learning and later classification.

## 3. Core Understanding
Exemplars has a distinct cognitive function that cannot be reduced to the broader chapter domain. Its operation is expressed through the specific process described in its Definition and is shaped by the representation, information, goals, prior knowledge, task demands, and context available to the person. Exemplar theories emphasize memory for particular encountered instances rather than a single abstract category summary. Classification can occur by comparing a new item with stored examples and aggregating their similarity or diagnostic value. This approach naturally explains sensitivity to unusual exemplars, recent experiences, and category members that differ substantially from an average. It also makes category learning partly dependent on episodic memory and attention to individual cases. The model does not require that every exemplar be equally accessible; frequency, distinctiveness, recency, and relevance can change their influence. Exemplar approaches can therefore account for flexible boundaries but may become computationally expensive if many instances must be considered. They also need not imply that people consciously retrieve examples during every categorization decision. Prototype and exemplar representations can coexist, with the relative contribution changing by expertise, task, and learning history. Empirical tests compare predictions about typicality, exceptions, transfer, and category structure rather than assuming that one representational format must explain every case.

Categorization is the process that maps a particular instance onto one or more learned or constructed categories. It requires selecting relevant features, comparing the instance with category knowledge, resolving ambiguity, and applying a criterion appropriate to the task. Categorization can be fast when the instance strongly matches a familiar category and slower when boundaries overlap or evidence is incomplete. Categorization should be distinguished from category representation itself: a category is an organized knowledge structure, whereas categorization is the active process of assigning or organizing an instance.

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Scripts** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Scripts are event-knowledge structures representing typical sequences, roles, actions, and expectations within recurring situations.

## 3. Core Understanding

Scripts are schemas specialized for temporally or causally organized event sequences. A script represents what usually happens, in what approximate order, and which roles or actions are expected in a familiar situation. Scripts reduce cognitive demands because predictable steps do not have to be inferred independently each time. They can also support comprehension when information is incomplete by supplying likely missing actions. Their weakness is that they can insert expected but unobserved events, especially when memory or comprehension is reconstructed from a familiar routine. Scripts are therefore closely related to memory reconstruction but are not identical to episodic memories of particular events. They can be learned from repeated experience, instruction, cultural knowledge, or language. Script flexibility matters because real events often violate canonical sequences, requiring updating of the representation. Measurement can examine recall intrusions, event prediction, order judgments, and transfer to unfamiliar variations. A script is best understood as a structured expectation about event organization, not a literal recording of a routine.

Mental models are internal representations of how a system, situation, or process is organized and how changes in one component may affect another. Unlike simple labels or isolated facts, a mental model supports simulation and inference about relations. People can use models of physical systems, social situations, procedures, causal mechanisms, or hypothetical states. Models may be incomplete, approximate, or internally inconsistent, yet still useful for prediction. Errors often arise when the model omits hidden variables, represents causal direction incorrectly, or is applied outside the conditions under which it was learned. Deductive reasoning derives conclusions that follow necessarily if the premises and inferential rules are correct. Validity concerns the structure of the inference, whereas soundness additionally requires true premises. Human deductive performance is not perfectly captured by formal logic because people construct mental representations of the premises, use working memory, apply relevant knowledge, and may misinterpret quantifiers or relations. Measurement should separately assess formal validity, response accuracy, confidence, and time. Deductive reasoning is consequently a normative relation among propositions expressed through psychological processes that can succeed or fail for identifiable reasons.

## 3. Core Understanding
Abductive reasoning has a distinct cognitive function that cannot be reduced to the broader chapter domain. Abductive reasoning involves inferring the most plausible explanation of observations from incomplete evidence. Unlike deduction, the conclusion need not be guaranteed; unlike simple induction, the central operation is often explanation rather than generalization. Abduction depends heavily on background knowledge because plausibility is determined partly by what mechanisms or causes are already considered possible. Competing explanations may account for the same evidence, and the preferred explanation can change when new evidence alters relative likelihoods. People can therefore be correct through abductive reasoning without explicitly calculating probabilities, but they can also overprefer coherent stories that lack adequate evidential support. Measurement should examine whether explanations predict additional observations and whether alternatives were considered. Abduction is especially important in diagnosis, scientific hypothesis generation, and everyday interpretation, but it should not be confused with proof: explanatory coherence is not equivalent to truth.
