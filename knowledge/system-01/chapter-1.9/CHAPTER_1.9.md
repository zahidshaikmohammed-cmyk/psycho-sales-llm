## 1.9.1 Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Thinking** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Thinking is the coordinated psychological activity of manipulating, evaluating, transforming, and using information in ways that support representation, reasoning, problem solving, planning, and goal-directed action.

## 3. Core Understanding

A further distinction is between the content being manipulated and the operation performed on it. The same factual knowledge can support recall, inference, simulation, or planning depending on the current operation. Thinking also unfolds over time: a state can be maintained briefly, transformed through intermediate steps, or reorganized after an impasse. Errors therefore can arise from representation, operation selection, control, or evaluation. This process-level decomposition prevents the chapter from treating every successful answer as evidence for one general thinking ability.

Measurement should distinguish process from product: response quality alone cannot reveal whether performance came from retrieval, transformation, simulation, or external support. Individual differences may reflect knowledge and strategy as much as general capacity. Thinking also changes with development and expertise, so the same task can measure different operations.

## 1.9.2 Cognition

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Cognition** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Cognition is the broad family of mental processes involved in acquiring, representing, transforming, storing, retrieving, and using information.

## 3. Core Understanding

Because cognition is an umbrella, its scientific usefulness depends on specifying the narrower process under investigation. Cognitive performance is distributed across levels from perceptual computation to abstract reasoning and can be influenced by bodily state and environmental structure. External tools can sometimes change performance by changing the representational burden without changing the person's underlying knowledge. Thus cognitive ability should be inferred cautiously from isolated task outcomes.

Because cognition spans multiple processes, causal claims require decomposition. Manipulating attention can change memory performance without proving attention is the sole cognitive mechanism; changing external representations can alter reasoning without changing internal knowledge. Cognition is therefore best studied through converging tasks that isolate components and test transfer across contexts.

## 1.9.3 Mental representation

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Mental representation** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
A mental representation is an internal state or structure that stands for, models, or carries information about an object, event, relation, rule, or possibility.

## 3. Core Understanding

Representations can be explicit or implicit, stable or transient, modality-specific or amodal, and highly detailed or compressed. They can preserve similarity, symbolic structure, spatial relations, temporal order, or causal dependencies. A representation is useful only relative to an operation: compression may help categorization while harming exact reconstruction. This makes representational adequacy a central explanatory question in cognition.

Evidence for representation is inferential and should be triangulated. Behavioral transfer can show that information is preserved in a usable form, error patterns can reveal representational structure, and neuropsychological dissociations can constrain competing models. None of these alone identifies a unique format, so implementation claims require stronger evidence than functional claims.

Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Concepts** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Concepts are mental structures that organize knowledge about entities, properties, events, or relations and support classification, inference, prediction, and communication.

## 3. Core Understanding

Concepts also have inferential consequences. Knowing that an instance belongs to a concept can activate expectations about properties that were not directly observed. Such inference can be useful when category regularities are reliable and misleading when exceptions are common. Concepts can be learned through direct experience, instruction, language, analogy, and social transmission, and their structure can change when evidence repeatedly violates prior expectations.

Concept theories are evaluated by their ability to explain typicality, exceptions, category learning, generalization, and context effects with plausible mechanisms. A concept can be represented through multiple interacting formats, so disagreement between prototype and exemplar predictions does not imply that one kind of knowledge must be absent. Measurement should test novel instances and relational transfer.

## 2. Definition

## 3. Core Understanding

Category boundaries are often probabilistic rather than absolute. An instance can provide evidence for several categories simultaneously, and categorization can depend on the consequence of choosing one category over another. This means category structure and categorization criteria should be separated. A category may remain stable while the decision criterion changes with task goals or context.

Category knowledge can be stable while category use is flexible. A person may change the decision criterion without changing what is known about the category. This distinction is especially important when categories have unequal costs or consequences. Category research therefore separates representational structure from decision policy.

## 1.9.6 Prototypes

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Prototypes** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
A prototype is a graded or summary representation of characteristic features associated with members of a category.

## 3. Core Understanding

Prototype representations are especially informative when category members vary continuously around a central tendency. Their limitations become apparent when rare or atypical members carry diagnostic information that an average representation would obscure. Prototype similarity can therefore be one cue among several rather than the sole determinant of membership. Expertise may change prototypes by weighting features differently as diagnostic knowledge accumulates.

Prototype effects are strongest when central tendency is informative, but diagnostic exceptions reveal where summary representations are insufficient. Prototype formation also depends on the distribution of experience, so changing exposure can change the apparent category center. Evidence should compare predictions from central tendency, individual instances, and structured knowledge.

Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Exemplars** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition

## 3. Core Understanding

Exemplar representations preserve variability that summary representations can lose. They can explain why a learner remains sensitive to unusual members and why recent or highly distinctive examples sometimes exert disproportionate influence. At the same time, stored instances need not be consciously retrieved one at a time; similarity-based influence can be distributed across many memories. Exemplar and prototype accounts are therefore competing explanatory emphases, not necessarily mutually exclusive psychological experiences.

Exemplar representations preserve variability that summary representations can lose. They can explain why atypical members sometimes receive accurate treatment despite low similarity to a prototype. Their main theoretical challenge is explaining abstraction and efficient generalization without assuming exhaustive comparison with every stored instance. Hybrid accounts remain possible.

## 1.9.8 Categorization

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Categorization** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Categorization is the process of assigning or organizing instances into categories using available information, goals, and learned knowledge.

## 3. Core Understanding

Categorization can be rule-based, similarity-based, theory-based, or hybrid. Early learning may depend on explicit rules while later performance becomes more automatic, or a learner may shift strategies when category structure changes. Category decisions can also be probabilistic when evidence is ambiguous. A complete analysis therefore specifies the information used, the criterion applied, and how that criterion was acquired.

Categorization errors can be diagnostic of the representation being used. Confusions among similar items suggest one type of feature weighting, while systematic sensitivity to causal properties suggests another. Learning studies can therefore infer category structure from the pattern of generalization rather than from labels alone.

Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Concept formation** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition

## 3. Core Understanding

Concept formation can involve discovering dimensions that were not explicitly provided by the environment. Learners must determine which regularities are stable enough to generalize and which are accidental features of the training examples. Feedback can accelerate this discovery, but feedback can also teach superficial rules if it is poorly aligned with the underlying structure. Transfer to new cases is therefore the strongest test of genuine concept acquisition.

Concept formation should be evaluated after the learner encounters cases that were not explicitly taught. Success on familiar training examples can reflect memorization or rule following without abstraction. Generalization, exception handling, and explanation provide stronger evidence that a concept has been formed.

## 1.9.10 Schemas

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Schemas** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Schemas are organized knowledge structures that represent expected relations among concepts, events, roles, or situations.

## 3. Core Understanding

Schemas can guide both assimilation and error. When incoming information fits expectations, processing can become efficient; when it conflicts, people may either revise the schema or reinterpret the information to preserve it. Which response occurs depends on evidence strength, flexibility, goals, and prior knowledge. Schema-based processing therefore supports efficient cognition while creating predictable pathways for omission and intrusion errors.

Schema research must distinguish facilitation from intrusion. A schema can speed interpretation when expectations are valid while inserting plausible but absent information when memory is incomplete. The same structure can therefore produce both accuracy and error depending on environmental regularity. Schema updating is central when violations accumulate.

## 1.9.11 Scripts

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Scripts** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Scripts are event-knowledge structures representing typical sequences, roles, actions, and expectations within recurring situations.

## 3. Core Understanding

Scripts are culturally and individually learned, so the expected sequence for an event is not universally identical. A script can include optional as well as obligatory elements, and context can activate different variants. Script knowledge can facilitate prediction and comprehension but can also cause people to remember expected actions that never occurred. This makes scripts especially relevant to reconstructive memory and event understanding.

Script knowledge is especially sensitive to cultural and contextual variation because event sequences are socially learned. Scripts can contain optional branches and role-specific expectations rather than one rigid order. Research should therefore avoid treating one group's routine as a universal script and should examine how variants are selected by context.

## 1.9.15 Symbolic thought

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Symbolic thought** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Symbolic thought uses symbols or representational tokens to stand for objects, concepts, relations, rules, or possibilities.

## 3. Core Understanding

Symbol systems are powerful because they permit compositional operations: relations among symbols can encode relations among referents. This allows recursive reasoning and abstraction but also creates vulnerability to superficial symbol manipulation. Expertise in a symbolic system can make operations highly efficient, while unfamiliar notation can impose substantial working-memory demands. Symbolic thought is therefore shaped by both representational structure and learned fluency.

Symbolic thought also depends on conventions and compositional rules. The same symbol can mean different things across systems, so successful manipulation requires knowing the representational scheme. Symbolic errors can arise from syntax, semantics, or failure to map symbols onto real-world relations.

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Abstract thinking** within the representational architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Abstract thinking focuses on general properties, relations, principles, or hypothetical possibilities beyond a single concrete instance.

## 3. Core Understanding

Abstraction involves selective preservation. The solver must retain the relations that remain relevant across cases while suppressing details that vary. Too little abstraction prevents transfer; too much can remove causal or diagnostic information. Effective abstraction therefore depends on identifying invariants relative to a problem class, not simply ignoring concrete detail.

Abstract reasoning can be tested by changing surface content while preserving relational structure. Successful transfer across different examples is stronger evidence for abstraction than success on one familiar formulation. Abstraction is therefore demonstrated through invariant structure, not merely difficult vocabulary.

## 1.9.66 Knowledge acquisition

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Knowledge acquisition** within the executive-control architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Knowledge acquisition is the process through which information becomes sufficiently represented, integrated, and accessible to support later understanding or use.

## 3. Core Understanding

Knowledge acquisition is cumulative because new structures change what later information can connect to. Prior knowledge can accelerate learning through meaningful integration but can also produce misconceptions that interfere with new material. Acquisition should therefore be evaluated not only by retention but by whether the learner can explain, discriminate, generalize, and apply the knowledge. Durable knowledge is an organized capability, not merely a stored list of facts.

Knowledge acquisition is demonstrated when learning survives delay and transfers to novel conditions. Immediate performance can be inflated by cues, imitation, or temporary activation. Durable acquisition therefore requires evidence that the learner can retrieve, explain, discriminate, and use the knowledge without the original supports.

This Topic occupies the executive-control layer and distinguishes regulation of cognition from the specific cognitive contents, memories, or social judgments being regulated. ## 1.9.67 Expertise and thinking

## 1. Purpose
Establish the meaning, function, and boundaries of **Expertise and thinking** within the executive-control architecture of Chapter 1.9.

## 2. Definition
Expertise and thinking concerns how extensive, organized, domain-specific knowledge and experience alter perception, representation, reasoning, problem solving, and strategic control.

## 3. Core Understanding

Expertise changes the representational starting point of a problem. Experts often perceive meaningful units where novices perceive disconnected details, allowing faster search and more accurate prediction. But expertise can also produce overgeneralization when familiar patterns are applied outside their valid range. Adaptive expertise therefore requires both efficient routine performance and the ability to recognize when a situation is novel enough to demand restructuring.

Expertise should be assessed across both routine and novel conditions. Experts often outperform novices on familiar structures but can lose their advantage when assumptions change. Adaptive expertise is therefore characterized by efficient performance plus the ability to recognize exceptions, revise representations, and transfer principles beyond routine cases.
