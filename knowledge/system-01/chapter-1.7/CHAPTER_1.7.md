## 1.7.1 Learning

## 1. Purpose
Explain the psychological process or learning principle represented by “Learning” and distinguish it from its nearest neighboring learning constructs.

## 2. Definition
relatively enduring change in knowledge, behavior, skill, or behavioral potential produced by experience.

## 3. Core Understanding
Learning must be distinguished from temporary performance changes caused by fatigue, motivation, arousal, maturation, or immediate state. It may involve associations, knowledge, strategies, skills, or behavioral tendencies.
Learning includes conditioning, observation, cognitive acquisition, habituation, sensitization, skill practice, and habit formation. No single mechanism explains every form.
What is learned depends on prior experience, biological preparedness, task structure, consequences, context, and opportunity to express the acquired capability.
Learning is best identified by change attributable to experience rather than by a single successful performance. Persistence, transfer, context sensitivity, and expression conditions help separate acquired capacity from transient motivation or state effects.

A useful scientific criterion is that learning changes the conditions under which later behavior or cognition can occur, not merely the behavior observed at one moment. Acquisition can be inferred through persistence, transfer, altered prediction, improved efficiency, or changed response probability, but expression depends on motivation, retrieval, context, and opportunity. Learning can be explicit or implicit, deliberate or incidental, and can involve associations, representations, procedures, or action policies. Different mechanisms have different time courses and signatures: conditioning can alter prediction, practice can refine coordination, observation can provide information without direct reinforcement, and habituation can reduce responding to repeated stimulation. Because performance is an expression of learning rather than its definition, careful analysis separates what was acquired from whether the person currently expresses it.

## 1.7.2 Learning from experience

## 1. Purpose
Explain the psychological process or learning principle represented by “Learning from experience” and distinguish it from its nearest neighboring learning constructs.

## 2. Definition
cquisition or modification of knowledge, associations, skills, expectations, or behavioral tendencies as a consequence of prior interaction with the environment.

## 3. Core Understanding
Experience can provide pairings, consequences, demonstrations, errors, feedback, and practice. The same exposure can produce different learning depending on prior knowledge, attention, motivation, and biological constraints.
Organisms learn from relationships, prediction errors, consequences, and absences, not simply from repetition. Context determines what information is available for later use.
Learned effects may generalize, remain context-bound, or appear only when motivation and opportunity permit.
Experience can change predictions, representations, strategies, skills, and response tendencies. What is learned depends on what the learner can discriminate, what information is predictive, and what prior knowledge makes salient.

Experience does not provide information in a psychologically neutral form. What becomes learned depends on what is noticed, discriminated, predicted, remembered, and consequential. Exposure can establish familiarity without producing the same kind of learning as feedback or consequence-based change. Prior knowledge changes the hypotheses a learner brings to new events, so identical experiences can produce different acquired structures. Experience can also produce competing learning: a person may acquire a new response while an older response remains retrievable under another context. Strong evidence for experience-dependent change therefore uses comparison over time or across controlled conditions and considers alternative explanations such as maturation, regression to the mean, changing incentives, or repeated testing.

## 1.7.3 Behavioral change through learning

## 1. Purpose
Explain the psychological process or learning principle represented by “Behavioral change through learning” and distinguish it from its nearest neighboring learning constructs.

## 2. Definition
relatively enduring alteration in the probability, form, timing, or effectiveness of behavior resulting from experience.

## 3. Core Understanding
Learning can increase, suppress, redirect, or refine behavior. Classical conditioning changes responses to predictive stimuli; operant learning changes behavior through consequences.
A temporary behavioral change does not establish learning. Stable change across relevant conditions provides stronger evidence.
Behavior learned under one contingency can weaken when cues, consequences, or contexts change. Maintenance therefore depends on continuing learning conditions and competing learning.
Behavioral change is evidence for learning only when plausible alternatives such as maturation, fatigue, arousal, opportunity, or immediate incentives are controlled. Learned behavior can be suppressed or expressed differently without the underlying acquisition being erased.

Behavioral change is one observable expression of learning, not a complete definition of it. A learned behavior may become faster, more accurate, more persistent, more context-sensitive, or more likely under a particular cue. Conversely, an acquired capability can fail to appear when the relevant motivation, retrieval conditions, reinforcement history, or physical opportunity is absent. This distinction matters in both laboratory and natural settings because immediate performance can change without acquisition and acquisition can occur without immediate performance. Behavioral maintenance also depends on what happens after acquisition: continued consequences can strengthen patterns, while changed contingencies can produce extinction, competition, or new learning. Thus durable behavior change is best interpreted together with the history and conditions that produced it.

## 1.7.4 Associative learning

## 1. Purpose
Explain the psychological process or learning principle represented by “Associative learning” and distinguish it from its nearest neighboring learning constructs.

## 2. Definition
earning in which experience establishes or modifies relationships among stimuli, responses, actions, and their consequences.

## 3. Core Understanding
Classical conditioning commonly establishes stimulus-outcome relations; operant conditioning establishes behavior-consequence relations. Association is therefore broader than either paradigm but narrower than learning as a whole.
Associative learning is sensitive to informativeness and prediction error rather than mere pairing frequency.
Blocking, overshadowing, latent inhibition, and related effects show that prior experience changes what a new cue can acquire.
Associative learning concerns acquired relations among events, actions, and outcomes. Predictiveness, timing, salience, prior learning, and competition determine what relationship is acquired; repetition alone is not a sufficient mechanism.

Associative learning is especially informative because it shows that organisms learn relations, not merely isolated events. Learning can involve stimulus–outcome, response–outcome, or cue–cue relations, and these relations are sensitive to timing, contingency, predictiveness, attention, and prior experience. Competition effects such as blocking and overshadowing demonstrate that adding a second cue does not guarantee additional learning when the first cue already explains the outcome. Association is also not equivalent to conscious belief: learned relations can guide responding without being verbally articulated. At the same time, associative models do not exhaust learning; people can acquire rules, strategies, skills, and abstract structures that are not adequately described as simple pairings.

## 1.7.7 Classical conditioning

## 2. Definition

Classical conditioning is fundamentally about acquired predictive relations. Spontaneous recovery is the return of a previously reduced conditioned response after a delay following extinction.
