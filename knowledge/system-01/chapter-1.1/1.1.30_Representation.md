# 1.1.30 — Representation

## 1. Purpose

Representation explains how psychological systems can operate on information that is not physically present in the immediate environment. It is foundational to memory, imagination, reasoning, language, planning, and perception.

## 2. Definition

A psychological representation is an internal form in which information about an object, event, relation, state, possibility, or concept is organized so that psychological processes can use it.

## 3. Core Understanding

Representations can concern present, past, future, hypothetical, or abstract information. A person can remember a place, imagine a future action, represent a rule, or categorize an object even when the object is not currently present.

Representations are not exact copies of reality. They are selective and structured. What is represented depends on perception, learning, language, goals, attention, and prior knowledge.

Different forms of representation include perceptual images, concepts, propositions, spatial structures, motor programs, schemas, and mental models. Psychology should not assume that one universal representational format explains all cognition.

Representations can be updated, combined, distorted, retrieved, and transformed. Their usefulness depends partly on how well they support the task and correspond to relevant features of the world.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

Representation connects interpretation, mental contents, processing, memory, thinking, language, decision-making, and action.

## 6. Key Principles

1. Psychological systems represent information for later processing.
2. Representations can concern absent, past, future, or hypothetical events.
3. Representations are selective rather than exact copies of reality.
4. Different tasks can require different representational forms.
5. Representations can be updated and transformed.
