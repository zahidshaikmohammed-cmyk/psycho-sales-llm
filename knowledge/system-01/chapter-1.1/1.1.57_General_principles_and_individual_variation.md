# 1.1.57 — General Principles and Individual Variation

## 1. Purpose
Psychology needs both general principles and recognition of variation. This Topic establishes how universal-looking findings should be interpreted without assuming identical functioning in every person.

## 2. Definition
General principles are patterns or relationships that hold across defined populations or conditions; individual variation refers to meaningful differences in how those principles are expressed among people, situations, or contexts.

## 3. Core Understanding

Psychology must explain both regularity and variation. A general principle can describe an average tendency or reliable relationship while individuals still differ in magnitude, timing, strategy, or response.

Variation can arise from traits, current states, learning histories, biological differences, social conditions, culture, measurement error, and interactions among these factors. The presence of variation does not mean that a general effect is false.

Scientific generalization depends on population and context. A finding from one sample may not apply equally to another population, culture, age group, or environmental condition. This is why replication and generalizability matter.

Individual variation should also not be confused with fixed categories. Many psychological characteristics vary continuously, and meaningful differences can exist within as well as between groups.

The goal is not to choose between universal laws and individual uniqueness. It is to identify which patterns are reliable, which conditions modify them, and how much variation remains after those conditions are considered.

## 6. Key Principles
1. General psychological principles are usually conditional, not universal in every circumstance.
2. Individual variation can coexist with population-level regularities.
3. Generalization requires attention to population and context.
4. Group averages should not be treated as individual predictions.
5. Good psychology explains both regularity and variation.
