# 1.1.41 — Levels of Analysis

## 1. Purpose
Levels of analysis provide a disciplined way to identify what unit is being studied, such as an individual, group, interaction, neural system, or population.

## 2. Definition
A level of analysis is the scale or unit at which a psychological phenomenon is described or investigated.

## 3. Core Understanding

A level of analysis identifies the unit being examined. In psychology, the unit might be a person, a dyad, a group, a population, a neural system, or a broader social or cultural system. The appropriate level depends on the question.

A finding at one level cannot automatically be transferred to another. A neural correlation does not by itself explain a social interaction, and a group average does not describe every individual. This is one reason multilevel reasoning must preserve the meaning of each level.

Levels can be nested. Individual behavior occurs within relationships, relationships within groups, and groups within cultural and institutional environments. Psychological systems also contain smaller processes and components.

The concept is especially important for avoiding ecological and reductionist errors. An aggregate pattern can differ from the pattern observed within individuals, while a component-level finding can fail to capture system-level organization.


## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Levels of analysis connect person, systems, biological, psychological, social, and cultural explanations.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
- Unit and scale of analysis as an architectural distinction.
### Defers

## 6. Key Principles
1. A level of analysis specifies what unit is being studied.
2. Different levels answer different questions.
3. Findings at one level do not automatically establish findings at another.
4. Multilevel explanation can combine levels when justified.
