# 1.1.14 — Individual Differences

## 1. Purpose

Psychology seeks general principles, but people do not respond identically. Individual differences provide the foundation for explaining meaningful variation among people without abandoning general psychological science.

## 2. Definition

Individual differences are relatively systematic differences among people in psychological characteristics, capacities, states, behaviors, or patterns of functioning. They can arise from biological variation, development, learning, experience, personality, context, culture, and interactions among these factors.

## 3. Core Understanding

Individual differences can be stable or changeable. Traits and abilities often show meaningful stability, while states and skills can change more rapidly. Stability is therefore a matter of degree, not an absolute property.

Differences can be quantitative, such as degree of a trait, or qualitative, such as different strategies or patterns of responding. A scientifically useful difference requires reliable measurement and should not be inferred from a single observation.

Individual differences can moderate psychological processes. The same environmental condition may affect people differently because they differ in prior experience, sensitivity, goals, resources, or biological characteristics. Conversely, different environments can produce similar outcomes in people with different characteristics.

Individual differences should not be used to create rigid categories when continuous variation is more accurate. Nor should group averages be treated as descriptions of every member of a group.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

Individual differences connect person, traits, capacities, states, context, development, and behavior. They are central to the person–situation architecture.

## 6. Key Principles

1. People differ in meaningful psychological characteristics.
2. Individual differences can be stable, flexible, or both depending on the construct.
3. Differences can alter how people respond to the same conditions.
4. Group averages do not describe every individual.
5. Individual variation does not invalidate general psychological principles.
