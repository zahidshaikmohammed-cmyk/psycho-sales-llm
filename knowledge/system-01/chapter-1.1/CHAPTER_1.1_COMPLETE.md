## 1.1.1 Psychology

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic establishes what psychology studies and gives the chapter its broadest foundation.

### 2. DEFINITION
Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes.

**What It Is Not:** Psychology is not limited to mental illness, personality, or conscious thought. It also studies healthy functioning, learning, emotion, perception, motivation, social behavior, and many other processes.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Psychology studies observable behavior and internal processes that help explain behavior. These processes include perception, attention, memory, thinking, emotion, motivation, and action.

Psychological explanations can operate at biological, psychological, social, and cultural levels. No single level explains every behavior completely.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Psychology is the parent domain for every concept in this chapter. The chapter then breaks psychology into more precise constructs, processes, mechanisms, states, and explanatory levels.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general scientific domain of psychology.  
**Defers:** Specific psychological constructs and methods to their canonical chapters.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Psychology studies behavior and mental processes scientifically.
- Human behavior usually requires explanation at more than one level.

## 1.1.2 Behavior

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic defines the observable side of human functioning.

### 2. DEFINITION
Behavior is an observable or measurable activity of an organism, including actions, responses, and patterns of activity.

**What It Is Not:** Behavior is not only deliberate action. It can include automatic, learned, emotional, social, and physiological responses.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Behavior ranges from simple responses to complex activities such as speaking, choosing, avoiding, helping, working, and interacting with other people.

Behavior is shaped by internal processes and environmental conditions. The same person may behave differently when goals, incentives, social conditions, physical states, or surroundings change.

Behavior can also change the environment, which then changes later behavior. This creates ongoing person–environment transactions.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Behavior is one of the two broad objects of psychology, alongside mental processes. It provides the observable output through which many psychological mechanisms are studied.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General behavior as a psychological object.  
**Defers:** Specific action-control mechanisms to 1.21 and learning mechanisms to 1.7.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Behavior is observable or measurable activity.
- Behavior reflects interaction between the person and the environment.
- Behavior itself does not reveal its psychological cause.

## 1.1.3 Mental Processes

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic defines the internal processes through which people perceive, represent, evaluate, feel, decide, and regulate activity.

### 2. DEFINITION
Mental processes are internal operations involved in receiving, representing, interpreting, storing, evaluating, or regulating information and experience.

**What It Is Not:** A mental process is not the same as a conscious thought. Many mental processes occur without conscious awareness.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Mental processes include attention, perception, memory, thinking, language, emotion, motivation, and decision-making. They can operate automatically or with deliberate control.

Mental processes interact. Attention can affect memory; emotion can affect judgment; motivation can affect attention and action. These are relationships, not separate owners.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Mental processes are a core category of psychological functioning. Later chapters provide the canonical explanations for particular processes.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general category of mental processing.  
**Defers:** Specific processes to their canonical chapters.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Mental processing can be conscious or nonconscious.
- Mental processes interact rather than operate as isolated modules.

## 1.1.4 Mental States

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic distinguishes relatively temporary conditions of the mind from ongoing processes and stable individual differences.

### 2. DEFINITION
A mental state is a condition of a person at a particular time that can influence experience, processing, or behavior.

**What It Is Not:** A mental state is not necessarily a permanent trait or a specific mental process.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Examples include being alert, anxious, motivated, fatigued, focused, confused, or emotionally activated. States can change with time and circumstances.

A state may influence how a person processes the same situation. The same stimulus can therefore produce different responses under different internal states.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
The state concept is needed to distinguish temporary conditions from traits, processes, and capacities.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general state category.  
**Defers:** Specific states to their canonical domains.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- States are time-sensitive conditions.
- States can change how other psychological processes operate.

## 1.1.5 Mental Contents

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic identifies what mental activity can contain or represent.

### 2. DEFINITION
Mental contents are the information, representations, thoughts, images, beliefs, memories, feelings, or other experiences present in mental activity.

**What It Is Not:** Mental content is not the same as the process that produces or operates on it.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
A belief is a content; believing is a process. A memory can refer to stored or retrieved information; remembering is an activity involving retrieval.

The distinction matters because the same content can be processed differently, and the same process can operate on different contents.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
This distinction prevents the chapter from treating every mental event as the same kind of object.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general distinction between mental contents and processes.  
**Defers:** Specific representations, beliefs, memories, and emotions to their owners.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Contents are what mental activity represents or contains.
- Processes are operations performed on or with contents.

## 1.1.6 Psychological Functions

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic introduces psychology as a study of functions that support human activity.

### 2. DEFINITION
A psychological function is a capacity or operation that contributes to how a person detects information, maintains internal organization, regulates activity, or responds to the environment.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Functions include selecting information, remembering, evaluating, regulating emotion, pursuing goals, communicating, and coordinating action.

A function describes what a psychological system does. It does not by itself specify the biological structure or exact mechanism responsible.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Functions provide a bridge between broad psychological description and more precise mechanisms.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general functional view of psychological activity.  
**Defers:** Specific functions to their canonical domains.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Function describes what a psychological system does.
- Function does not by itself identify the mechanism producing it.

## 1.1.7 Psychological Mechanisms

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic defines the idea of mechanism, which is central to deep psychological explanation.

### 2. DEFINITION
A psychological mechanism is a process or organized set of processes through which a psychological condition, input, or event produces or changes another psychological outcome.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
A mechanism explains how an outcome comes about rather than merely stating that two things are related.

Mechanisms can involve multiple levels. For example, a behavioral change may involve learning, changes in expectation, motivation, attention, and action selection.

Mechanistic explanations require evidence. A plausible story is not automatically an established mechanism.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Mechanisms are the basic explanatory units used throughout System 1.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general concept of psychological mechanism.  
**Defers:** Specific mechanisms to their canonical owners.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- A mechanism explains how change occurs.
- Mechanistic claims require evidence.
- Correlation alone does not establish a mechanism.

## 1.1.8 Psychological Systems

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains why psychological functioning is often better understood as interacting systems than as isolated parts.

### 2. DEFINITION
A psychological system is an organized set of interacting processes, structures, states, or functions that together contribute to psychological activity.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Systems have parts and relationships. Their behavior can depend on interactions among those parts and with the environment.

A system can show properties that are not obvious from one component alone. This does not mean the whole is mysterious; it means interactions matter.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
The systems concept supports the chapter's multilevel and interaction-based view of human psychology.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General systems thinking in psychology.  
**Defers:** Specific psychological systems to their domain chapters.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Psychological systems contain interacting elements.
- System behavior depends partly on relationships among elements.

## 1.1.9 Psychological Constructs

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains how psychology gives stable names to important but often indirectly observed phenomena.

### 2. DEFINITION
A psychological construct is an abstract concept used to represent a psychological property or phenomenon that may not be directly observable.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Examples include intelligence, anxiety, motivation, self-esteem, and working memory. A construct is studied through observable indicators and measurements.

Constructs are useful because they organize evidence across observations. They must still be defined carefully and measured validly.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Constructs are the basic named objects of the System 1 ontology.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general concept of psychological constructs.  
**Defers:** Specific constructs to their canonical owners; measurement details to 1.2.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Constructs are abstract scientific concepts.
- A construct is not identical to any single measurement.

## 1.1.10 Psychological Traits

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic introduces relatively stable patterns of individual difference.

### 2. DEFINITION
A psychological trait is a relatively enduring characteristic associated with consistent patterns of thinking, feeling, or behavior across situations and time.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Traits describe tendencies, not fixed behavior. A person with a high level of a trait may still behave differently across contexts.

Trait stability can coexist with change. Traits can also interact with situations, meaning behavior cannot always be predicted from traits alone.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Traits are one important form of individual difference and are distinguished from temporary states and momentary processes.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general trait concept.  
**Defers:** Personality traits and models to 1.14.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Traits are tendencies, not guarantees.
- Traits and situations jointly matter for behavior.

---

## 1.1.11 Psychological Capacities

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic defines capacities as abilities that enable psychological functioning.

### 2. DEFINITION
A psychological capacity is a person's ability or potential to perform a psychological function under suitable conditions.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Capacities can differ between people and can be affected by development, learning, health, context, and temporary states.

Capacity is not the same as current performance. A person may have a capacity but fail to express it because of fatigue, distraction, stress, lack of opportunity, or other conditions.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Capacity helps distinguish what a person can potentially do from what the person does at a particular moment.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general capacity concept.  
**Defers:** Specific capacities such as intelligence or working memory to their canonical chapters.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Capacity and performance are not identical.
- Context can affect the expression of capacity.

## 1.1.12 Psychological Organization

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains how psychological elements can be arranged into coordinated patterns rather than existing as unrelated pieces.

### 2. DEFINITION
Psychological organization is the structured arrangement and coordination of psychological processes, states, representations, capacities, and tendencies.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
People do not use isolated mechanisms independently. Attention, memory, emotion, motivation, beliefs, and action can form coordinated patterns.

Organization can be stable enough to support continuity while remaining flexible enough to adapt.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Organization provides the structural idea connecting individual psychological components into functioning persons.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General organization of psychological functioning.  
**Defers:** Specific organizational structures to their canonical domains.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Psychological parts operate within organized systems.
- Organization can be stable and adaptive at the same time.

## 1.1.13 Human Functioning

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic provides the broad outcome-level concept for understanding how psychological systems operate in real life.

### 2. DEFINITION
Human functioning is the way a person thinks, feels, regulates, relates, adapts, and acts across real-life conditions.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Functioning reflects the interaction of capacities, states, processes, traits, relationships, environments, and demands.

Good functioning does not mean the absence of every negative state. A person can experience stress, sadness, uncertainty, or failure and still function effectively.

Functioning is therefore contextual and multidimensional.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Human functioning integrates the chapter's foundational architecture without replacing the more specific constructs that explain its parts.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The integrative concept of functioning.  
**Defers:** Specific domains of functioning to their canonical chapters.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Human functioning is multidimensional.
- Functioning depends on person and context.
- No single psychological construct fully defines functioning.

## 1.1.14 Individual Differences

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains why people can respond differently to similar situations.

### 2. DEFINITION
Individual differences are relatively systematic differences between people in psychological characteristics, capacities, states, or patterns of behavior.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Differences can arise from biological variation, development, learning, personality, experience, context, culture, and their interactions.

Individual differences are distributions, not simple categories. Two people can differ in degree without belonging to completely different psychological types.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Individual differences prevent universal psychological principles from being mistaken for identical behavior in every person.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General individual variation.  
**Defers:** Personality differences to 1.14 and domain-specific differences to their owners.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- People are similar enough for general principles to exist.
- People differ enough that averages do not describe every individual.

## 1.1.15 Person

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic establishes the person as the integrated unit in which psychological processes operate.

### 2. DEFINITION
A person is an individual human organism considered as an integrated psychological, biological, and social system.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
A person has biological characteristics, psychological processes, a history of learning and development, relationships, goals, and a social and cultural context.

No single component fully explains the person.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
The person is the central unit connecting internal processes with environments and social systems.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general person-level unit of psychological analysis.  
**Defers:** Biological, developmental, personality, and social properties to their owners.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- A person is an integrated system.
- Person-level explanations can be connected to lower and higher levels of analysis.

## 1.1.16 Situation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic defines the immediate circumstances in which psychological activity occurs.

### 2. DEFINITION
A situation is the set of immediate circumstances, events, cues, demands, and opportunities surrounding a person at a particular time.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Situations can change what information is available, what actions are possible, what goals are active, and what responses are likely.

The same person can behave differently across situations without the person having fundamentally changed.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Situation is a central part of person–situation interaction.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general psychological concept of immediate situation.  
**Defers:** Social and cultural structures to 1.16 and 1.17.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Situations can change behavior without changing the person's basic traits.
- Situation and person must often be analyzed together.

---

## 1.1.17 Context

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic distinguishes broader conditions from the immediate situation.

### 2. DEFINITION
Context is the broader set of social, cultural, historical, physical, institutional, and environmental conditions in which psychological activity occurs.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Context can shape what a situation means and what responses are available. Context may include social norms, culture, resources, roles, institutions, and previous events.

Context does not determine behavior mechanically. People interpret and respond to context differently.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Context expands the analysis beyond immediate stimuli to the larger environment surrounding psychological functioning.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General context as a level of psychological explanation.  
**Defers:** Specific cultural and social mechanisms to 1.16 and 1.17.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Context gives situations broader meaning and constraints.
- Context influences without mechanically determining behavior.

---

## 1.1.18 Person–Situation Interaction

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains why behavior often depends on both the person and the situation.

### 2. DEFINITION
Person–situation interaction is the dependence of psychological responses or behavior on the combination of individual characteristics and situational conditions.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
A trait may become more visible in one situation than another. A situation may affect two people differently because they interpret or respond to it differently.

The interaction can be statistical, psychological, or both. It should not be assumed merely because two variables are present.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
This is a core foundation for avoiding person-only and situation-only explanations.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General person–situation interaction.  
**Defers:** Specific trait activation and personality mechanisms to 1.14.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Behavior depends partly on person and situation together.
- The same situation need not produce the same response in everyone.

---

## 1.1.19 Person–Environment Interaction

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic extends person–situation analysis to broader environments.

### 2. DEFINITION
Person–environment interaction is the ongoing relationship in which personal characteristics and environmental conditions influence one another.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
People respond to environments, but they also choose, change, avoid, create, and reshape environments.

Development, learning, relationships, work, and health can therefore involve two-way influence.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
It establishes the environment as an active part of psychological functioning rather than a passive background.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General person–environment interaction.  
**Defers:** Specific environmental mechanisms to developmental, social, cultural, and health chapters.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Person and environment influence one another.
- Environmental effects depend partly on how people perceive and use environments.

---

## 1.1.20 Behavior–Environment Transaction

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic defines the reciprocal process through which behavior changes the environment that later shapes behavior.

### 2. DEFINITION
A behavior–environment transaction occurs when a person's behavior changes environmental conditions, and those changed conditions subsequently influence the person's behavior.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
A person may avoid a situation, which reduces immediate discomfort but changes future opportunities. A person may also seek social contact, creating interactions that affect later behavior.

Transactions unfold over time. They therefore differ from a one-way environmental influence.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
This concept adds temporal feedback to the person–environment relationship.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General transactional behavior–environment dynamics.  
**Defers:** Specific learning and social mechanisms to their owners.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Behavior can change its own future conditions.
- Psychological development and functioning can involve feedback loops.

## 1.1.21 Psychological Adaptation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains how psychological systems adjust to changing demands.

### 2. DEFINITION
Psychological adaptation is the process by which psychological functioning changes or organizes itself in response to demands, conditions, or environmental challenges.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Adaptation can involve learning, changes in expectations, emotional adjustment, behavior change, or changes in goals.

Adaptation is not always beneficial. A response may help in one environment but create costs in another.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Adaptation is a foundational principle connecting stability, change, flexibility, and person–environment interaction.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General psychological adaptation.  
**Defers:** Specific coping and resilience processes to 1.18 and learning processes to 1.7.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Adaptation involves adjustment to conditions.
- An adaptive response is context-dependent.

---

## 1.1.22 Psychological Plasticity

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains the capacity for psychological systems to change.

### 2. DEFINITION
Psychological plasticity is the capacity of psychological functioning to change in response to experience, learning, development, or environmental conditions.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Plasticity does not mean unlimited change. Existing biology, development, prior learning, and environmental constraints can limit what changes are possible.

Plasticity can occur across different timescales, from short-term learning to long-term development.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Plasticity explains how change is possible without treating humans as infinitely malleable.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General psychological capacity for change.  
**Defers:** Neural plasticity to 1.3 and learning mechanisms to 1.7.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Psychological systems can change.
- Plasticity has constraints.

---

## 1.1.23 Psychological Stability

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains continuity in psychological functioning.

### 2. DEFINITION
Psychological stability is the tendency for a psychological characteristic, pattern, or organization to remain relatively consistent across time or conditions.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Stability does not mean complete permanence. A characteristic can be stable on average while still changing.

Stability may result from biological factors, repeated environments, habits, learned patterns, or organized psychological systems.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Stability balances plasticity and explains why psychological change is not constant.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General stability concept.  
**Defers:** Trait stability to 1.14 and developmental stability/change to 1.15.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Stability is relative, not absolute.
- Stability and change can coexist.

---

## 1.1.24 Psychological Flexibility

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains adaptive adjustment without requiring a person to abandon stable organization.

### 2. DEFINITION
Psychological flexibility is the capacity to adjust psychological processes or behavior appropriately when goals, demands, or circumstances change.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Flexibility involves changing strategy when the current response no longer fits the situation. It is not simply being agreeable or changing one's mind frequently.

Flexibility can be useful when environments change, but constant switching can also reduce consistency and efficiency.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Flexibility connects adaptation with regulation and stability.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General psychological flexibility.  
**Defers:** Clinical and coping applications to 1.18 and 1.19.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Flexibility means appropriate adjustment.
- Flexibility is not the same as inconsistency.

## 1.1.25 Psychological Change

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic defines change as a basic property of psychological functioning.

### 2. DEFINITION
Psychological change is a meaningful alteration in psychological processes, states, traits, capacities, representations, or behavior over time.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Change can result from development, learning, experience, biological change, social conditions, intentional effort, or their interaction.

Change may be gradual or relatively rapid. It can be temporary or enduring.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Change completes the chapter's basic stability–plasticity architecture.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General psychological change.  
**Defers:** Specific forms of change to their canonical chapters.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Psychological change has multiple causes.
- Change must be distinguished from temporary fluctuation.

# PROCESSING ARCHITECTURE

## 1.1.26 Stimulus

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic defines the input side of a basic psychological processing sequence.

### 2. DEFINITION
A stimulus is an event, object, signal, or change in the environment or body that can be detected and potentially influence psychological processing.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
A stimulus is not automatically perceived or interpreted. Detection depends on sensory systems, attention, context, and other conditions.

The same physical stimulus can produce different psychological responses in different people.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Stimulus is the starting concept in the chapter's processing architecture.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General stimulus concept.  
**Defers:** Sensory detection and perception to 1.4.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- A stimulus can provide input without determining a response.
- Detection and interpretation are separate processes.

---

## 1.1.27 Psychological Processing

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic provides the general concept for operations performed on psychological input.

### 2. DEFINITION
Psychological processing is the set of operations through which information or internal states are detected, selected, represented, interpreted, evaluated, and used to guide responses.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Processing can involve multiple stages, but real psychological systems do not always operate as a simple fixed sequence.

Processing may be automatic, controlled, conscious, or nonconscious, depending on the task and conditions.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
It is the umbrella concept connecting the processing architecture topics that follow.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General processing architecture.  
**Defers:** Specific processes to their canonical chapters.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Processing transforms information into usable psychological representations or responses.
- Processing can involve interacting pathways rather than one fixed sequence.

## 1.1.28 Information Selection

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains why only some available information receives processing priority.

### 2. DEFINITION
Information selection is the process of prioritizing some available information for further processing while other information receives less processing.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Selection can be influenced by goals, salience, expectations, learned relevance, and limitations in processing capacity.

Selection does not mean ignored information is necessarily absent from all processing.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
It provides the bridge from available input to attention and interpretation.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General selection concept.  
**Defers:** Attention mechanisms to 1.6.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Humans do not process all available information equally.
- Selection is influenced by both the person and the environment.

## 1.1.29 Interpretation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains how psychological systems assign meaning to information.

### 2. DEFINITION
Interpretation is the process of assigning meaning, significance, or likely explanation to information or events.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Interpretation depends on available information, prior knowledge, expectations, goals, context, and current state.

Interpretation can be useful while still being wrong. Meaning is not guaranteed by confidence.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Interpretation is a central transformation between input and response.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General interpretation process.  
**Defers:** Perception, attribution, appraisal, and reasoning mechanisms to their owners.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Interpretation adds meaning to information.
- Interpretation can be accurate, incomplete, or mistaken.

## 1.1.30 Representation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains how information can be represented internally.

### 2. DEFINITION
A mental representation is an internal format or structure used by the mind to stand for information, objects, events, relationships, or possibilities.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Representations can support memory, planning, reasoning, language, and action.

Representations are not necessarily literal copies of reality. They can be selective, abstract, incomplete, or distorted.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Representation is the bridge between incoming information and later psychological operations.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General representation concept.  
**Defers:** Concepts, schemas, mental models, and imagery to 1.9; memory representations to 1.8.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Representations allow information to be used beyond the immediate stimulus.
- A representation is not the same thing as reality.

## 1.1.31 Evaluation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains how psychological systems assign significance or value to information.

### 2. DEFINITION
Evaluation is the process of assessing information, events, states, or options in relation to goals, standards, expectations, or values.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Evaluation can involve judgments of relevance, desirability, danger, usefulness, fairness, confidence, or importance.

Different goals or states can produce different evaluations of the same event.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Evaluation connects representation with decision, emotion, motivation, and action.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General evaluation process.  
**Defers:** Appraisal, judgment, and decision mechanisms to their canonical chapters.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Evaluation depends partly on standards and goals.
- The same event can receive different evaluations under different conditions.

## 1.1.32 Response Selection

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic defines the transition from psychological evaluation to choosing a response.

### 2. DEFINITION
Response selection is the process of choosing among available behavioral, cognitive, emotional, or regulatory responses.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Selection can depend on goals, expected outcomes, habits, learned associations, available options, current state, and environmental constraints.

Selection is not identical to execution. A person can select a response but fail to carry it out.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Response selection connects internal processing with action.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General response-selection concept.  
**Defers:** Decision-making to 1.10 and action control to 1.21.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Selecting a response and executing it are distinct.
- Available options constrain selection.

## 1.1.33 Action

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic introduces action as an output of psychological organization.

### 2. DEFINITION
An action is behavior organized toward an outcome, purpose, or response to a condition.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Actions can be deliberate or automatic. They can be shaped by goals, learning, habits, motivation, social context, and environmental feedback.

An action is not simply movement; it is behavior understood in relation to its organization and outcome.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Action closes the basic processing architecture and is explored in depth in Chapter 1.21.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** Action as the general output concept in this architecture.  
**Defers:** Action control, initiation, execution, and monitoring to 1.21.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Actions connect psychological processing to behavior.
- Action is shaped by both internal and environmental factors.

## 1.1.34 Feedback

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains how outcomes can change later psychological processing.

### 2. DEFINITION
Feedback is information about the effects or results of a process or action that can influence subsequent processing or behavior.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Feedback can confirm, correct, or change expectations and behavior. It can come from the environment, other people, internal states, or performance outcomes.

Feedback does not automatically produce learning. Its effect depends on interpretation, attention, motivation, timing, and prior knowledge.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Feedback creates a loop between action and later processing.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General feedback architecture.  
**Defers:** Learning from feedback to 1.7 and performance feedback to 1.21.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Feedback connects outcomes to later processing.
- Feedback must be processed to influence behavior.

## 1.1.35 Self-Regulation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic introduces the general capacity to influence one's own states, processes, and behavior.

### 2. DEFINITION
Self-regulation is the process of monitoring and influencing one's thoughts, emotions, motivation, attention, or behavior in relation to goals or standards.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Self-regulation can involve setting or maintaining goals, monitoring progress, controlling responses, changing strategies, and recovering after setbacks.

It is not a single mental power. It depends on multiple processes, including attention, motivation, emotion, learning, and action control.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Self-regulation is a central bridge between internal processing and adaptive behavior. Its canonical home is Chapter 1.12.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General self-regulation as an architectural concept.  
**Defers:** Full self-regulation mechanisms to 1.12.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Self-regulation involves monitoring and adjustment.
- Self-regulation depends on multiple psychological systems.

## 1.1.36 Reciprocal Causation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains why psychological relationships can run in both directions.

### 2. DEFINITION
Reciprocal causation refers to a process in which two or more variables influence one another over time.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
A person's behavior can change the environment, while the environment changes later behavior. Beliefs can influence behavior, and behavior can provide experiences that change beliefs.

Reciprocal causation is a causal claim and therefore requires evidence appropriate for causal inference.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
It supports the chapter's rejection of overly simple one-way explanations.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General reciprocal-causation architecture.  
**Defers:** Specific causal mechanisms to their canonical domains.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Causal influence can be bidirectional.
- Temporal order matters when evaluating reciprocal causation.

## 1.1.37 Dynamic Interaction

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains psychological systems as changing interactions rather than static collections of parts.

### 2. DEFINITION
Dynamic interaction is ongoing change produced by interactions among psychological processes, internal states, behavior, and environmental conditions.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
The importance of one process can change as another process changes. Feedback can amplify, reduce, stabilize, or redirect a pattern.

Dynamic interaction does not mean behavior is unpredictable. It means behavior may depend on changing relationships over time.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
It provides the process-level foundation for systems and dynamic-systems perspectives.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General dynamic interaction.  
**Defers:** Formal dynamic-systems models to the Theory/Model registry.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Psychological patterns can change through interaction.
- Time and feedback can alter relationships among processes.

## 1.1.38 Temporal Dynamics

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic makes time an explicit part of psychological explanation.

### 2. DEFINITION
Temporal dynamics are patterns of psychological change and interaction across time.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Some processes occur within seconds; others develop across days, years, or a lifetime. A cause can have immediate effects and different later effects.

Cross-sectional observations can therefore miss important temporal processes.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Temporal dynamics distinguish static description from processes unfolding over time.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General temporal dimension of psychology.  
**Defers:** Developmental change to 1.15 and time-specific processes to their owners.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Psychological processes operate on different timescales.
- Timing can change the meaning of a relationship.

## 1.1.39 Psychological Continuity

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains how psychological functioning can remain connected across time despite change.

### 2. DEFINITION
Psychological continuity is the persistence of meaningful organization, identity, tendencies, memories, or processes across time.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Continuity does not require identical states. A person can change while maintaining enough organization to experience a connected life history.

Memory, identity, personality, relationships, and habits can contribute to continuity.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Continuity balances temporal change and supports the concept of a psychologically organized person.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General continuity concept.  
**Defers:** Memory and identity mechanisms to 1.8 and 1.14.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Continuity is compatible with change.
- Multiple systems can support continuity.

## 1.1.40 Psychological Emergence

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains how organized psychological properties can arise from interactions among components.

### 2. DEFINITION
Psychological emergence refers to properties or patterns that arise from interactions among multiple components and are not adequately described by any single component alone.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Complex behavior can depend on interactions among perception, memory, motivation, emotion, social information, and action.

Emergence does not mean that higher-level behavior cannot be scientifically explained. It means the explanation must include relevant interactions.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Emergence supports a multilevel systems view without rejecting lower-level mechanisms.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General emergence concept.  
**Defers:** Specific emergent patterns to their canonical domains.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Some psychological patterns arise from interactions.
- Emergence does not make explanation impossible.

# LEVELS AND EXPLANATIONS

## 1.1.41 Levels of Analysis

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic explains the different units at which psychological questions can be studied.

### 2. DEFINITION
A level of analysis is the scale or unit used to examine a psychological phenomenon.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Possible levels include neural activity, individual psychological processes, interpersonal interaction, groups, institutions, and broader cultural systems.

A phenomenon can be studied at several levels without those levels being interchangeable.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
Levels of analysis prevent researchers from treating one scale as the only legitimate way to understand behavior.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General distinction among analytical levels.  
**Defers:** Specific scientific methods to 1.2.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Different levels answer different questions.
- A higher-level explanation does not automatically replace a lower-level explanation.

## 1.1.42 Levels of Explanation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic distinguishes where a phenomenon is observed from how it is explained.

### 2. DEFINITION
A level of explanation is the kind of mechanism or account used to explain a psychological phenomenon, such as biological, cognitive, social, or cultural explanation.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
The same behavior can have biological, psychological, social, and cultural explanations that address different parts of the causal system.

These explanations can complement one another. They should not be treated as interchangeable or automatically combined without evidence.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
This distinction supports the chapter's multilevel architecture.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General explanatory-level distinction.  
**Defers:** Specific explanatory mechanisms to their owners.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Different explanations can answer different questions about the same phenomenon.
- Explanation should match the question being asked.

### 2. DEFINITION
A biological explanation accounts for psychological phenomena using biological structures, processes, activity, regulation, or biological variation.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Biological explanations can involve nervous-system activity, hormones, genes, bodily regulation, and evolutionary processes.

A biological explanation does not automatically make psychological or social explanations unnecessary.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER

### 2. DEFINITION
A psychological explanation accounts for behavior or experience through psychological processes, states, representations, mechanisms, or functions.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Examples include explaining behavior through attention, memory, motivation, emotion, learning, beliefs, or decision processes.

Psychological explanations can themselves operate at different levels of detail.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER

This Topic defines explanation through interpersonal and group processes.

### 2. DEFINITION
A social explanation accounts for psychological phenomena through interactions with other people, social relationships, norms, groups, roles, or social structures.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Social conditions can shape perception, identity, emotion, motivation, judgment, and behavior.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Culture can influence how people interpret situations, regulate behavior, understand the self, and relate to others.

A multilevel explanation integrates relevant biological, psychological, social, cultural, and environmental levels without treating them as identical.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
A multilevel account should specify how levels relate. Simply listing biology, psychology, and culture is not an explanation.

## 1.1.51 Functional Explanation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic distinguishes explanation based on what a psychological process does from explanation based only on its structure.

### 2. DEFINITION
A functional explanation describes a process or system in terms of the role it performs or the outcome it contributes to.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
A functional account may explain why a process is useful or what problem it helps solve without identifying every mechanism that produces it.

Functional explanations can guide mechanistic research, but function does not prove mechanism.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
It provides one of the basic ways to explain psychological systems.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** Functional explanation as an explanatory type.  
**Defers:** Specific functions and mechanisms to their canonical domains.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Function describes what a process contributes.
- Function and mechanism are not identical.

A structural explanation accounts for a psychological phenomenon through the organization, components, or architecture of the system involved.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Structural explanations can identify components and their relationships. They are useful for understanding how a system is organized.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Process explanations can identify changes from input to output, including intermediate states and feedback.

## 1.1.54 Mechanistic Explanation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic distinguishes a true mechanism-focused explanation from a simple description of association.

### 2. DEFINITION
A mechanistic explanation identifies the organized processes and interactions through which one condition produces or changes another.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
A mechanistic explanation should identify relevant components, their operations, and how their interaction produces the outcome.

Mechanistic explanations can be incomplete and can operate across levels. Evidence must support the proposed mechanism rather than merely the observed association.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
This is the deepest explanatory standard in the chapter and supports the project's emphasis on mechanism over surface description.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** General mechanistic explanation.  
**Defers:** Specific mechanisms to their canonical owners.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES

- A plausible mechanism is not automatically an established mechanism.

## 1.1.55 State–Trait–Process Distinction

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic prevents temporary conditions, enduring tendencies, and ongoing operations from being treated as the same kind of psychological object.

### 2. DEFINITION
A state is a relatively temporary condition; a trait is a relatively enduring tendency; a process is an operation or change occurring over time.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
The distinction is about conceptual role, not absolute duration. A state can persist for a long period, and traits can change.

For example, temporary anxiety can be treated as a state, anxiety proneness as a trait-like individual difference, and threat appraisal as a process.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
This distinction provides a basic ontology for classifying psychological knowledge.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general state–trait–process distinction.  
**Defers:** Specific states, traits, and processes to their owners.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- State, trait, and process describe different kinds of psychological objects.
- Duration alone does not determine the category.

## 1.1.56 Nature–Nurture Question

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic frames the long-standing question of how biological inheritance and experience contribute to psychological development.

### 2. DEFINITION
The nature–nurture question concerns the relative and interacting contributions of inherited biological factors and environmental experience to psychological characteristics.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Modern psychology generally does not treat nature and nurture as independent alternatives. Genes develop and operate within environments, and environments can influence biological development and expression.

Heritability describes variation within a population under particular conditions. It does not measure how much of one individual's characteristic was caused by genes.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
This question belongs in the foundation because it shapes how psychological differences and development are explained.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general nature–nurture framework.  
**Defers:** Heritability, gene–environment interaction, and development to 1.3 and 1.15.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Nature and nurture interact.
- Heritability is not an individual's genetic percentage.

## 1.1.57 General Principles and Individual Variation

### 1. PURPOSE
This Topic closes the chapter by establishing how universal psychological knowledge can coexist with individual differences.

### 2. DEFINITION
General principles are patterns that apply across people or contexts to a meaningful degree; individual variation refers to systematic differences in how those principles appear across individuals or situations.

### 3. CORE UNDERSTANDING
Psychological science often identifies average or probabilistic patterns rather than rules that predict every individual perfectly.

A principle can be real even when exceptions exist. At the same time, individual variation can reveal important moderators, mechanisms, or boundary conditions.

The correct question is often not whether a psychological principle is universal in every case, but under what conditions it holds and for whom.

### 4. RELATIONSHIP TO THIS CHAPTER
This Topic is the final architectural principle of Chapter 1.1. It protects the chapter from two opposite errors: treating psychology as a set of universal rigid laws, or treating individual variation as proof that general psychological principles do not exist.

### 5. BOUNDARIES
**Owns:** The general relationship between psychological regularities and individual variation.  
**Defers:** Specific individual differences, moderators, and population differences to their canonical chapters.

### 6. KEY PRINCIPLES
- Psychological principles are often probabilistic rather than absolute.
- Individual differences do not eliminate general psychological science.
- Boundary conditions are part of correct understanding.
