# 1.1.13 — Human Functioning

## 1. Purpose

Human functioning is the broad outcome-level concept used to describe how psychological, biological, behavioral, social, and environmental capacities work together in real life. It prevents psychology from reducing a person to one isolated symptom, trait, behavior, or mental process.

## 2. Definition

Human functioning is the way a person operates across psychological, behavioral, biological, social, and environmental domains over time, including capacities, limitations, adaptation, relationships, goal pursuit, and everyday activity.

## 3. Core Understanding

Functioning is multidimensional. A person can perform strongly in one domain while experiencing difficulty in another. Cognitive ability, emotional regulation, relationships, health, work, self-care, and social participation can interact without being identical.

Functioning is also context-dependent. A capacity may be expressed well in one environment and poorly in another because demands, supports, resources, expectations, and opportunities differ.

Functioning should not be equated with conformity to a single social standard. Psychological science must distinguish impairment, distress, difference, and cultural variation rather than treating every deviation from a norm as dysfunction.

Functioning changes over time. Development, learning, illness, stress, relationships, and environmental change can improve or constrain functioning. Assessment therefore needs to consider time and context.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

Human functioning integrates the foundational architecture: person, behavior, mental processes, states, capacities, context, adaptation, stability, and change.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
- General integrated functioning of a person across domains.
- The multidimensional and contextual nature of functioning.

### Defers
- Health and well-being → 1.18.

## 6. Key Principles

1. Human functioning is multidimensional.
2. Functioning depends on person and context.
3. Strength in one domain does not imply strength in all domains.
4. Functioning can change across development and circumstances.
5. Difference should not automatically be treated as dysfunction.
