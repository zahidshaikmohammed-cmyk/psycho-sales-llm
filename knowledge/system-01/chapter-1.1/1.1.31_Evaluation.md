# 1.1.31 — Evaluation

## 1. Purpose
Evaluation explains how psychological systems assign significance, value, threat, usefulness, desirability, or relevance to information. It is a core bridge from representation to decision, emotion, motivation, and action.

## 2. Definition
Evaluation is the process of assessing information, events, options, or internal conditions against goals, standards, expectations, values, or learned criteria.

## 3. Core Understanding

Evaluation is one of the main ways a psychological system moves from representation to significance. A person can represent an event without yet deciding whether it is good, bad, useful, dangerous, unfair, desirable, or relevant. Evaluation adds a relation between information and some standard, goal, need, expectation, value, or learned association.

Evaluations can be rapid or deliberate. A familiar stimulus may immediately feel safe or threatening. A complex decision may require explicit comparison of costs, benefits, probabilities, and values. These forms can coexist: a rapid evaluation can provide an initial orientation while deliberate reasoning revises it.

Evaluation is relative to a reference point. The same outcome may be experienced as success by one person and failure by another because their goals differ. The same objective event may also be evaluated differently by the same person at different times because needs, expectations, or information have changed.

Evaluation can influence emotion, motivation, attention, and action. An event evaluated as threatening can increase defensive readiness; an option evaluated as valuable can increase pursuit. These are relationships, not universal deterministic rules. Other goals, constraints, and competing processes can alter the outcome.

Evaluation is also vulnerable to error. People may use incomplete information, biased comparisons, misleading expectations, or inaccurate beliefs. A subjective evaluation can be psychologically real without being objectively correct. Scientific analysis therefore distinguishes what a person evaluates from whether that evaluation corresponds to independently supported facts.

Evaluation can be updated. New evidence can change the perceived meaning of an event, although updating may be limited by prior commitments, emotional investment, habits, or social consequences. This makes evaluation part of an ongoing feedback system rather than a one-time judgment.


## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Evaluation connects representation, interpretation, motivation, emotion, decision-making, response selection, and action.

## 6. Key Principles
1. Evaluation assigns significance using internal and contextual criteria.
2. Evaluations can be automatic or deliberate.
3. Goals, expectations, values, and prior learning influence evaluation.
4. Subjective evaluation is not identical to objective reality.
5. Evaluation can change when information or standards change.
