## 1.13.1 Affect

## 1. Purpose
Establish affect as the broad class of psychological phenomena involving experienced or expressed positive and negative feeling states.

## 2. Definition
Affect is the general psychological term for evaluative feeling states, including their positive–negative quality and, depending on the framework, their activation or arousal.

### Conceptual distinction
Affect is broader than a discrete emotion. Mood, emotion, and momentary feelings can all be studied as affective phenomena, but they differ in temporal pattern, elicitation, specificity, and organization.

## 3. Core Understanding
Affect provides a broad descriptive language for how experience is felt as pleasant or unpleasant, activated or subdued, and otherwise emotionally toned. In research, affect is often represented dimensionally rather than as a list of named emotions. Valence describes whether an experience is positive or negative; arousal describes activation or energy. These dimensions can vary partly independently, allowing states such as calm pleasantness, excited pleasantness, tense unpleasantness, or low-energy unpleasantness. Affect can be conscious, behaviorally expressed, physiologically accompanied, or inferred from several indicators, and these channels do not always agree.

Affective states influence attention, memory, action readiness, motivation, and social behavior, but these relationships are context-dependent. Affect is not a single internal signal that mechanically determines behavior. The same unpleasant state can lead to avoidance, persistence, information seeking, or social approach depending on goals and circumstances. Likewise, a positive state can facilitate exploration in some settings while reducing vigilance in others. Affect should therefore be described without assuming that “positive” always means adaptive or “negative” always means harmful.

Measurement also requires care. Self-report captures conscious experience, while facial behavior, physiology, reaction time, and behavioral choice provide different kinds of evidence. No single measure is a complete readout of affect.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Affect is the broad organizing concept for the chapter's treatment of mood, emotion, feeling, valence, arousal, expression, regulation, and discrete affective experiences.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The broad construct of affect and its general dimensions as affective phenomena.

### Defers
Mood, emotion, arousal, specific emotions, regulation, and expression to their dedicated Topics.

## 6. Key Principles
- Affect is a broad family of feeling-related psychological phenomena.
- Valence and arousal are useful dimensions but do not exhaust affective organization.
- Different affective measures capture different components.

## 1.13.2 Mood

## 1. Purpose
Distinguish mood as a relatively diffuse and temporally extended affective state.

## 2. Definition
Mood is a relatively sustained affective state that is often less tightly tied to a specific identifiable stimulus than an emotional episode.

### Conceptual distinction
Mood is not simply a weak emotion. It can last longer, have less clearly identified elicitation, and influence how subsequent events are interpreted.

## 3. Core Understanding
Moods vary in valence, activation, intensity, duration, and stability. A person can experience a positive, negative, activated, or subdued mood without being able to identify one immediate event that caused it. Moods may arise from recent experiences, bodily conditions, social environments, expectations, sleep and other contextual factors, but their causes are often distributed rather than singular.

Because mood persists across situations, it can bias attention, interpretation, memory retrieval, social behavior, and judgments. These effects are tendencies rather than deterministic rules. A negative mood may increase attention to threat or loss in some circumstances, while a positive mood may broaden exploration in others; task demands and goals can reverse or constrain such effects.

Mood is also partly inferred from patterns over time. A momentary unpleasant feeling does not by itself establish a sustained mood state. Research commonly measures mood through repeated self-report or state measures, while recognizing that retrospective reports can be affected by memory and interpretation.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Mood is the chapter's principal sustained affective state and provides an important contrast with discrete emotional episodes.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Mood as a relatively diffuse, sustained affective state.

### Defers
Discrete emotion, emotional episodes, and clinical mood disorders.

## 6. Key Principles
- Mood is typically more diffuse and extended than a discrete emotional episode.
- Mood can influence processing without determining behavior.
- Duration and elicitation pattern matter for distinguishing mood from emotion.

## 1.13.3 Emotion

## 1. Purpose
Define emotion as an organized psychological process involving meaningful events, appraisal, feeling, action readiness, expression, and bodily change.

## 2. Definition
An emotion is a coordinated affective response to an actual, imagined, remembered, or anticipated event that is relevant to the person's goals, needs, values, or concerns.

### Conceptual distinction
Emotion is not identical to subjective feeling. Feeling is the consciously experienced component of an emotional process; emotion can include appraisal, action tendency, physiology, expression, and regulation.

## 3. Core Understanding
Emotion is a multicomponent phenomenon. Depending on the theoretical framework, an emotional response can include changes in subjective feeling, physiological activity, attention, interpretation, action readiness, facial or vocal expression, and behavior. These components often co-occur but are not perfectly synchronized. A person can show physiological activation without reporting a strong emotion, or experience an emotion while suppressing its expression.

Emotions are often elicited by events that matter to the person. The same event can therefore produce different emotions in different people or in the same person at different times. Appraisal processes, learning history, goals, social context, and bodily state can alter what an event means. Emotions can be generated by external events as well as memory, imagination, prediction, or interpretation.

Emotion is neither inherently rational nor irrational. It provides information about relevance and prepares action, but emotional responses can also be shaped by inaccurate interpretations, learned associations, or context-insensitive habits. Scientific theories disagree about whether emotions are best understood as biologically prepared categories, dimensional states, appraisals, or constructed episodes. These disagreements concern the architecture of emotion, not whether people experience affective responses.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Emotion is the chapter's central construct. Other Topics specify its components, generation processes, theories, regulation, expression, and particular emotional experiences.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The general construct of emotion as an organized affective response.

### Defers
Specific mechanisms, theories, regulation processes, and discrete emotions to their dedicated Topics.

## 6. Key Principles
- Emotion is multicomponent rather than reducible to feeling alone.
- Emotional responses depend on significance and context.
- Major theories disagree about emotional architecture.

## 1.13.4 Emotional episode

## 1. Purpose
Explain the temporally bounded occurrence of an emotion as it unfolds in response to a meaningful event or representation.

## 2. Definition
An emotional episode is a temporally organized occurrence in which affective, cognitive, physiological, expressive, and action-related processes become coordinated around an emotionally significant event or representation.

## 3. Core Understanding
An emotional episode has a beginning, development, and changing course rather than existing as a static label. A person notices or represents an event, evaluates its significance, undergoes affective and bodily changes, develops action readiness, and may express or regulate the response. These processes can overlap and unfold at different speeds.

Episodes vary greatly. Some are brief and intense; others develop gradually and remain active as circumstances are reconsidered. An episode can also be reactivated by memory or imagination after the original event is gone. Its course depends on continued appraisal, environmental feedback, competing goals, and regulation.

The concept is useful because emotion words can conceal temporal dynamics. “Anger” may describe an initial response, sustained state, or recurrent episode, even though each has different mechanisms. Studying episodes makes it possible to ask what triggered the response, how it changed, what interrupted it, and which components were synchronized or dissociated.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Emotional episode connects the chapter's static-sounding constructs to the dynamic sequence of emotion generation and regulation.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The episode-level organization and temporal unfolding of emotional responding.

### Defers
The general construct of emotion and the specific processes that generate or regulate episodes.

## 6. Key Principles
- Emotional episodes unfold over time.
- Components can change at different rates.
- An emotion label does not describe the whole temporal process.

## 1.13.5 Emotional stimulus

## 1. Purpose
Identify the event, object, cue, memory, thought, or representation that becomes emotionally significant.

## 2. Definition
An emotional stimulus is an internal or external event or representation that elicits or modulates an affective response because it has psychological significance for the individual.

## 3. Core Understanding
Emotional stimuli are not inherently emotional in a universal sense. Their effects depend on the organism, learning history, current goals, context, and interpretation. A loud sound may elicit fear in one context, surprise in another, or little response after repeated harmless exposure. A remembered event can function as an emotional stimulus even when nothing comparable is happening externally.

Stimuli can be concrete, social, symbolic, anticipated, remembered, or imagined. Their emotional effect can also change as the person learns more about them. A cue associated with threat may become less potent when safety learning changes its meaning, while a neutral cue can acquire emotional significance through conditioning or social learning.

The term stimulus should not imply a simple stimulus-response chain. The same input can produce different emotional outcomes because perception, appraisal, memory, goals, and bodily state intervene between event and response.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Emotional stimulus provides the event-side counterpart to emotion generation and appraisal.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The concept of emotionally significant eliciting or modulating events and representations.

### Defers
Appraisal and emotion generation mechanisms.

## 6. Key Principles
- Emotional significance is context-dependent.
- Internal representations can function as emotional stimuli.
- Stimulus does not mean deterministic cause.

## 1.13.6 Emotional valence

## 1. Purpose
Define the positive–negative dimension of affective experience.

## 2. Definition
Emotional valence is the degree to which an affective experience is experienced or characterized as pleasant versus unpleasant, desirable versus aversive.

## 3. Core Understanding
Valence is a fundamental dimension used to describe affective quality. Positive valence indicates that an experience is generally pleasant or desirable; negative valence indicates unpleasantness or aversiveness. Valence does not specify the emotion's exact identity. Fear, anger, and disgust can all be negatively valenced while differing greatly in action tendency, appraisal, and physiology. Likewise, joy and calmness can both be positive while differing in activation.

Valence can change during an emotional episode and can depend on comparison standards, context, and interpretation. An initially unpleasant experience can acquire positive meaning if it signals progress toward a valued goal, while a pleasant experience can become aversive if it conflicts with another goal.

Valence is often measured with self-report scales, affective ratings, or behavioral and physiological indicators. These measures should not be assumed interchangeable. Valence also does not equal adaptive value. Some unpleasant states are useful signals, and some pleasant states can accompany poor decisions.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Valence supplies one major dimension for organizing affective states and complements arousal and more specific emotional categories.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The pleasant–unpleasant dimension of affective experience.

### Defers
Arousal, intensity, discrete emotions, and motivational consequences.

## 6. Key Principles
- Valence concerns pleasantness versus unpleasantness.
- Valence does not identify a discrete emotion.
- Pleasantness and adaptive value are not identical.

## 1.13.7 Arousal

## 1. Purpose
Distinguish affective activation from emotional valence.

## 2. Definition
Arousal is the degree of activation or energetic mobilization associated with a psychological or affective state.

## 3. Core Understanding
Arousal is often used as a dimension of affect describing how activated or energized a state is, ranging from relatively low activation to high activation. It is conceptually distinct from valence. Excitement and calm satisfaction can both be positive while differing in arousal; fear and sadness can both be negative while also differing in activation.

Arousal can refer to psychological activation, physiological activation, or both, depending on the measurement framework. These forms are related but not identical. Heart rate, skin conductance, and other physiological measures provide evidence about bodily activation, while self-report can capture perceived activation. A person can feel highly activated without every physiological measure showing a corresponding increase, and physiological activation can occur without a clearly identified conscious emotion.

Arousal can alter attention, action readiness, and performance, but effects depend on task demands, baseline state, and the specific source of activation. It should therefore not be treated as a universal “more is better” or “more is worse” variable.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Arousal is one of the chapter's major affective dimensions and must be distinguished from physiological arousal and emotional intensity.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Affective activation as a psychological dimension.

### Defers
Physiological arousal and emotional intensity.

## 6. Key Principles
- Arousal concerns activation, not pleasantness.
- Psychological and physiological activation can diverge.
- Effects of arousal depend on context and task demands.

## 1.13.8 Emotional intensity

## 1. Purpose
Explain how strongly an emotional experience is experienced or expressed.

## 2. Definition
Emotional intensity is the magnitude or strength of an emotional response as experienced, expressed, or otherwise measured.

## 3. Core Understanding
Intensity is distinct from valence and arousal. Two experiences can have the same pleasantness but differ in intensity, and high physiological activation does not automatically establish high subjective emotional intensity. Intensity can refer to the felt strength of an emotion, the magnitude of expression, or the strength of another measurable component, so research must specify what is being measured.

Intensity changes during episodes and can be amplified or reduced by attention, appraisal, uncertainty, personal significance, bodily state, and regulation. Strong emotion does not necessarily imply accurate appraisal or important objective circumstances. Conversely, emotionally important events can sometimes produce muted outward expression because expression is regulated or because people differ in expressive style.

The meaning of intensity is therefore measurement-dependent. A self-report rating answers a different question from facial movement or physiological response. Treating all as interchangeable can create false precision.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Intensity clarifies the magnitude dimension of emotion and prevents confusion between strength, valence, and activation.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The general concept of emotional response magnitude.

### Defers
Arousal, expression, physiology, and subjective feeling as distinct components.

## 6. Key Principles
- Intensity is about magnitude, not emotional category.
- Intensity must be tied to the component being measured.
- Strong expression is not identical to strong subjective feeling.

## 1.13.9 Physiological arousal

## 1. Purpose
Explain bodily activation associated with affective and emotional states.

## 2. Definition
Physiological arousal is a change in bodily activation involving systems such as cardiovascular, autonomic, respiratory, endocrine, or other physiological processes.

## 3. Core Understanding
Emotional states are often accompanied by bodily changes, including altered heart rate, respiration, skin conductance, vascular activity, muscle readiness, and hormonal activity. These changes help prepare the organism for action and reflect changing demands on regulation. However, physiological arousal is not a one-to-one signature of a particular emotion. The same bodily pattern can occur across different emotional states, and emotional experiences can occur with relatively modest measurable activation.

Measurement therefore requires interpretation. Heart rate, skin conductance, pupil size, respiration, and endocrine measures index different physiological processes. They are not interchangeable measures of a single “emotion meter.” Context, baseline physiology, movement, medication, health, and measurement conditions can also affect signals.

Physiological arousal can contribute to how people experience and interpret emotion, but the causal role differs across theories. Bodily feedback may be part of emotional generation without being sufficient to specify the emotion's meaning.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Physiological arousal provides the bodily component that must be distinguished from subjective feeling and broader psychological arousal.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The affect-relevant construct of bodily activation.

### Defers
General biological regulation and the nervous/endocrine systems to biological psychology.

## 6. Key Principles
- Bodily activation accompanies many emotional states.
- No single physiological measure uniquely identifies an emotion.
- Physiology and subjective feeling are related but not identical.

## 1.13.10 Subjective feeling

## 1. Purpose
Identify the consciously experienced aspect of affect and emotion.

## 2. Definition
Subjective feeling is the person's conscious experience of an affective or emotional state.

## 3. Core Understanding
Feeling is the experiential component of emotion: what an emotional state is like from the person's perspective. It can include perceived pleasantness, unpleasantness, tension, warmth, sadness, fear, anger, or other qualities. Feelings are usually accessed through self-report, but self-report is not a transparent recording of every underlying process. People can have limited awareness of their own affective states, and memory, language, interpretation, and social expectations can influence reports.

Subjective feeling should be distinguished from emotional expression and physiological arousal. A person may feel angry while remaining outwardly calm; another may show expressive behavior without reporting the corresponding feeling. Similarly, physiological activation can occur without a clear emotional label.

The concept is central to theories of consciousness and emotion because different theories disagree about whether conscious feeling is a necessary component of emotion or an emergent representation of coordinated processes. Regardless of theoretical position, subjective feeling is an important empirical phenomenon that must be measured on its own terms.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Subjective feeling is the experiential counterpart to physiological, expressive, appraisal, and action components of emotion.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The conscious experiential component of affective responding.

### Defers
Consciousness generally, emotional generation, and expression.

## 6. Key Principles
- Feeling is the experienced component, not the whole emotional process.
- Self-report is informative but not infallible.
- Feeling, expression, and physiology can dissociate.

## 1.13.11 Emotional expression

## 1. Purpose
Explain how affective states are communicated or manifested through observable behavior.

## 2. Definition
Emotional expression is observable behavior or physiological signaling through which an affective or emotional state is displayed, communicated, or inferred.

## 3. Core Understanding
Emotional expression can occur through facial movement, vocal characteristics, posture, gestures, language, action, and other socially observable behavior. Expression may communicate internal states to others, regulate interaction, or occur as a partially automatic response. It is influenced by the underlying emotional state but is not a perfect readout of it.

People regulate expression according to social norms, goals, relationships, culture, and consequences. A person may conceal fear, amplify enthusiasm, or display calmness while internally distressed. Conversely, expressive behavior can be habitual or strategically produced without matching the reported emotion.

The relation between expression and emotion is therefore probabilistic and context-sensitive. Facial movement should not be treated as a universal decoder that reveals a person's exact internal state. Different expressions can have overlapping meanings, and observers use situational information to interpret them.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Expression connects internal affective processes with interpersonal behavior and leads directly to display rules and expression regulation.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Observable affective and emotional expression as a psychological phenomenon.

### Defers
Specific display rules and regulation processes.

## 6. Key Principles
- Expression can communicate emotion but does not perfectly reveal it.
- Expression is shaped by context and social goals.
- Internal feeling and outward display can diverge.

## 1.13.12 Action tendency

## 1. Purpose
Explain the action-oriented component through which emotion prepares or biases behavior.

## 2. Definition
An action tendency is a motivational readiness or directional inclination toward a class of actions associated with an affective state.

## 3. Core Understanding
Emotions can organize behavior by making some actions more ready or attractive than others. Fear may prepare escape or avoidance; anger may increase readiness to confront an obstacle; disgust may support withdrawal; interest may support exploration. These are tendencies, not compulsory commands. The actual behavior depends on appraisal, goals, learned strategies, social constraints, available options, and self-regulation.

Action tendencies help explain why emotion is not merely a feeling. They connect affective significance to adaptive or learned behavioral responses. Yet a tendency can be inhibited, redirected, or overridden. A person can feel anger without confronting anyone, or fear without fleeing when escape is impossible or undesirable.

The same emotion can also support different actions in different contexts. Anger may promote confrontation when action is possible but persistence or strategic planning when direct confrontation is costly. Thus action tendency should be treated as directional readiness rather than a fixed behavioral script.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Action tendency links emotion to motivation and behavior while remaining distinct from the actual action.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Emotion-linked readiness toward classes of action.

### Defers
Motivation, action execution, and self-control.

## 6. Key Principles
- Emotion can bias action without determining it.
- Action tendency is not identical to overt behavior.
- Context can redirect the same emotional tendency.

## 1.13.13 Emotional awareness

## 1. Purpose
Define the capacity to notice, identify, and understand one's own affective states.

## 2. Definition
Emotional awareness is the ability to notice, identify, and represent one's own emotional or affective states with some degree of accuracy and differentiation.

## 3. Core Understanding
Emotional awareness ranges from detecting that “something feels different” to identifying a specific emotion and understanding its triggers, bodily signals, and implications. Awareness is therefore not all-or-none. A person may recognize arousal but misidentify it, notice distress without identifying its source, or accurately distinguish several related emotional states.

Awareness depends partly on attention to internal signals, language, conceptual knowledge, memory, context, and interpretive ability. It can support regulation because a person generally needs some representation of a state to deliberately modify it, although regulation can also occur without explicit emotional labeling.

Awareness should not be equated with emotional control or intelligence. Knowing what one feels does not guarantee effective regulation, and limited labeling can coexist with adaptive behavior. Measurement also requires care because self-report of emotional awareness is itself a report about a person's perceived awareness.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Emotional awareness provides a foundation for emotional granularity, differentiation, and deliberate regulation.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Awareness of one's own affective states.

### Defers
Emotional regulation, intelligence, and broader metacognition.

## 6. Key Principles
- Awareness varies in specificity and accuracy.
- Awareness and regulation are related but distinct.
- Emotional awareness is partly shaped by attention and concepts.

## 1.13.14 Emotional granularity

## 1. Purpose
Explain differences in how specifically people represent and describe emotional experiences.

## 2. Definition
Emotional granularity is the degree to which a person represents emotional experiences with fine-grained, differentiated categories rather than broad undifferentiated labels.

## 3. Core Understanding
A person with greater emotional granularity may distinguish irritation, frustration, resentment, disappointment, and anger rather than labeling all of them simply “bad.” Granularity concerns the specificity of emotional representation across experiences, not merely vocabulary size. It can help a person detect meaningful differences in context, bodily state, goals, and action requirements.

Granularity is influenced by language, conceptual knowledge, learning history, attention, and cultural context. It should not be interpreted as a fixed trait that guarantees better emotional functioning. More detailed labeling can be useful when distinctions are accurate and actionable, but excessive categorization is not inherently superior.

Research often examines granularity by analyzing patterns of repeated emotion ratings and the consistency of differentiation across contexts. Measurement therefore concerns how a person organizes experience rather than simply how many emotion words they know.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Granularity explains one route by which emotional awareness becomes more precise and connects naturally with emotional differentiation.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The specificity of emotional representation across categories.

### Defers
Emotional awareness, language, and emotion regulation.

## 6. Key Principles
- Granularity concerns differentiated representation.
- More labels do not automatically mean more accuracy.
- Context and conceptual knowledge influence granularity.

## 1.13.15 Emotional differentiation

## 1. Purpose
Explain the capacity to distinguish among affective experiences that might otherwise be treated as similar.

## 2. Definition
Emotional differentiation is the degree to which a person experiences or represents distinct emotional states as meaningfully different from one another.

## 3. Core Understanding
Emotional differentiation is closely related to granularity but emphasizes the separation of emotional states within a person's experience. Someone may distinguish anxiety from sadness, anger from frustration, or excitement from fear even when the states share bodily activation. Differentiation can occur at the level of feeling, interpretation, action tendency, or context.

High differentiation does not mean experiencing emotions less strongly. It means that emotional space is represented with more distinctions. This can matter because different states may call for different responses. If all unpleasant activation is interpreted as “stress,” important differences in threat, loss, interpersonal conflict, or goal obstruction may be missed.

Differentiation is shaped by attention, learning, concepts, language, and repeated experience. It can vary by context and across individuals. It is therefore better understood as an empirical property of emotional representation than as a simple measure of emotional maturity.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Differentiation complements granularity by explaining how distinct emotional experiences are separated within affective representation.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The differentiation of emotional experiences from one another.

### Defers
Emotional granularity, awareness, and regulation.

## 6. Key Principles
- Differentiation means distinguishing emotional states.
- Similar arousal does not imply identical emotion.
- Differentiation is shaped by experience and conceptual systems.
