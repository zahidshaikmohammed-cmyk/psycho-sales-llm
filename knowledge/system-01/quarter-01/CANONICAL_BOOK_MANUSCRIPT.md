## CHAPTER 1.1 — FOUNDATIONS OF HUMAN PSYCHOLOGY

Human psychology is the scientific study of how people experience the world, process information, feel, think, learn, decide, relate to others, and act.

It is the foundation of PSYCHO SALES because every later sales event is carried by human psychological processes. A product does not make a decision. A market does not make a decision. A human being does.

But people do not respond to the outside world in a perfectly direct way.

Information is selected. It is given meaning. It is connected with memory. It is compared with goals and expectations. It can create emotion. Emotion can change judgment. Judgment can change action.

This does not mean that people live in imaginary worlds. It means that behavior is produced by an interaction between the environment and the psychological system interpreting that environment.

### 1.1.1 Human Psychology

Human psychology studies the processes through which people perceive, think, feel, learn, remember, choose, regulate themselves, interact with others, and behave.

Psychology is not simply the study of unusual behavior. It includes ordinary functioning. Attention, memory, motivation, emotion, learning, judgment, and social behavior are psychological processes operating every day.

A strong psychological explanation does not stop at what a person did. It asks what processes may have produced that behavior, what conditions changed those processes, and what evidence supports the explanation.

The same behavior can have different causes. A person may remain silent because of uncertainty, politeness, fear, lack of interest, or simple distraction. Behavior is evidence. It is not automatically a complete explanation.

### 1.1.2 Psychological Processes

A psychological process is an ongoing activity through which the person receives information, creates meaning, regulates an internal state, pursues a goal, learns from experience, or produces behavior.

Perception, attention, memory, emotion, motivation, reasoning, and self-regulation are examples.

These processes do not operate as separate boxes. Attention changes what is remembered. Memory changes what is expected. Expectations influence perception. Emotion can change attention. Goals can change what a person notices.

Human psychology is therefore a system of interacting processes.

### 1.1.3 Mental Processes

Mental processes are internal activities involved in experiencing and responding to the world.

They include representing information, interpreting events, remembering previous experiences, imagining possibilities, evaluating choices, and monitoring one's own thinking.

Some mental processes are conscious. Many are not.

A person can recognize a familiar pattern without deliberately explaining how recognition occurred. A feeling can influence a judgment before the person becomes aware of the influence.

Conscious thought is important, but it is not the whole of psychology.

### 1.1.4 Behavior

Behavior is what a person does, says, approaches, avoids, repeats, changes, or stops doing.

Behavior is the observable side of psychological functioning.

It gives us evidence, but it has limits. The same behavior can be produced by different psychological states.

A person who says "no" may be rejecting an idea, protecting themselves from uncertainty, asking for more information, or simply ending a conversation.

Psychological analysis therefore asks not only what happened, but what mechanisms could explain it.

### 1.1.5 Mind and Behavior

The mind and behavior are connected, but they are not identical.

Mental activity includes processes that may never become directly visible. A person may experience fear without showing obvious fear. Another person may behave calmly while experiencing strong internal conflict.

Behavior is an expression of psychological functioning, but it is not a transparent window into the entire mind.

Good psychology keeps internal processes and observable behavior connected without pretending that one perfectly reveals the other.

### 1.1.6 Psychological Mechanisms

A psychological mechanism explains how one condition contributes to another psychological or behavioral outcome.

A mechanism answers the question: how does this happen?

For example, attention can determine which information receives deeper processing. Deeper processing can influence memory. Memory can then influence later judgment.

Saying that two things are related is not the same as explaining the mechanism between them.

PSYCHO SALES therefore treats mechanism as a central part of depth.

### 1.1.7–1.1.12 States, Traits, Person, Situation, Context, and Groups

A psychological state is a person's relatively temporary condition at a particular time. Attention level, emotional state, uncertainty, fatigue, and motivation can change quickly.

A psychological trait is a relatively stable tendency to think, feel, or behave in certain ways across situations.

Neither state nor trait should be treated as destiny.

Human behavior develops through interaction between the person and the situation. The person brings memory, beliefs, goals, abilities, emotion, and learned tendencies. The situation provides information, constraints, opportunities, social signals, rewards, and consequences.

Context gives an event its meaning. The same words or action can produce different responses when the surrounding conditions change.

People also function inside groups. Group membership can influence identity, expectations, norms, attention, and behavior. Individual behavior cannot always be explained by individual characteristics alone.

### 1.1.13–1.1.15 Consciousness, Automaticity, and Change

Conscious processes are available to awareness at a given time. They allow deliberate examination, reflection, comparison, and intentional control.

Automatic processes operate with relatively little deliberate effort. They make familiar behavior efficient and reduce mental demand.

Automaticity is not automatically good or bad. It is useful when the learned response fits the situation. It can become a problem when a familiar response is applied to a new situation where the old rule no longer works.

Psychological change occurs when experience, learning, development, environment, or deliberate effort changes how a person thinks, feels, perceives, or behaves.

Human beings therefore have both stability and capacity for change.

### 1.1.16–1.1.18 Individual Differences, Adaptation, and Functioning

Individual differences are systematic differences between people in abilities, experience, beliefs, personality, goals, preferences, and psychological tendencies.

They matter because the same event does not produce identical responses in every person.

Psychological adaptation is the process of adjusting thoughts, feelings, behavior, or strategies to changing conditions.

Adaptation can be useful, but not every adaptation is healthy or accurate. A response may work in one environment and create problems in another.

Psychological functioning describes how well the person's psychological processes work together across thinking, emotion, motivation, learning, relationships, and behavior.

Healthy functioning does not mean feeling positive all the time. It can include tolerating difficult emotion, revising beliefs, learning from mistakes, and responding flexibly to reality.

### 1.1.19–1.1.20 Psychological Science and Evidence

Psychology becomes science when claims are studied systematically rather than accepted because they sound convincing.

Scientific psychology uses methods such as experiments, observation, measurement, correlation, longitudinal research, statistical analysis, theory testing, and replication.

Evidence quality depends on the question, method, sample, measurement, analysis, and consistency of findings.

A single observation may suggest a possibility. It usually cannot establish a universal psychological law.

Correlation does not automatically prove causation.

A practical technique may be useful without its underlying explanation being scientifically established.

This distinction is permanent in PSYCHO SALES: practical usefulness is not the same thing as scientific proof.

## CHAPTER 1.2 — PERCEPTION AND ATTENTION

People do not consciously experience every signal that reaches their senses.

The environment contains more information than the human system can process equally. Perception organizes sensory information into a meaningful experience. Attention determines which parts of available information receive greater processing.

The central idea is simple:

What exists in the environment is not identical to what a person notices, and what a person notices is not identical to what the person finally understands.

### 1.2.1–1.2.3 Perception, Sensation, and Sensory Processing

Sensation is the detection of physical stimulation by sensory systems.

Perception goes further. It organizes and interprets sensory information so that the person can experience objects, events, locations, movement, sounds, and bodily states as meaningful.

Sensory processing transforms physical signals into neural information that can be used by the brain.

Detection does not guarantee awareness.

A signal can reach a sensory system without becoming a clearly noticed or correctly interpreted experience.

### 1.2.4–1.2.6 Organization, Interpretation, and Selection

Perceptual organization combines separate sensory elements into meaningful patterns and objects.

The mind does not simply receive disconnected fragments. It searches for structure.

Perceptual interpretation then assigns meaning to what has been organized.

When evidence is incomplete or ambiguous, interpretation depends more strongly on previous knowledge, expectation, context, and current goals.

Perceptual selection determines which available information receives more processing.

This selection is necessary because the environment contains more information than the mind can process at equal depth.

### 1.2.7–1.2.10 Attention

Attention is the allocation of limited processing resources toward selected information, objects, locations, tasks, or thoughts.

Selective attention allows a person to focus on one source while reducing processing of competing information.

Sustained attention allows concentration over time.

Divided attention occurs when processing resources are shared across tasks.

Executive attention helps manage conflict and maintain goal-directed control.

Attention is limited. A person can be physically present while psychologically attending to something else.

### 1.2.11–1.2.15 Capture, Control, Blindness, Change, and Salience

Attentional capture occurs when something attracts attention because of novelty, movement, emotional significance, threat, reward, contrast, or personal relevance.

Attentional control is the ability to direct, maintain, shift, and disengage attention according to goals.

Inattentional blindness occurs when a visible stimulus is not noticed because attention is strongly engaged elsewhere.

Change blindness is difficulty detecting a change in a scene, especially when interruption or distraction separates the before and after states.

Salience describes how strongly something stands out relative to its surroundings.

These mechanisms show that attention must balance two needs: noticing important events and remaining focused on the chosen task.

### 1.2.16–1.2.21 Perceptual Bias, Processing Direction, Expectation, and Context

Perceptual bias occurs when perception or interpretation is systematically influenced by factors beyond the immediate sensory signal.

Top-down processing uses prior knowledge, expectations, goals, and context to interpret incoming information.

Bottom-up processing begins with incoming sensory information and builds toward more complex representations.

Real perception normally combines both.

Expectations can improve speed because the mind is prepared for likely information. But expectations can also distort interpretation when they become stronger than the evidence.

Context changes meaning. The same signal can be understood differently depending on what happened before it, what surrounds it, what the person expects, and what the person is trying to accomplish.

Perception is therefore not simply the recording of reality. It is an interaction between evidence and interpretation.

## CHAPTER 1.3 — LEARNING AND MEMORY

Learning changes what a person knows, expects, can do, or is likely to repeat.

Memory allows previous experience to influence the present.

Together they create continuity across time.

Learning is not only the memorization of facts. It can change habits, emotional responses, skills, expectations, categories, and strategies.

Memory is not a perfect recording device. What is remembered depends on how information was encoded, retained, retrieved, and interpreted later.

### 1.3.1–1.3.5 Learning and Association

Learning is a relatively lasting change in knowledge, skill, behavior, or response caused by experience or practice.

Associative learning occurs when the mind learns relationships between events, stimuli, actions, and outcomes.

Classical conditioning is one form of associative learning. A previously neutral stimulus can become associated with another stimulus and later produce a learned response.

Operant conditioning focuses on consequences. Reinforcement increases the future likelihood of a behavior. Punishment decreases it.

These terms must be used precisely. Reinforcement is defined by an increase in future behavior, not simply by whether something feels pleasant. Punishment is defined by a reduction in future behavior, not simply by whether something feels unpleasant.

### 1.3.6–1.3.9 Punishment, Observation, Social Learning, and Cognitive Learning

Punishment can suppress behavior, but suppression is not the same as teaching a better alternative.

Observational learning occurs when people acquire information or behavior by watching others. Direct experience is not always required.

Social learning is broader. It includes learning shaped by models, social feedback, norms, relationships, and shared environments.

Cognitive learning involves changes in knowledge, understanding, expectations, and mental representations.

A person can therefore learn without immediately showing a visible behavioral change.

### 1.3.10–1.3.13 Memory, Encoding, Storage, and Retrieval

Memory is the set of processes through which information is encoded, retained, and later retrieved.

Encoding transforms information into a form that can be retained. Attention and meaning strongly affect encoding.

Storage concerns retention over time.

Retrieval brings stored information into current mental use.

These processes are related but not identical.

A person can fail to retrieve information that was previously learned. Retrieval failure is not always proof that the information has disappeared.

Memory therefore needs to be understood as a process rather than as a single storage box.

### 1.3.14–1.3.18 Working and Long-Term Memory

Working memory temporarily holds and manipulates information during active thinking. Its limited capacity helps explain why complex tasks become harder when too much information must be handled at once.

Long-term memory supports information retained beyond immediate processing.

Episodic memory concerns personally experienced events.

Semantic memory stores general knowledge, meanings, concepts, and facts.

Procedural memory supports learned skills and action sequences.

These systems serve different functions. A person may remember how to perform a skill without remembering when it was learned, or know a fact without remembering the episode in which it was acquired.

### 1.3.19–1.3.23 Recognition, Recall, Forgetting, Distortion, and Cues

Recognition occurs when information is identified as familiar or previously encountered.

Recall requires the person to retrieve information with less direct support.

Forgetting is failure to retain or retrieve information that was previously available. It can arise from weak encoding, interference, reduced accessibility, or retrieval difficulty.

Memory distortion occurs when remembered information differs from the original experience.

Retrieval cues are pieces of information that help activate access to stored memories. A word, place, emotion, question, or related idea can serve as a cue.

Confidence and accuracy are not identical. A person can feel certain about a memory that contains errors.

### 1.3.24–1.3.25 Repetition and Transfer

Repetition can strengthen learning, but repetition alone is not always enough.

Learning becomes stronger when the person attends, understands, retrieves, practices, receives feedback, and applies the knowledge.

Transfer of learning occurs when knowledge or skill learned in one situation affects performance in another.

Transfer can be positive when previous learning helps. It can be negative when old learning interferes. It can also be weak when the new environment is very different.

Deep learning therefore requires more than memorizing surface patterns. It requires understanding principles that remain useful when conditions change.

### The Learning–Memory Loop

Learning changes memory.

Memory changes interpretation.

Interpretation changes behavior.

Behavior creates new experience.

New experience produces new learning.

This creates a continuing psychological loop.

Attention determines what receives deeper processing. Emotion can increase significance. Repetition can strengthen accessibility. Retrieval can change later accessibility. Feedback can correct inaccurate learning.

This loop is one of the foundations of human development.

It also explains why repeated experience does not automatically create expertise. If the person repeatedly practices an incorrect response, the system can become more efficient at producing the wrong response.

Accurate learning therefore requires accurate feedback.
