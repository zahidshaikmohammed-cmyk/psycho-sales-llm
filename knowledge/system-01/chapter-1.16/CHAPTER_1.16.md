# 1.16 — SOCIAL PSYCHOLOGY AND SOCIAL COGNITION

## 1. Social psychology

## 1. Purpose

Social psychology provides the chapter's broad scientific frame for understanding how people's thoughts, feelings, judgments, identities, and behavior are shaped by actual, imagined, or symbolically represented social contexts. This Topic establishes the field-level object of study before the chapter separates social cognition, social influence, groups, relationships, and social behavior into more specific canonical Topics.

## 2. Definition

Social psychology is the scientific study of how individuals' psychological processes and behavior are influenced by the presence, actions, expectations, characteristics, and representations of other people and by the social contexts in which interaction occurs.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components / Dimensions

Social psychology operates across several connected levels. At the individual level, it examines perception, attribution, attitudes, emotion, judgment, motivation, and behavior in social contexts. At the interpersonal level, it examines attraction, intimacy, trust, cooperation, conflict, helping, and rejection. At the group level, it examines norms, roles, status, cohesion, leadership, influence, collective decisions, and intergroup relations. These levels are analytically distinguishable but often causally connected.

The field therefore does not treat the individual and the social environment as separate systems. A person's interpretation of another person can alter behavior; that behavior can change the other's response; the resulting interaction can modify later expectations. Social psychological processes are consequently often reciprocal and temporally dynamic.

### Mechanism

Social psychological effects can arise through several mechanisms, including social information processing, learning, norm perception, identity processes, motivational responses, emotional reactions, and changes in perceived costs and benefits. The relevant mechanism depends on the phenomenon being studied. A person may conform because others provide useful information, because disagreement carries social costs, because a group identity is salient, or because several of these processes operate simultaneously.

### Moderators / Boundary Conditions

Social psychological effects are rarely universal in identical form. Their magnitude and direction can depend on context, relationship history, social identity, cultural norms, power, uncertainty, accountability, incentives, individual differences, and the behavior of other people. Findings obtained in one social setting should therefore not automatically be treated as invariant properties of human behavior.

### Evidence Status

Social psychology contains a large empirical literature using experiments, longitudinal studies, surveys, observational designs, field studies, and network methods. Effects can vary across populations and contexts, and some historically influential findings have generated replication or generalizability debates. A sound account therefore distinguishes robust mechanisms from effects that are conditional, heterogeneous, or actively debated.

### Levels of social explanation

A social psychological explanation should specify what level is being invoked. An individual's judgment may be explained by a cognitive process, by an interpersonal interaction, by a group norm, or by a broader social structure. These explanations can be compatible rather than competing. For example, a group norm can influence an individual's perception of what others expect, which changes a cognitive judgment and ultimately alters behavior.

### Dynamic social feedback

Social behavior frequently creates the conditions for subsequent social behavior. Expectations can influence actions that elicit confirming responses; rejection can change approach behavior; trust can facilitate cooperation that subsequently strengthens trust. Such feedback means that observed social outcomes may be both causes and consequences of earlier interaction patterns.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

Social psychology is the chapter-level umbrella Topic. The remaining Topics specify particular social cognitive processes, social identities, influence processes, group dynamics, relationships, and social behaviors. This Topic should therefore orient retrieval rather than duplicate every mechanism covered elsewhere.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns

The general field-level psychological concept of social psychology and its broad object of study.

### Defers

Specific cognitive, motivational, emotional, developmental, cultural, personality, clinical, and methodological constructs to their canonical owners. Specific social processes are developed in their dedicated Topics within this chapter.

## 6. Key Principles

- Social psychology examines psychological functioning in social context.
- Social effects can operate through cognitive, motivational, emotional, interpersonal, and group mechanisms.
- Individual and social processes can influence one another reciprocally.
- Context and boundary conditions matter.
- Field-level framing should not replace specific mechanism-level explanations.

### Topic-specific evidence layer

Evidence in social psychology is strongest when the proposed social mechanism is distinguished from the mere presence of another person. Social context can alter attention, interpretation, motivation, emotion, and behavior through different pathways, and the same observable outcome can therefore have different explanations. Experimental designs can isolate particular situational influences, while longitudinal and field research can reveal how social processes unfold beyond controlled settings. Replication and generalizability are especially important because social behavior is sensitive to context, relationship history, norms, and population characteristics. The field should therefore be represented as a collection of empirically testable mechanisms rather than a catalogue of universal social effects. The canonical role of this Topic is to provide that organizing frame while leaving detailed ownership to the specific social psychological processes that follow.

# 2. Social cognition

## 1. Purpose

Social cognition establishes the chapter's core framework for understanding how people acquire, represent, interpret, remember, and use information about other people, groups, relationships, and social situations.

## 2. Definition

Social cognition is the set of cognitive processes involved in perceiving, interpreting, representing, remembering, evaluating, and reasoning about social information.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components / Dimensions

Social cognition includes attention to socially relevant information, interpretation of ambiguous behavior, formation and updating of impressions, attribution of causes, categorization of people and groups, retrieval of social knowledge, evaluation of attitudes and intentions, and reasoning about social outcomes. These processes can operate deliberately or with limited conscious awareness.

Social cognition is not simply cognition with social content. The social world creates distinctive informational conditions. Other people are intentional agents whose behavior is often ambiguous, strategically variable, and responsive to the observer. Consequently, social cognition must frequently infer hidden states such as goals, beliefs, emotions, intentions, and group memberships from incomplete evidence.

### Mechanism

People use prior knowledge, expectations, contextual cues, observed behavior, and current goals to interpret social information. Processing can be rapid and relatively automatic, or slower and more deliberative. Initial interpretations may guide attention and memory, while later evidence can confirm, modify, or contradict them.

Social cognition also involves predictive processing. People do not encounter every social situation as a blank slate; expectations about roles, norms, identities, and likely behavior influence what is noticed and how ambiguous information is interpreted. These expectations can be useful because they reduce uncertainty, but they can also introduce systematic bias.

### Moderators / Boundary Conditions

Social cognitive processing varies with motivation, cognitive load, emotional state, expertise, familiarity, relationship closeness, uncertainty, accountability, and the diagnostic value of available information. A person may process information differently when accuracy is highly important than when speed or self-protection is more important.

### Evidence Status

Social cognition is supported by experimental, correlational, longitudinal, and computational research. However, specific effects differ in robustness and dependence on context. A general claim that social information processing is biased should not be interpreted as meaning that people are uniformly inaccurate. Heuristics can be efficient and often adaptive when environmental regularities make them informative.

### Updating and correction

Social cognition is dynamic. A first impression can persist because later information is interpreted through an existing framework, but people can also revise judgments when evidence is sufficiently diagnostic, inconsistent, repeated, or personally consequential. Correction itself requires cognitive resources and depends on what information is available.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

Social cognition is the organizing cognitive Topic for the chapter. Social perception, person perception, impression formation, attribution, attitudes, social comparison, self-categorization, and related Topics specify particular forms of social information processing.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns

General cognition specifically as it operates on social information and social representations.

### Defers

General memory, attention, reasoning, decision-making, emotion, motivation, and self-processes to their canonical owners when the question is not specifically social.

## 6. Key Principles

- Social cognition concerns the processing of information about people, groups, and social situations.
- Social information is often incomplete and ambiguous.
- Prior knowledge and expectations guide interpretation.
- Processing can be automatic, controlled, or interactive.
- Social judgments can be updated when evidence and processing conditions permit.

### Topic-specific evidence layer

A useful evidence distinction is between the existence of a social judgment and the process proposed to produce it. Behavioral studies can show differences in judgments or responses, but mechanism claims require designs that distinguish competing explanations. Time-course measures, manipulations of cognitive load, information availability, motivation, and accountability can help determine whether a process depends on rapid categorization, deliberate reasoning, or both. Social cognition should also be treated as adaptive and fallible rather than simply rational or irrational. A shortcut may produce systematic error in one environment while efficiently exploiting regularities in another. Evidence should therefore specify the task, information available, competing goals, and population before extending a finding to social judgment generally.

# 3. Social perception

## 1. Purpose

Social perception addresses how people detect and interpret information about other people and social situations, providing the perceptual foundation for later judgments, interactions, and behavior.

## 2. Definition

Social perception is the process of detecting, organizing, and interpreting socially relevant information from other people, their behavior, expressions, actions, and contextual cues.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components / Dimensions

Social perception includes perception of facial and bodily cues, emotional expressions, action patterns, interpersonal distance, gaze, status-related signals, and situational context. It also includes interpretation of behavior in relation to inferred goals and social meaning.

Perception is therefore not limited to sensory registration. The same physical behavior can be interpreted differently depending on context. A brief facial expression may indicate amusement, discomfort, politeness, uncertainty, or another state depending on the surrounding interaction.

### Mechanism

Social perception integrates observable cues with prior knowledge and contextual information. Perceivers use multiple cues rather than relying on a single signal in every situation. Cue integration can be efficient, but ambiguous or contradictory cues increase uncertainty.

Perception is also influenced by expectations. What a person expects to see can alter attention to particular cues and the interpretation of ambiguous behavior. This creates a bridge between perception and social cognition without making the two constructs identical.

### Moderators / Boundary Conditions

Accuracy depends on cue quality, familiarity, cultural context, relationship history, situational complexity, observer goals, and the degree to which the target's internal state is observable. Some social properties are directly signaled more clearly than others. Intentions and future behavior, for example, generally require inference beyond immediate perception.

### Evidence Status

Research uses behavioral judgments, reaction-time methods, eye-tracking, physiological measures, naturalistic observation, and experimental manipulation of cues and contexts. Findings support the importance of both person-level cues and situational information, while also showing that perception can be influenced by expectations and social categories.

### Perception and inference

A central boundary is the distinction between perceiving a cue and inferring what the cue means. Seeing a person move away is perceptual; concluding that the person dislikes the observer is an inference. These stages can become tightly linked in ordinary experience, but separating them is essential for scientific explanation.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

Social perception provides the perceptual entry point for person perception, impression formation, attribution, emotion-related social judgments, and interpersonal behavior.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns

The general process of perceiving socially meaningful cues and organizing them into socially relevant representations.

### Defers

General sensory perception to the relevant cognitive/biological owner and specific attribution, attitude, emotion, or identity processes to their dedicated Topics.

## 6. Key Principles

- Social perception involves both cue detection and contextual interpretation.
- Observable behavior does not uniquely determine internal states.
- Expectations can shape interpretation.
- Perceptual accuracy depends on cue quality and context.
- Perception and inference should be analytically distinguished.

### Topic-specific evidence layer

Evidence is strongest when studies manipulate or measure the cues available to observers rather than assuming that judgments directly reveal perceptual accuracy. Accuracy requires an appropriate criterion, and many social properties are only partially observable. Familiarity may improve knowledge of an individual without guaranteeing accurate interpretation in every context. Cultural learning can also influence which cues are attended to and how they are interpreted. Social perception should therefore be represented as an interaction among available signals, perceptual capacities, prior knowledge, and context rather than as a transparent reading of other people's internal states.

# 4. Person perception

## 1. Purpose

Person perception focuses specifically on how observers form representations and judgments about individual people, including their characteristics, intentions, abilities, traits, and likely behavior.

## 2. Definition

Person perception is the process of forming and updating psychological representations of another individual from observed behavior, appearance, contextual information, prior knowledge, and interpersonal experience.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components / Dimensions

Person perception can concern traits, temporary states, intentions, abilities, preferences, motives, trustworthiness, competence, and social roles. Some judgments concern relatively stable characteristics, while others concern momentary conditions.

The observer rarely has complete information. Person perception therefore involves inference from limited observations. A single action can provide information, but its meaning depends on situational constraints and alternative explanations.

### Mechanism

Observers combine behavioral evidence with prior expectations and contextual information. They may categorize a person, form an initial impression, attribute causes to behavior, and then update the representation as additional information arrives.

Information can receive different weight depending on its diagnostic value. Behavior that would be unlikely under competing explanations may provide stronger evidence than behavior that is common under many possible explanations.

### Moderators / Boundary Conditions

Judgments depend on familiarity, relationship closeness, observer goals, available information, accountability, time pressure, social categories, and the ambiguity of the target's behavior. The same observation can therefore support different judgments under different informational conditions.

### Evidence Status

Research on person perception includes laboratory experiments, naturalistic interaction studies, longitudinal relationship research, and studies of judgment accuracy. Evidence supports both systematic structure in person impressions and substantial context sensitivity. Accuracy is best assessed against meaningful behavioral criteria rather than against agreement alone.

### Accuracy and bias

Consensus among observers is not automatically equivalent to accuracy. Several people can agree because they share the same information, expectations, or bias. Conversely, disagreement does not necessarily mean that one observer is wrong if different observers have access to different behavioral samples.

Person perception can therefore contain useful information and systematic distortion at the same time.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

Person perception connects social perception with impression formation and attribution. It concerns the representation of an individual person, while social perception is broader and can include social situations and cues without requiring a stable person representation.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns

The general social-psychological process of perceiving and representing individual people.

### Defers

Specific attribution biases, stereotypes, attitudes, identity processes, and general personality constructs to their canonical Topics.

## 6. Key Principles

- Person perception involves inference from incomplete information.
- Stable traits and temporary states must be distinguished.
- Context changes the meaning of behavior.
- Agreement is not identical to accuracy.
- Person representations can be updated as evidence accumulates.

### Topic-specific evidence layer

Person-perception evidence benefits from distinguishing judgment consistency, interpersonal agreement, predictive accuracy, and criterion validity. A judgment may be consistent without being accurate, and a person may appear different across contexts without the observer necessarily making an error. Longitudinal behavioral sampling is especially useful because single episodes can be unrepresentative. Studies should also specify whether observers are judging observable behavior, inferred internal states, or predictions about future behavior. These are different evidentiary tasks and should not be collapsed into a single claim about whether an impression is “right.”

# 5. Impression formation

## 1. Purpose

Impression formation explains how observations and social information become an organized overall representation of another person, including judgments that integrate multiple cues.

## 2. Definition

Impression formation is the process by which people integrate information about an individual into an overall representation or evaluation of that person.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components / Dimensions

Impressions can include trait judgments, evaluations, emotional reactions, expectations, and predictions about future behavior. Information can be acquired sequentially, meaning that impressions may develop over time rather than appearing fully formed after one observation.

### Mechanism

Observers integrate multiple pieces of information using prior knowledge, contextual interpretation, and the perceived diagnostic value of each cue. Some information can disproportionately influence an impression when it changes the interpretation of other information. Other information may have limited impact when it is redundant or weakly diagnostic.

Impression formation also involves coherence. People often seek an understandable representation of another person rather than maintaining every observation as an unrelated fact. This can make the resulting representation efficient but can also produce assimilation of ambiguous evidence into an existing impression.

### Moderators / Boundary Conditions

Time pressure, cognitive load, motivation, familiarity, accountability, relationship goals, information order, and the diagnosticity of cues can affect impression formation. A rapid first impression may be useful for immediate interaction while remaining provisional and vulnerable to later revision.

### Evidence Status

Research supports the integration of multiple information sources but does not justify a single universal rule for how every impression is formed. Different tasks can recruit different processing strategies. Strong claims about information weighting should therefore be tied to the conditions under which the relevant evidence was obtained.

### Updating

New information can modify an impression when it is sufficiently diagnostic, repeated, credible, or consequential. Updating can be asymmetric when earlier beliefs shape how later evidence is interpreted. This does not mean impressions are immutable; rather, updating is itself a social cognitive process with conditions that affect its likelihood.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

Impression formation is a bridge between person perception and later judgment. It overlaps with attribution because explanations of behavior can influence impressions, but attribution specifically concerns causal interpretation whereas impression formation concerns the organization of information into an overall person representation.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns

The general process of integrating person information into an overall impression.

### Defers

Halo effect and horn effect to their dedicated Topics, attribution processes to the attribution cluster, stereotypes to the identity-and-groups cluster, and general personality judgment to its canonical owner.

## 6. Key Principles

- Impressions integrate multiple sources of person information.
- Information can differ in diagnostic value.
- Existing impressions can influence interpretation of later evidence.
- First impressions can be consequential without being permanently fixed.
- Impression formation is context-sensitive.

### Topic-specific evidence layer

Evidence on impression formation should distinguish immediate evaluation from durable representation. A rapid response may show that information affected an observer's judgment, but it does not by itself demonstrate that a stable person model was formed. Sequential-information paradigms are useful for examining updating, while criterion-based studies can examine whether impressions predict later behavior. Information-order effects should also be interpreted carefully because order can alter attention, interpretation, memory, or perceived diagnosticity. The most defensible account therefore treats impressions as structured but revisable representations whose formation depends on information, context, goals, and prior knowledge.
