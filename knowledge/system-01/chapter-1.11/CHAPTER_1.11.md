## 1.11.1 Language

## 1. Purpose
Establish language as the general human capacity and system for using structured symbols to represent and communicate meanings, while distinguishing language from speech, writing, and thought.

## 2. Definition
Language is a rule-governed system for representing and communicating meanings through structured symbols, including spoken, signed, and written forms.

## 3. Core Understanding
Language has multiple interacting levels. Phonological structure organizes speech sounds; morphology organizes meaningful word parts; syntax organizes combinations of words; semantics concerns encoded meaning; pragmatics concerns meaning in context and use. Language therefore cannot be reduced to vocabulary or speech alone. It can be externalized through speech, writing, or sign, and comprehension and production involve partially distinct but interacting processes. Human languages differ substantially in their sound systems, grammatical structures, lexicons, and conventions while sharing broad properties that allow speakers to generate and understand novel expressions. Language is closely related to memory, attention, executive control, perception, and social interaction, but these neighboring processes are not themselves identical to language. Psychological study therefore treats language both as a representational system and as a set of real-time cognitive processes.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Language is the organizing construct for the first major part of Chapter 1.11. The chapter then decomposes it into structure, processing, development, variation, and its relationships with cognition.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Language as a psychological and communicative system, including its defining organization and general operation.

### Defers
Phoneme, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, pragmatics, language processing, language development, language disorders, and related specialized constructs to their dedicated Topics in this chapter. Perception, memory, attention, executive control, and social cognition remain owned by their canonical chapters.

## 6. Key Principles
- Language is a structured symbolic system, not merely speech.
- Its components interact but are conceptually distinguishable.
- Language is learned and used within biological, cognitive, social, and cultural constraints.

## 1.11.2 Symbolic communication

## 1. Purpose
Explain how humans use conventional symbols to stand for objects, actions, relations, events, and abstract meanings.

## 2. Definition
Symbolic communication is the use of socially shared signs or symbols whose relationship to what they represent is established by convention, resemblance, or other learned mappings.

## 3. Core Understanding
A symbol allows communication to refer beyond the immediately present stimulus. Words can refer to absent objects, hypothetical events, abstract properties, and relationships, making communication substantially more flexible than simple signal-response systems. Symbolic communication depends on shared conventions: a symbol works because communicators have overlapping knowledge about what it means and how it should be used. Symbols can be arbitrary, as with many spoken words, or partly motivated by resemblance or iconic structure. Human symbolic systems can be nested and recombined, allowing people to construct novel messages rather than merely reproduce fixed signals. Symbolic communication is therefore related to language but is broader than any single language modality. Meaning is also shaped by context and communicative goals, so the same symbol can acquire different interpretations in different settings.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Symbolic communication provides the functional bridge between language as a system and the structural components used to encode and exchange meaning.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
The psychological principle of using conventional symbolic systems for communication.

### Defers
Formal language structure to phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics; general social cognition to Chapter 1.16; nonlinguistic symbolic thought to Chapter 1.9.

## 6. Key Principles
- Symbols depend on shared mappings between forms and meanings.
- Symbolic reference permits communication about absent and abstract content.
- Symbol use is broader than spoken language but language is a highly developed symbolic system.

## 1.11.3 Phoneme

## 1. Purpose
Define the smallest contrastive sound unit used to distinguish words within a particular language.

## 2. Definition
A phoneme is an abstract category of speech sound that functions to distinguish meaning between words in a language.

## 3. Core Understanding
A phoneme is not simply a single physical sound. Different spoken realizations can belong to the same phoneme category when they do not change lexical identity in that language. Such context-dependent realizations are often called allophones. Phonemic inventories differ across languages, so a sound contrast that is meaningful in one language may not function as a contrast in another. Speech perception requires listeners to map variable acoustic input onto relatively stable linguistic categories. This makes phonemes psychologically useful abstractions rather than direct copies of acoustic events. Phonemic structure supports lexical recognition because changing a phoneme can change the identity of a word. However, listeners also use surrounding context, lexical knowledge, and prosodic information when interpreting speech. Phoneme processing is therefore a component of speech and language perception rather than the whole of speech perception.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Phoneme establishes the sound-unit level of the chapter's language architecture and precedes phonology as the broader organization of sound patterns.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Phoneme as a contrastive linguistic sound category.

### Defers
Sound-system organization to Phonology; acoustic processing to Speech perception; word-level recognition to Word recognition.

## 6. Key Principles
- A phoneme is an abstract contrastive category, not merely an acoustic event.
- Phoneme inventories are language-specific.
- Context and linguistic knowledge influence how variable speech is categorized.

## 1.11.4 Phonology

## 1. Purpose
Explain how a language organizes and combines speech sounds into a structured sound system.

## 2. Definition
Phonology is the system of language-specific patterns and constraints governing the organization and combination of speech sounds.

## 3. Core Understanding
Phonology concerns the organization of sounds rather than their physical production alone. It includes which sound contrasts a language recognizes, which sequences are permitted, how sounds vary across contexts, and how sound patterns interact with linguistic structure. A language can contain many physically possible sounds while using only a subset as meaningful contrasts. Phonological knowledge allows speakers to recognize familiar sound patterns, produce forms that conform to language-specific constraints, and detect forms that sound unusual or impossible within the language. Phonology is partly abstract: speakers need not consciously know the rules, yet their behavior reflects sensitivity to them. Phonological organization also interacts with morphology and lexical knowledge, because pronunciation can change when words are combined or inflected. Phonology therefore forms one layer of language structure rather than a complete account of speech perception or production.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Phonology organizes the phonemic material introduced by Phoneme and provides the sound-system foundation for later lexical and sentence processing.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Language-specific organization of speech sounds and their allowable patterns.

### Defers
Individual sound categories to Phoneme; acoustic interpretation to Speech perception; word-level knowledge to Lexical knowledge.

## 6. Key Principles
- Phonology describes patterned organization, not just individual sounds.
- Phonological systems differ across languages.
- Speakers can possess detailed phonological knowledge without conscious access to its rules.

## 1.11.5 Morphology

## 1. Purpose
Explain how languages organize meaningful units inside words.

## 2. Definition
Morphology is the study and psychological organization of the smallest meaningful units of language and the rules by which they combine or change word forms.

## 3. Core Understanding
Morphemes may be free forms that can occur independently or bound forms that must attach to another unit. Morphological structure can encode grammatical information such as tense, number, case, or agreement, as well as lexical information that changes or extends a word's meaning. Languages differ greatly in how much information they express morphologically. Some use extensive inflection; others rely more heavily on word order, particles, or contextual interpretation. During comprehension and production, morphological structure can help people identify words, infer meaning, and construct grammatically appropriate forms. Morphological processing interacts with lexical knowledge because a complex word can be represented partly as a whole and partly through its constituent morphemes. The balance can depend on familiarity, transparency, frequency, and language-specific structure. Morphology therefore occupies an intermediate level between individual lexical items and larger syntactic constructions.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Morphology provides the word-internal structural layer connecting sound patterns with lexical and grammatical processing.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Meaningful internal structure of words and its linguistic organization.

### Defers
Sound organization to Phonology; word knowledge to Lexical knowledge; sentence-level combination to Syntax.

## 6. Key Principles
- Words can contain multiple meaningful units.
- Morphological organization differs substantially across languages.
- Morphological processing interacts with lexical and syntactic processing.

## 1.11.6 Syntax

## 1. Purpose
Explain how languages organize words and phrases into structured combinations.

## 2. Definition
Syntax is the system of rules and representations governing how words and phrases are structurally combined into sentences and larger grammatical expressions.

## 3. Core Understanding
Syntax determines structural relationships that cannot be recovered reliably from individual word meanings alone. Word order, agreement, constituency, dependency, and grammatical structure can signal who did what to whom, what modifies what, and how clauses relate. Languages differ in the mechanisms they use, so syntax cannot be reduced to one universal word-order pattern. During comprehension, people rapidly construct and revise structural interpretations as linguistic input unfolds. During production, speakers select words and organize them into structures that conform to the grammar of the language being used. Syntactic processing interacts with semantics and pragmatics: grammatical structure constrains interpretation, while meaning and context can influence how ambiguous structures are resolved. Syntax is therefore neither identical to sentence meaning nor independent of it. It is one component of a larger language-processing system.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Syntax establishes the sentence-structure layer between morphology and the meaning and use of sentences.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Structural organization of words and phrases into grammatical expressions.

### Defers
Word-internal structure to Morphology; encoded meaning to Semantics; context-dependent interpretation to Pragmatics.

## 6. Key Principles
- Syntax represents structural relationships among linguistic units.
- Structural interpretation unfolds incrementally during comprehension.
- Syntax interacts with semantic and pragmatic information without being reducible to either.

## 1.11.7 Semantics

## 1. Purpose
Explain how linguistic expressions encode and combine meaning.

## 2. Definition
Semantics is the system of meaning associated with linguistic expressions and the ways those meanings combine across words, phrases, and sentences.

## 3. Core Understanding
Semantics concerns meaning that is conventionally associated with linguistic forms, including lexical meanings and compositional meaning created by combining expressions. A sentence can therefore convey a structured proposition rather than a list of unrelated word meanings. Semantic interpretation depends partly on knowledge: people use stored lexical knowledge, conceptual knowledge, and grammatical structure to construct interpretations. Semantic ambiguity can arise when a word or expression has multiple possible meanings, requiring context or additional processing to select an interpretation. Semantics should be distinguished from pragmatics because communicators often convey more than the literal or conventionally encoded content of their words. The boundary is not absolute in every theoretical framework, but the distinction is useful for separating linguistic meaning from context-dependent communicative interpretation. Semantic processing also interacts with memory and perception without being reducible to either.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Semantics supplies the meaning layer needed for comprehension, production, discourse, reading, and writing.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Linguistically encoded and compositionally derived meaning.

### Defers
Contextual use and speaker meaning to Pragmatics; conceptual knowledge to Chapter 1.9 where appropriate.

## 6. Key Principles
- Meaning can be encoded at lexical and compositional levels.
- Semantic interpretation uses linguistic structure and stored knowledge.
- Semantic meaning and pragmatic implication are related but distinguishable.

## 1.11.8 Pragmatics

## 1. Purpose
Explain how people use context, intentions, social knowledge, and shared assumptions to interpret and produce language.

## 2. Definition
Pragmatics concerns how linguistic expressions are used and interpreted in context, including how speakers communicate meanings that are not fully specified by literal linguistic content.

## 3. Core Understanding
Pragmatic interpretation requires attention to who is speaking, to whom, under what circumstances, with what goals, and against what shared background. A speaker can communicate an implication, request, warning, or social signal without stating it directly. Listeners infer such meanings by combining linguistic content with contextual information and assumptions about communicative intentions. Pragmatics includes phenomena such as reference resolution, conversational implicature, indirect requests, turn-taking, and sensitivity to social norms. Pragmatic processing is therefore not simply an optional layer added after sentence comprehension; contextual information can influence interpretation as language unfolds. However, pragmatic inference is not unlimited: interpretations are constrained by linguistic form, available context, plausibility, and communicative conventions. Pragmatics overlaps with social cognition but retains a specific focus on language use and interpretation.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Pragmatics completes the core language-structure sequence by connecting linguistic form and meaning to actual communicative use.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Context-sensitive interpretation and use of language.

### Defers
General social cognition to Chapter 1.16; literal linguistic meaning to Semantics; sentence structure to Syntax.

## 6. Key Principles
- Language use depends on context and communicative intentions.
- People routinely infer meanings beyond literal linguistic content.
- Pragmatic interpretation is constrained by language, context, and social conventions.

## 1.11.9 Lexical knowledge

## 1. Purpose
Explain the stored knowledge people have about words and how that knowledge supports language use.

## 2. Definition
Lexical knowledge is the stored linguistic information associated with words, including their forms, meanings, grammatical properties, and relationships to other lexical items.

## 3. Core Understanding
Knowing a word involves more than knowing a dictionary definition. Lexical knowledge can include information about pronunciation, spelling, grammatical category, morphological structure, meaning, typical usage, frequency, and associations with other words. The mental lexicon is therefore better understood as a highly connected system than as a simple alphabetical list. Words differ in frequency, familiarity, ambiguity, concreteness, morphological complexity, and contextual predictability, and these properties can influence processing. Lexical knowledge supports both comprehension and production: listeners and readers use it to identify incoming forms, while speakers and writers retrieve it when constructing messages. Word knowledge also changes with experience. New words can be learned, existing representations can become more accessible, and contextual knowledge can become richer. Lexical knowledge is distinct from lexical access, which concerns the process of retrieving or activating stored information during real-time language use.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Lexical knowledge provides the stored word-level knowledge required by lexical access, recognition, comprehension, and production.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Stored knowledge associated with words.

### Defers
Real-time retrieval to Lexical access; visual identification to Word recognition; sentence-level meaning to Semantics.

## 6. Key Principles
- Word knowledge includes multiple kinds of linguistic information.
- Lexical representations are interconnected and experience-sensitive.
- Stored lexical knowledge is distinct from the process of accessing it.

## 1.11.10 Lexical access

## 1. Purpose
Explain how stored lexical information becomes activated and available during language processing.

## 2. Definition
Lexical access is the process by which a person activates or retrieves stored lexical information relevant to a perceived or intended word.

## 3. Core Understanding
Lexical access occurs during both comprehension and production. A listener may need to map an acoustic sequence onto a candidate word and activate its meaning and grammatical information. A speaker may begin with an intended concept and retrieve a suitable lexical form before articulating it. Access is influenced by variables such as frequency, repetition, contextual predictability, semantic priming, competition among alternatives, and the quality of available input. Access is therefore dynamic rather than a simple lookup operation. Multiple candidate words may become partially active before one interpretation or production choice is selected. Failures such as tip-of-the-tongue experiences illustrate that partial access to lexical information can occur without complete retrieval. Lexical access also interacts with phonological, semantic, morphological, attentional, and memory processes, but none of those processes is identical to lexical access itself.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Lexical access is the processing bridge between stored lexical knowledge and real-time word recognition, comprehension, and production.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Real-time activation and retrieval of lexical information.

### Defers
Stored word knowledge to Lexical knowledge; visual word identification to Word recognition; articulation planning to Speech production planning.

## 6. Key Principles
- Lexical access is an active retrieval process, not passive dictionary lookup.
- Multiple candidates can be activated during processing.
- Frequency, context, and competing candidates influence access.

## 1.11.11 Word recognition

## 1. Purpose
Explain how people identify written or spoken word forms as meaningful lexical items.

## 2. Definition
Word recognition is the process of identifying an incoming spoken or written form as a known word and activating its lexical representation.

## 3. Core Understanding
Word recognition requires mapping variable sensory input onto stable lexical representations. In reading, visual patterns must be processed sufficiently to distinguish words and activate their orthographic and lexical information. In spoken language, listeners must interpret rapidly changing acoustic signals despite variation in accent, speaking rate, pronunciation, and background noise. Recognition is strongly influenced by lexical frequency, context, prior knowledge, and similarity to competing words. A familiar word can often be recognized efficiently even when its physical form is degraded or partially obscured because higher-level information constrains interpretation. Word recognition therefore illustrates interaction between bottom-up input and stored knowledge. It should be distinguished from lexical access: recognition concerns identifying the word form, while lexical access concerns activating the broader information associated with that word.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Word recognition connects sensory language input with lexical knowledge and supports later comprehension.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Identification of incoming word forms as lexical items.

### Defers
Acoustic analysis to Speech perception; stored lexical information to Lexical knowledge; meaning construction to Language comprehension.

## 6. Key Principles
- Recognition maps variable input onto stable lexical representations.
- Frequency, context, and competition influence recognition speed and accuracy.
- Word recognition and lexical access are related but distinct operations.

## 1.11.12 Speech perception

## 1. Purpose
Explain how listeners convert complex acoustic signals into linguistically meaningful speech representations.

## 2. Definition
Speech perception is the process of interpreting acoustic speech signals as phonological, lexical, and ultimately meaningful linguistic information.

## 3. Core Understanding
Speech is a continuous acoustic signal containing overlapping information about multiple linguistic units. Listeners must segment this stream, identify likely sound categories, cope with speaker and environmental variation, and connect the resulting representations to words and meanings. Speech perception is therefore an active inferential process rather than a simple recording of sound. Context can influence how ambiguous or degraded input is interpreted, and listeners can adapt to unfamiliar speakers or accents through experience. Perception also interacts with phonological knowledge because languages differ in which sound distinctions are meaningful. At higher levels, lexical and semantic expectations can constrain interpretation of uncertain input. These interactions do not imply that speech perception is wholly top-down; sensory information remains essential. The psychological system integrates multiple information sources to achieve relatively stable linguistic interpretation from highly variable physical input.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Speech perception connects the chapter's sound structure with word recognition and language comprehension.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Psychological processing of spoken acoustic input into linguistic representations.

### Defers
General auditory perception to Chapter 1.4; phoneme categories to Phoneme; lexical identification to Word recognition.

## 6. Key Principles
- Speech is physically variable but perceived as structured linguistic information.
- Bottom-up acoustic information and linguistic knowledge interact.
- Speech perception adapts to speakers, accents, context, and environmental conditions.

## 1.11.13 Sentence processing

## 1. Purpose
Explain how people construct and revise interpretations of sentences as linguistic input unfolds.

## 2. Definition
Sentence processing is the real-time construction of structural and semantic interpretations for words combined into sentences.

## 3. Core Understanding
Sentence processing requires integrating lexical information, syntactic structure, semantic relationships, and contextual expectations. Because speech and reading unfold over time, people often begin interpreting a sentence before all relevant information has arrived. Temporary ambiguity can therefore lead to an initial interpretation that must later be revised. Processing is influenced by word frequency, structural expectations, plausibility, working-memory demands, and contextual information. Comprehension is not simply a final-stage activity: interpretation is continuously updated as new words arrive. Different theories disagree about the degree to which syntactic analysis initially operates independently of meaning and context, so no single processing architecture should be treated as settled fact. The most robust conclusion is that successful sentence comprehension requires coordinated use of linguistic structure and meaning under time and memory constraints.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Sentence processing is the central bridge from word-level language to larger-scale comprehension.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Real-time processing of sentence-level linguistic structure and meaning.

### Defers
General working memory to Chapter 1.8; syntax to Syntax; discourse-level integration to Discourse processing.

## 6. Key Principles
- Sentence interpretation unfolds incrementally.
- Ambiguity can require revision of an initial interpretation.
- Syntax, semantics, context, and memory demands interact during comprehension.

## 1.11.14 Discourse processing

## 1. Purpose
Explain how people construct coherent interpretations across multiple sentences and communicative turns.

## 2. Definition
Discourse processing is the integration of linguistic and contextual information across sentences or conversational contributions to construct a coherent representation of the larger message.

## 3. Core Understanding
Discourse comprehension requires more than understanding individual sentences. Readers and listeners track references, causal and temporal relationships, topic structure, speaker intentions, and information that may be distributed across many statements. They build a situation model that connects new information with prior discourse and relevant knowledge. Coherence can be explicit, as when a causal relation is stated, or inferred when the relationship is left unstated. Ambiguous references such as pronouns often require discourse-level information to resolve. Discourse processing is also sensitive to genre, conversational goals, and expectations. Because discourse extends over time, working memory and memory retrieval contribute to maintaining and integrating earlier information, but discourse processing itself is the higher-level organization of that information into a coherent communicative representation.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Discourse processing extends sentence-level processing into connected language and provides the foundation for reading and extended comprehension.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Integration and interpretation of connected linguistic material across sentences or turns.

### Defers
General memory to Chapter 1.8; social cognition to Chapter 1.16; sentence structure to Sentence processing.

## 6. Key Principles
- Discourse comprehension requires integration across linguistic units.
- Coherence often depends on information that is distributed or inferred.
- Memory and world knowledge support discourse processing without becoming the construct itself.

## 1.11.15 Language comprehension

## 1. Purpose
Explain how people derive linguistic meaning from spoken, signed, or written input.

## 2. Definition
Language comprehension is the coordinated process of constructing meaningful interpretations from linguistic input.

## 3. Core Understanding
Comprehension involves multiple stages and interacting sources of information. Perceptual input must be identified as linguistic units; lexical and grammatical information must be activated; meanings must be combined; and context must be used to resolve ambiguity and construct a coherent interpretation. Comprehension can operate rapidly and often automatically, but it is not infallible. Ambiguous, degraded, unfamiliar, or contradictory input can increase processing demands and produce misunderstanding. Comprehension also extends beyond literal word meaning because listeners and readers infer relationships, intentions, references, and implications from context. It is therefore better viewed as active construction than passive reception. Language comprehension draws on memory, attention, knowledge, and executive control, but these are supporting processes rather than alternate names for comprehension.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Language comprehension integrates the chapter's sound, word, sentence, and discourse components into the overall process of understanding language.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Coordinated understanding of linguistic input across modalities and levels.

### Defers
Specific subcomponents to their dedicated Topics; general memory, attention, and executive control to their canonical chapters.

## 6. Key Principles
- Comprehension is an active construction of meaning.
- It integrates information across multiple linguistic levels.
- Context and prior knowledge can improve or distort interpretation.

## 1.11.16 Language production

## 1. Purpose
Explain how people transform intended meanings into communicative linguistic output.

## 2. Definition
Language production is the process of formulating intended content into linguistic structures and preparing them for spoken, signed, or written expression.

## 3. Core Understanding
Production begins with an intended message and requires selection of appropriate words, grammatical structures, and modality-specific forms. The speaker or writer must organize content, retrieve lexical information, construct larger structures, and prepare output for articulation or inscription. Production is incremental: people often formulate upcoming material while producing earlier material. Errors such as substitutions, omissions, and blends provide evidence that planning occurs at multiple levels and can involve competition among alternatives. Production is constrained by knowledge, context, communicative goals, and the properties of the language being used. Although comprehension and production share representations, they are not mirror-image processes. Production places particular demands on planning, selection, sequencing, and monitoring.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Language production is the complementary process to comprehension and provides the umbrella for speech production planning and writing.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
General formulation of linguistic output from intended content.

### Defers
Speech-specific planning to Speech production planning; written output to Writing; lexical retrieval to Lexical access.

## 6. Key Principles
- Production transforms intended content into structured linguistic output.
- It involves multiple planning levels and continuous monitoring.
- Production and comprehension share information but impose different processing demands.

## 1.11.17 Speech production planning

## 1. Purpose
Explain the cognitive planning processes required to turn intended linguistic content into coordinated speech.

## 2. Definition
Speech production planning is the preparation of linguistic and motor information required to produce spoken language.

## 3. Core Understanding
Speech production requires coordination across several levels: intended message, lexical selection, grammatical formulation, phonological encoding, and preparation of articulatory movements. Planning is partly incremental, allowing speakers to begin speaking before the entire utterance has been fully specified. This flexibility supports fluent conversation but also creates opportunities for slips, pauses, repetitions, and self-corrections. Speakers monitor their own output and can detect some errors while producing or shortly after producing them. Planning demands vary with message complexity, lexical difficulty, unfamiliar words, and conversational conditions. Speech production therefore cannot be reduced to motor articulation; linguistic planning must precede and interact with the motor system. At the same time, planning and articulation are distinct levels of the overall production system.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
This Topic specializes Language production for the spoken modality and links linguistic formulation with speech output.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Cognitive planning of spoken linguistic output.

### Defers
General motor control to Chapter 1.3; lexical retrieval to Lexical access; general language production to Language production.

## 6. Key Principles
- Speech production coordinates linguistic and articulatory planning.
- Planning is incremental rather than necessarily complete before speaking begins.
- Errors reveal interactions among planning levels but do not map one-to-one onto single mechanisms.

## 1.11.18 Reading

## 1. Purpose
Explain reading as a psychological process that converts visual symbols into linguistic and meaningful representations.

## 2. Definition
Reading is the process of interpreting written symbols to identify linguistic forms and construct meaning.

## 3. Core Understanding
Reading requires coordination of visual analysis, orthographic pattern recognition, word recognition, language processing, and comprehension. Skilled reading becomes highly efficient because familiar written patterns can activate lexical representations rapidly, while less familiar material requires more deliberate processing. Reading can be described at several levels, from identifying letters and word forms to integrating sentences and building a coherent representation of a text. Background knowledge, vocabulary, attention, and working-memory demands affect performance, but reading is not reducible to any one of these factors. Different writing systems place different demands on the mapping between visual symbols and language. Reading development also changes the relative contribution of decoding and higher-level comprehension processes as skill increases.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Reading is the principal written-language process linking visual word recognition with language comprehension.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Psychological process of interpreting written language.

### Defers
General visual perception to Chapter 1.4; word recognition to Word recognition; comprehension to Language comprehension; writing to Writing.

## 6. Key Principles
- Reading integrates visual, lexical, linguistic, and conceptual processes.
- Skilled reading depends on efficient recognition and language comprehension.
- Writing-system characteristics influence how written forms map onto language.

## 1.11.19 Reading comprehension

## 1. Purpose
Explain how readers construct meaningful and coherent representations from written text.

## 2. Definition
Reading comprehension is the process of constructing an integrated interpretation of written language using text information, prior knowledge, and contextual inference.

## 3. Core Understanding
Reading comprehension requires more than decoding words correctly. Readers must combine sentence meanings, track references and relationships, infer unstated information, monitor coherence, and connect the text with relevant knowledge. A reader can therefore recognize every word yet misunderstand the larger message. Comprehension depends partly on vocabulary and background knowledge, but also on monitoring, attention, memory, and the ability to construct a coherent situation model. Text structure and reader goals influence what information receives emphasis and how deeply it is processed. Difficult texts can expose limits in working memory, knowledge, or monitoring, although poor comprehension is not explained by one universal bottleneck. Reading comprehension is thus an emergent outcome of coordinated language and cognitive processes rather than a simple extension of word recognition.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Reading comprehension is the higher-level meaning-building process that follows and integrates written-language recognition.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Construction of meaning from written text at sentence, paragraph, and discourse levels.

### Defers
Word identification to Word recognition; general discourse processing to Discourse processing; memory and attention to their canonical Topics.

## 6. Key Principles
- Accurate word recognition is necessary but not sufficient for comprehension.
- Comprehension depends on integration, inference, knowledge, and monitoring.
- Text and reader characteristics jointly shape understanding.

## 1.11.20 Writing

## 1. Purpose
Explain how people formulate and externalize ideas through written language.

## 2. Definition
Writing is the process of transforming intended content into organized written linguistic symbols that can communicate meaning across time and distance.

## 3. Core Understanding
Writing requires planning content, selecting linguistic forms, organizing discourse, generating written symbols, and monitoring the emerging text. Unlike conversation, writing often permits more deliberate revision and can therefore involve cycles of planning, drafting, evaluating, and revising. Writers must manage both local choices, such as words and sentences, and global structure, such as argument, narrative coherence, and organization. Writing skill depends on language knowledge, topic knowledge, executive control, practice, and the demands of the task. Mechanical transcription and composition are related but distinguishable: fluent handwriting or typing does not guarantee coherent writing, and strong ideas can be constrained by weak transcription or language skills. Writing therefore represents a coordinated production system rather than a direct transfer of thought onto paper.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Writing completes the core language-production architecture by specifying the written modality.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Psychological processes involved in composing and producing written language.

### Defers
General language production to Language production; reading to Reading; executive control to Chapter 1.9.

## 6. Key Principles
- Writing converts intended content into persistent linguistic form.
- Planning, transcription, organization, and revision interact.
- Writing quality reflects both language knowledge and task-specific cognitive control.

## 1.11.21 Language acquisition

## 1. Purpose
Explain how people acquire the knowledge and abilities needed to understand and use a language.

## 2. Definition
Language acquisition is the development of knowledge and abilities that allow a person to comprehend and produce a language.

## 3. Core Understanding
Language acquisition includes learning sound patterns, vocabulary, morphology, syntax, meaning, and pragmatic conventions. In typical development, children acquire extensive language knowledge without receiving explicit instruction for every rule they use, indicating that learning is supported by powerful interactions among biological capacities, perceptual abilities, social experience, statistical regularities, and communicative interaction. Acquisition is not a single event but a changing process across development. Input matters, but children are not simply copying adult sentences; they detect regularities, form generalizations, and adapt to communicative contexts. Developmental outcomes vary with the quantity and quality of input, social environment, hearing and neurological status, opportunities for interaction, and individual differences. Competing theories emphasize different mechanisms, and no single explanation accounts for every aspect of acquisition.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Language acquisition introduces the developmental process underlying the mature language structures and abilities described earlier.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
General acquisition of language knowledge and use.

### Defers

## 6. Key Principles

## 1.11.23 Vocabulary development

## 1. Purpose
Explain how knowledge of words grows and becomes increasingly organized across development.

## 2. Definition
Vocabulary development is the acquisition and refinement of knowledge about word forms, meanings, grammatical properties, and relations among words.

## 3. Core Understanding

Early vocabulary growth is influenced by exposure, interaction, attention to communicative signals, and the ability to map words to referents or concepts. Later growth becomes increasingly dependent on reading, formal education, specialized experience, and inferential learning from context. Word meanings are also refined over time as people encounter words across varied situations. Vocabulary is therefore dynamic rather than a fixed store. Differences in vocabulary can affect language comprehension because known words provide the raw material for constructing meaning, but vocabulary size alone does not fully determine broader language ability.

## 1.11.24 Grammar development

The rate and pathway of development vary across languages because languages distribute grammatical information differently. Input frequency, communicative demands, cognitive development, and learning mechanisms all contribute. Grammar development should therefore not be equated with memorizing rules. Describe the relationship between language and thinking without collapsing either construct into the other.

## 1.11.29 Linguistic relativity

## 1. Purpose
Explain the hypothesis that characteristics of a language can influence habitual patterns of perception, categorization, memory, or thought.

## 2. Definition
Linguistic relativity is the view that differences among languages can contribute to differences in cognitive processing or habitual patterns of thought.

## 3. Core Understanding
Linguistic relativity is broader than the strong claim that language determines what people can think. Research has examined whether grammatical categories, lexical distinctions, spatial terms, number systems, color terms, and other linguistic features influence attention, categorization, memory, or reasoning. Some studies report language-linked differences, while the size, durability, generality, and causal interpretation of such effects vary. Learning a linguistic distinction can make that distinction more accessible in tasks that use or require it, but this does not demonstrate that speakers of another language are incapable of representing the same concept. Effects may also reflect cultural practices, task demands, bilingual experience, or differences in learned attention. Linguistic relativity is therefore best treated as an empirical research program with specific hypotheses rather than as a universal deterministic law.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns

### Defers

## 6. Key Principles
- Linguistic relativity does not require linguistic determinism.
- Reported effects are often specific to tasks and linguistic distinctions.
- Language, culture, and cognition must be separated when causal claims are made.

## 1.11.30 Sensitive period in language

## 2. Definition

Different language components can show different developmental trajectories, and later learners can achieve high levels of proficiency under favorable conditions. Evidence from first-language deprivation, second-language learning, and developmental neuroscience informs the question but does not reduce it to one simple critical-age rule. ## 1.11.35 Aphasia

Explain acquired language impairment associated with neurological damage.

## 1.11.36 Intelligence

## 1.11.37 General intelligence

## 1. Purpose

## 2. Definition

## 1.11.38 g factor

## 1. Purpose
Explain the statistical factor commonly used to represent general cognitive ability.

## 2. Definition
The g factor is a statistical construct representing common variance among measures of cognitive ability.

## 3. Core Understanding
The g factor emerged from factor-analytic work showing that diverse mental ability tests tend to correlate positively. Depending on the model, g can appear as a general factor above more specific abilities or as a broad factor within a hierarchical structure. g is therefore a statistical representation of covariance, not direct proof of one biological mechanism. Researchers disagree about what psychological process, if any, should be considered the single causal basis of g. Some theories interpret it as broad efficiency or capacity; others emphasize sampling of cognitive processes, neural properties, or developmental mechanisms. The distinction between g as a statistical factor and intelligence as a broader psychological construct is important. A high loading on g means that a measure shares variance with the general factor; it does not mean the measure is caused entirely by one unitary mental process.

Processing speed is the efficiency with which a person can perform basic cognitive operations accurately under specified time constraints.

## 3. Core Understanding
Processing-speed measures typically involve rapid discrimination, identification, comparison, or simple responding rather than complex reasoning. Performance can be influenced by perceptual efficiency, attention, motor speed, familiarity, and decision demands, so the construct should not be interpreted as a pure measure of one biological rate. Processing speed often correlates with broader cognitive ability and can matter for tasks that require maintaining information while rapidly transforming or responding to it. Slower performance can therefore affect higher-level tasks without implying that the underlying reasoning capacity is absent. Processing speed also changes with age, fatigue, health, and task conditions. Its interpretation is strongest when the exact task and response demands are considered rather than treating speed as a universal measure of mental quickness.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Processing speed is a differentiated cognitive ability within the intelligence architecture.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Speed of relatively basic cognitive operations under defined task conditions.

### Defers
General attention to Chapter 1.6; motor speed to Chapter 1.3; broad intelligence to Intelligence.

## 6. Key Principles
- Processing speed concerns efficiency under specified task demands.
- It is influenced by multiple cognitive and motor factors.
- Speed and reasoning are related but not interchangeable.

## 1.11.45 Working memory and intelligence

## 1. Purpose
Describe the relationship between temporary information management and broader cognitive ability.

## 2. Definition
Working memory and intelligence refers to the relationship between maintaining and manipulating information over short periods and performance on broader cognitive ability measures.

## 3. Core Understanding
Working memory supports tasks that require information to remain accessible while it is transformed, compared, or integrated with new information. Many complex reasoning tasks place demands on this capacity, which helps explain why working-memory measures often correlate with intelligence measures. The relationship should not be interpreted as proof that working memory is identical to intelligence or that one simple capacity fully causes general intelligence. Working memory itself contains multiple components and is affected by attention, interference, strategy, and task structure. Some intelligence models treat working memory as a specific ability; others place it within broader hierarchical structures. The strongest interpretation is relational: working memory provides cognitive resources relevant to many demanding tasks, while intelligence reflects broader patterns of performance across many kinds of tasks.

## 1.11.46 Practical intelligence

## 1. Purpose
Describe intelligence as effective adaptation to everyday and real-world demands.

## 2. Definition
Practical intelligence refers to the ability to use knowledge and reasoning effectively in everyday situations, including adapting to, shaping, or selecting environments.

## 3. Core Understanding
Practical intelligence emphasizes successful functioning in contexts that may not resemble formal academic tests. It can involve recognizing relevant information, applying experience, dealing with people and constraints, and choosing workable solutions. The concept has been associated with theories that distinguish practical performance from analytic test performance, but its exact boundaries and incremental validity beyond established cognitive abilities remain debated. Everyday success is also influenced by personality, motivation, social resources, education, opportunity, and environmental structure. Practical intelligence should therefore not be treated as a hidden universal ability that explains all real-world success. Its value lies in highlighting that intelligent behavior is expressed in context and that standardized tests capture only some forms of performance.

## 1.11.47 Multiple intelligences theory

## 1. Purpose
Describe the theory proposing multiple relatively distinct forms of human intellectual ability.

## 2. Definition
Multiple intelligences theory is a framework proposing that human intellectual competence consists of several distinct forms of ability rather than one general intelligence alone.

## 3. Core Understanding
The theory is most associated with Howard Gardner and proposes forms such as linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, and intrapersonal intelligence, with later discussion of additional candidates. The framework broadened public discussion about human abilities, but its categories do not map straightforwardly onto the psychometric structure established by conventional intelligence research. Evidence for completely independent intelligences is substantially weaker than the evidence that cognitive abilities are correlated. The theory therefore should be presented as an influential conceptual framework rather than as an established replacement for general intelligence models. Its educational popularity does not itself establish its measurement validity. Some of its distinctions may capture meaningful talents or competencies even when they do not constitute independent psychometric intelligences.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
This Topic records a major theory that challenges unitary conceptions of intelligence and illustrates the distinction between theory and established measurement structure.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Multiple intelligences theory as a theoretical model.

### Defers
General intelligence and psychometric evidence to General intelligence, g factor, and Hierarchical intelligence models.

## 6. Key Principles
- The theory broadens the concept of intellectual competence.
- Its proposed intelligences should not be assumed to be psychometrically independent.
- Influence is not equivalent to empirical confirmation.

## 1.11.49 Intelligence testing

## 1. Purpose
Explain how intelligence-related performance is systematically measured and interpreted.

## 2. Definition
Intelligence testing is the standardized assessment of cognitive abilities using tasks designed to estimate specific and broader dimensions of intellectual functioning.

## 3. Core Understanding
Modern intelligence tests typically contain multiple tasks designed to sample different cognitive abilities and may yield both subscale and composite scores. Standardized administration and scoring allow an individual's performance to be compared with an appropriate normative reference group. Test interpretation requires attention to the purpose of assessment, the construct being measured, reliability, validity, normative sample, language, culture, and testing conditions. A score is not a complete description of a person and should not be interpreted without its measurement error and contextual limitations. Intelligence testing can be used in educational, clinical, research, and occupational contexts, but the appropriateness of an interpretation depends on the test and decision being made. The strongest use of testing is therefore evidence-based and context-specific rather than score absolutism.

## 1.11.50 IQ

## 1. Purpose
Explain IQ as a standardized score used to summarize performance on particular intelligence tests.

## 2. Definition
IQ is a standardized score, conventionally centered near 100 in a norm-referenced population, used to express relative performance on an intelligence assessment.

## 3. Core Understanding
Modern IQ scores are generally derived by comparing performance with a norm group rather than by directly measuring a fixed quantity of mental power. A score therefore depends on the test, normative sample, scoring model, age group, and administration conditions. IQ can summarize broad cognitive performance when supported by a well-designed test, but it is not interchangeable with every concept of intelligence. A single score also contains measurement error and can conceal meaningful variation across cognitive domains. Interpretation must consider confidence intervals, test validity, cultural and linguistic context, and the purpose of assessment. IQ scores are useful because standardized scaling makes comparison possible, but the apparent precision of a number should not be mistaken for perfect measurement.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
IQ is the principal standardized score representation used in many intelligence assessments.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Interpretation and conceptual meaning of IQ as a standardized score.

### Defers
Test construction to Intelligence testing; reliability and validity to their dedicated Topics.

## 6. Key Principles
- IQ is a standardized relative score, not a direct physical quantity.
- Scores contain measurement error and depend on norms.
- A single IQ score does not exhaustively describe cognitive functioning.

## 1.11.51 Standardization

## 1. Purpose
Explain how intelligence tests are administered and interpreted against consistent procedures and reference populations.

## 2. Definition
Standardization is the process of establishing consistent test administration, scoring, and normative reference procedures so that scores can be interpreted comparably.

## 3. Core Understanding
Standardization has two closely related components. First, administration and scoring procedures are made sufficiently consistent to reduce irrelevant variation. Second, performance is interpreted using data from a defined normative population. Norms can include age-based or other appropriate reference distributions. A standardized score is meaningful only relative to the population and procedures used to create it. Changes in population performance over time can therefore make old norms less appropriate, which is one reason tests require periodic restandardization. Standardization does not eliminate all measurement error or cultural and contextual influences. It also does not guarantee validity: a perfectly standardized test can still measure the wrong construct. Standardization is therefore a foundation for comparability, not a substitute for evidence that the interpretation is valid.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Standardization supports the interpretability of IQ and other intelligence-test scores.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Consistent administration, scoring, and normative reference procedures for intelligence assessment.

### Defers
General measurement theory to Chapter 1.2; test validity to Validity of intelligence tests.

## 6. Key Principles
- Standardization supports comparability.
- Norms are population- and time-dependent.
- Standardization alone does not establish validity.

## 1.11.52 Reliability of intelligence tests

## 1. Purpose
Explain the consistency and measurement precision of intelligence-test scores.

## 2. Definition
Reliability of intelligence tests is the degree to which scores are consistent and sufficiently precise under specified conditions of measurement.

## 3. Core Understanding
Reliability can be examined through different forms, including internal consistency, test–retest stability, inter-rater agreement where relevant, and measurement-error estimates. High reliability means that observed scores are relatively stable or consistent under the conditions being evaluated; it does not mean that the test measures the intended construct. A test can be highly reliable yet invalid if it consistently measures something other than the intended ability. Reliability also depends on the score and context: a composite score can be more reliable than an individual subtest, and short tests may be less precise. Reliability should therefore be interpreted alongside standard errors of measurement and validity evidence. For individual decisions, uncertainty around a score is especially important because small score differences may reflect measurement error rather than real differences.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Reliability determines how much confidence can be placed in the consistency of intelligence-test scores.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Reliability and precision of intelligence-test measurement.

### Defers
General reliability theory to Chapter 1.2; validity to Validity of intelligence tests.

## 6. Key Principles
- Reliability concerns consistency and precision, not truth.
- Different reliability forms answer different questions.
- Individual score interpretation must account for measurement error.

## 1.11.53 Validity of intelligence tests

## 1. Purpose
Explain whether interpretations and uses of intelligence-test scores are supported by evidence.

## 2. Definition
Validity of intelligence tests concerns the degree to which evidence supports the intended interpretation and use of scores for a specified purpose and population.

## 3. Core Understanding
Validity is not a property that a test permanently possesses independent of use. Evidence can address content, internal structure, relations with other measures, predictive performance, and consequences of interpretation. A test may be useful for one purpose and inappropriate for another. Validity also depends on the population and context: evidence from one group does not automatically generalize to another. Intelligence-test validity is therefore stronger when multiple sources of evidence converge and when alternative explanations have been considered. Predictive correlation alone does not establish that the measured ability causes the outcome. Likewise, a high correlation with another test does not prove that the two tests measure exactly the same construct. Validity is consequently an evidence-based argument about score interpretation rather than a simple yes/no label.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Validity provides the central scientific safeguard for interpreting intelligence-test scores.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Validity of intelligence-test score interpretations and uses.

### Defers
General validity theory to Chapter 1.2; reliability to Reliability of intelligence tests.

## 6. Key Principles
- Validity concerns interpretation and use, not the test in isolation.
- Multiple evidence sources are needed.
- Validity claims are population- and purpose-dependent.

## 1.11.54 Intelligence and heredity

## 1. Purpose
Explain the evidence for genetic contributions to individual differences in intelligence without confusing heritability with immutability.

## 2. Definition
Intelligence and heredity refers to the relationship between genetic variation and individual differences in measured cognitive ability.

## 3. Core Understanding
Behavior-genetic research indicates that genetic differences contribute to variation in cognitive ability, while environmental conditions also contribute. Heritability is a population statistic describing the proportion of variation associated with genetic differences in a particular population and environment; it does not describe the percentage of an individual's intelligence that is genetic. High heritability within one environment does not imply that an ability cannot change or that environmental differences are unimportant. Genetic effects can also depend on environmental conditions, and environments can influence the expression of genetic differences. Heritability estimates can change across populations and developmental periods. The scientifically appropriate conclusion is therefore neither genetic determinism nor environmental exclusivity: cognitive development reflects complex interactions among genetic variation, environments, development, and measurement.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
This Topic examines the hereditary contribution to intelligence while preserving the distinction between statistical heritability and individual causation.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Relationship between genetic variation and individual differences in intelligence.

### Defers
General behavioral-genetic methods to Chapter 1.3 and evidence methodology to Chapter 1.2.

## 6. Key Principles
- Heritability is a population statistic, not an individual genetic percentage.
- Heritable traits can change with environment and development.
- Genetic and environmental influences can interact.

## 1.11.55 Intelligence and environment

## 1. Purpose
Explain how environmental conditions contribute to the development and expression of cognitive ability.

## 2. Definition
Intelligence and environment refers to the influence of environmental conditions and experiences on cognitive development and measured intellectual performance.

## 3. Core Understanding
Environmental influences can include prenatal conditions, nutrition, health, family resources, education, exposure to cognitively demanding activities, stress, neighborhood conditions, and broader social opportunities. Their effects are not uniform: timing, duration, quality, and individual differences matter. Environmental effects can also interact with genetic differences, making simple genetic-versus-environmental partitions inadequate for explaining individual development. Improvements in average cognitive-test performance across historical cohorts provide one illustration that population-level cognitive outcomes can change substantially over time, although the causes of such changes are complex. Environmental influence also does not mean every intervention will produce large or permanent gains in every cognitive measure. The strongest interpretation is that cognitive development is environmentally responsive within biological and developmental constraints.

## 1.11.58 Flynn effect

## 1. Purpose
Explain the observed historical rise in average scores on many standardized intelligence tests across successive cohorts.

## 2. Definition
The Flynn effect is the observed increase in average scores on standardized intelligence tests across generations or successive normative cohorts.

## 3. Core Understanding
The Flynn effect is a population-level measurement phenomenon: average scores on many intelligence tests have risen across successive cohorts in numerous settings, although the size and pattern vary by test, country, period, and ability level. A major meta-analysis found substantial average gains across hundreds of comparisons, while also finding meaningful heterogeneity. The effect does not mean that a person's intelligence automatically increases simply because a newer test is used. Nor does it identify a single cause. Proposed explanations include education, health, nutrition, environmental complexity, changes in test-taking and measurement, and other social or biological factors. Because IQ tests are normed to population distributions, older norms can become inappropriate over time, which is why restandardization matters. ## 1.11.60 Individual differences in intelligence

## 1. Purpose
Explain why people differ in measured cognitive ability while avoiding reduction of those differences to a single cause.

## 2. Definition
Individual differences in intelligence are relatively stable but developmentally modifiable differences among people in cognitive abilities and patterns of cognitive performance.

## 3. Core Understanding
People differ in general cognitive performance and in the relative strengths of particular abilities. These differences arise from interacting genetic, developmental, educational, health, social, cultural, and experiential factors. Stability is therefore not the same as immutability: cognitive performance can show continuity while also changing with development, learning, illness, environmental opportunity, and aging. Measurement adds another layer because observed scores contain error and depend on the tasks and norms used. Individual differences should also be interpreted within populations rather than as fixed rankings of human worth. A person's cognitive profile can be uneven, and strengths in one domain do not necessarily imply strengths in every domain. The scientifically appropriate approach is to describe the measured ability, population, context, and uncertainty rather than treating intelligence as a single permanent essence.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
This Topic closes the intelligence architecture by integrating variation across general and specific abilities.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns

### Defers

## 6. Key Principles
- Intelligence varies across people in both general level and cognitive profile.
- Individual differences reflect interacting biological and environmental influences.
- Stability does not mean immutability, and test scores are not measures of human worth.
