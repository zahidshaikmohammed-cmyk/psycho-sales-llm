# 1.3.1 Human Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Human Thinking is the chapter-level foundation for understanding how people mentally represent, transform, evaluate, connect, and use information. It provides the broad construct within which the more specific processes in this chapter are located.

## 2. Definition
Human thinking is the set of psychological processes through which a person mentally represents information and transforms those representations to understand, interpret, reason about, evaluate, imagine, plan, solve problems, make decisions, and guide action.

**What it is not:** Thinking is broader than any single process such as reasoning, decision-making, or problem solving. Those are particular forms or functions of thinking.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components / Dimensions
Thinking can involve representation, attention to relevant information, comparison, categorization, inference, evaluation, simulation, reasoning, decision-making, and generation of new possibilities. These processes can operate sequentially, recursively, or simultaneously.

Thinking may concern current information, remembered information, imagined situations, hypothetical possibilities, or combinations of these.

### Mechanism
Thinking depends on mental representations and operations performed on them. A person may retrieve information, combine representations, detect relationships, evaluate implications, simulate possible states, or select among alternatives. The resulting thought can then influence further processing or behavior.

### Forms
Thinking may be relatively automatic or deliberate, intuitive or analytical, concrete or abstract, convergent or generative, and more or less effortful. These distinctions describe processing characteristics rather than permanently separate kinds of people.

### Moderators / Boundary Conditions
Thinking is affected by available information, knowledge, attention, goals, memory, time pressure, cognitive capacity, emotional and motivational states, environmental structure, and the quality of representations being used. A person can reason well in one context and poorly in another without possessing a single globally fixed level of thinking ability.

### Evidence Status
Thinking is a broad descriptive construct supported by extensive research across cognitive psychology and related disciplines. Specific claims about particular mechanisms require evidence appropriate to the mechanism being proposed.

### Misconceptions
Thinking is not synonymous with conscious verbal reasoning. People can think through imagery, nonverbal representations, learned procedures, intuitive processing, and other forms of cognition.

### Related Concepts
Reasoning is a subset of thinking concerned with drawing or evaluating conclusions. Decision-making concerns selecting among possibilities. Problem solving concerns transforming an initial problem state toward a desired state. Mental representation provides the informational substrate on which many thinking processes operate.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Human Thinking is the broad organizing concept for Chapter 1.3. The remaining topics specify important forms, operations, outputs, constraints, and theories of thinking.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The general psychological construct of human thinking and its broad functional scope.

### Defers
Specific mechanisms and constructs are owned by the corresponding topics in this chapter, including reasoning, judgment, decision-making, problem solving, mental simulation, and the theories in Topics 1.3.67–1.3.70.

## 6. Key Principles
- Thinking involves mental representation and transformation.
- Thinking includes more than conscious verbal reasoning.
- Specific cognitive processes are forms or components of broader thinking.
- Thinking is context-sensitive rather than reducible to a single fixed mode.

# 1.3.2 Thought

## 1. Purpose
This Topic defines the immediate mental content or episode that can be described as a thought, distinguishing the product or event from the broader processes that generate it.

## 2. Definition
A thought is a consciously accessible or otherwise psychologically meaningful mental representation, content, or cognitive episode concerning an object, event, proposition, possibility, memory, intention, or relationship.

**What it is not:** A thought is not identical to the entire thinking process that produced it. Thinking can be treated as the activity or processing; thought can refer to what is represented or experienced during that activity.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Forms
Thoughts may take verbal, propositional, imagistic, spatial, conceptual, evaluative, prospective, or mixed forms. A thought may be simple, such as recognizing an object, or complex, such as considering several possible consequences of an action.

### Mechanism
Thoughts arise from interactions among perception, memory, language, attention, knowledge, goals, and other cognitive processes. A thought may be generated deliberately, emerge spontaneously, or be triggered by external or internal events.

### Temporal Character
A thought can be a brief mental event, a sustained train of thought, or part of a larger cognitive episode. Thoughts can influence subsequent thoughts, producing chains of association, elaboration, evaluation, or reasoning.

### Moderators
The content and accessibility of thought depend on current goals, context, knowledge, attention, memory accessibility, and task demands. The same external event can therefore produce different thoughts in different contexts or people.

### Misconceptions
A thought is not necessarily a fully formed sentence in the mind. Thinking can occur through imagery, spatial relations, patterns, concepts, and other representations.

### Related Concepts
Mental representation describes the representational structures used by cognition. Thought is also related to mental contents, interpretation, judgment, reasoning, and metacognitive awareness, but these concepts should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Thought provides a useful unit for describing the contents and episodes generated within the broader dynamics of thinking.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The general construct of thought as a mental content or cognitive episode.

### Defers
Detailed representational mechanisms to 1.3.8 Mental Representation; broader processing to 1.3.4 Cognitive Processing and 1.3.5 Mental Processing.

## 6. Key Principles
- Thoughts are mental contents or cognitive episodes.
- Thoughts can be verbal, imagistic, conceptual, spatial, or mixed.
- A thought is not the same thing as the entire process that produced it.
- Thought content is shaped by context, knowledge, attention, and goals.

# 1.3.3 Thinking Process

## 1. Purpose
This Topic explains thinking as an unfolding process rather than merely a collection of mental contents.

## 2. Definition
A thinking process is an organized sequence or dynamic interaction of cognitive operations through which information or representations are transformed toward an outcome such as understanding, inference, evaluation, planning, problem solving, or decision-making.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
A thinking process may involve selecting information, activating representations, retrieving knowledge, comparing alternatives, detecting relationships, generating possibilities, evaluating consequences, and producing an output. The sequence is not necessarily linear; later processing can modify earlier interpretations.

### Temporal Dynamics
Thinking can unfold rapidly or slowly. Some processes operate with little conscious monitoring, whereas others involve sustained attention and deliberate evaluation. Iteration is common: a person may generate an interpretation, test it, revise it, and test it again.

### Components
Important components include inputs, representations, transformations, intermediate states, evaluations, outputs, and feedback. The exact architecture depends on the task.

### Boundary Conditions
Time pressure, information load, knowledge, task structure, ambiguity, goals, and feedback can alter how a thinking process unfolds.

### Consequences
The output may be a conclusion, judgment, decision, plan, explanation, hypothesis, or new representation. Errors can arise at any stage, including poor information selection, misleading representations, invalid transformations, or inappropriate evaluation.

### Related Concepts
Cognitive Processing and Mental Processing describe broader processing activity. Reasoning specifies particular transformations aimed at drawing conclusions. Decision Process and Problem Solving specify organized classes of thinking directed toward particular outcomes.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
The concept provides the process-level perspective needed to connect the chapter's individual forms of thinking into dynamic sequences.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The general idea of thinking as temporally unfolding cognitive activity.

### Defers
Detailed mechanisms to the specific topics that own them, including reasoning, decision-making, problem solving, and mental simulation.

## 6. Key Principles
- Thinking unfolds over time.
- Cognitive operations can interact recursively rather than occur in a fixed linear order.
- Errors can originate at multiple stages of a thinking process.
- The process must be distinguished from its mental products.

# 1.3.6 Reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Reasoning explains how people derive, evaluate, or revise conclusions from information, assumptions, evidence, or premises.

## 2. Definition
Reasoning is cognitive activity in which a person uses representations, premises, evidence, or assumptions to generate, evaluate, or revise conclusions or implications.

**What it is not:** Reasoning is not synonymous with logical correctness. People can reason using invalid rules, incomplete evidence, biased assumptions, or uncertain information.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Reasoning commonly involves identifying relevant premises, representing relationships among them, applying inferential operations, and evaluating the resulting conclusion. The process may be deductive, inductive, abductive, analogical, causal, probabilistic, conditional, or mixed.

### Forms
Deduction derives conclusions intended to follow necessarily from premises. Induction generalizes or estimates from observations. Abduction seeks plausible explanations. Other forms use similarity, causal structure, or probability.

### Quality
Reasoning quality depends on the validity of the inferential operation, the accuracy and completeness of the premises, the quality of representations, and the handling of uncertainty.

### Moderators
Knowledge, expertise, time pressure, working-memory demands, task framing, familiarity, and available cues can influence reasoning.

### Evidence Status
Reasoning is extensively studied experimentally and formally. Claims about particular reasoning mechanisms vary in evidence strength and depend on task design and theoretical framework.

### Related Concepts
Logical Reasoning is a specific class of reasoning. Inference is the broader act of deriving information not directly given. Judgment concerns evaluation, while decision-making concerns selecting among alternatives.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Reasoning is one of the central transformation processes connecting information and representations to conclusions and decisions.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The general construct of reasoning.

### Defers
Specific reasoning forms to 1.3.19–1.3.27; inference to 1.3.63; judgment to 1.3.28.

## 6. Key Principles
- Reasoning transforms information into conclusions or implications.
- Reasoning can be valid or invalid.
- Different reasoning forms solve different inferential problems.
- Reasoning quality depends on both inference rules and input representations.

# 1.3.8 Mental Representation

## 1. Purpose
Defines the role of mental representation within the dynamics of human thinking while relying on the foundational representation concept owned by Chapter 1.1.

## 2. Definition
Mental representation, in the context of thinking, is the internally available representation of an object, event, relation, state, possibility, or other content that can be used in cognitive operations.

The general psychological concept of representation is owned by 1.1.30 Representation. This Topic specializes that concept for thinking.

## 3. Core Understanding
Mental representations allow a thinker to operate on information that is not necessarily present in the immediate environment. They can support comparison, reasoning, simulation, planning, problem representation, and evaluation.

Representations may be verbal, propositional, spatial, imagistic, conceptual, procedural, or mixed. They can be incomplete, selective, distorted, or updated as new information is processed.

A mental representation is not necessarily a literal picture and does not guarantee accurate correspondence with reality. Its usefulness depends on what information it preserves and what cognitive operation it supports.

Mental Representation is the representational substrate through which the chapter's thinking processes operate. It connects directly to Problem Representation, Mental Simulation, Reasoning, and Mental Model Theory.

## 1. Purpose
Mental Representation explains how information about objects, events, relations, concepts, possibilities, and states can be represented within cognition.

## 2. Definition
A mental representation is an internal cognitive state or structure that stands for, encodes, or carries information about something, whether present, remembered, imagined, abstract, or hypothetical.

# 1.3.9 Automatic Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Automatic Thinking describes cognitive processing that occurs with relatively little deliberate control, effort, or conscious monitoring.

## 2. Definition
Automatic thinking is thinking that is initiated or carried out with relatively low deliberate control and often with limited subjective awareness of the underlying processing.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Characteristics
Automatic processing can be rapid, efficient, routine, cue-driven, and strongly influenced by learned associations. It can produce perceptions, interpretations, evaluations, or action tendencies without extended conscious analysis.

### Mechanism
Repeated learning can make certain cognitive operations highly accessible. When relevant cues appear, the associated processing may be activated quickly without requiring the person to consciously reconstruct every step.

### Advantages
Automaticity can conserve cognitive effort and allow familiar tasks to be performed efficiently.

### Limitations
Automatic processing can also preserve outdated associations, respond to misleading cues, or operate before relevant information has been deliberately examined. Automatic does not mean irrational, and deliberate does not guarantee correctness.

### Moderators
Expertise, practice, familiarity, context, time pressure, and task demands influence automaticity.

### Related Concepts
Deliberate Thinking contrasts with automaticity. Intuitive Thinking can involve automatic processing but is not identical to it. Dual-Process Theory in 1.3.67 provides one theoretical framework for contrasting relatively automatic and deliberative processing.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Automatic Thinking is one of the major processing modes through which human thought unfolds.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The construct of relatively low-control, low-effort thinking.

### Defers
The broader intuition construct to 1.3.11 and dual-process theoretical framing to 1.3.67.

## 6. Key Principles
- Automatic does not mean unconscious in every case.
- Automaticity concerns processing characteristics, not correctness.
- Automatic thinking can improve efficiency and also produce error.

# 1.3.10 Deliberate Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Deliberate Thinking describes thinking involving intentional allocation of attention and cognitive control toward a task or problem.

## 2. Definition
Deliberate thinking is intentional, relatively controlled cognitive processing in which a person actively directs attention and mental effort toward evaluating, transforming, or generating information.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Characteristics
Deliberate thinking commonly involves sustained attention, monitoring, comparison, explicit evaluation, and willingness to revise an initial response.

### Mechanism
A person may identify a question, hold relevant information active, generate alternatives, test implications, compare evidence, and select or revise a conclusion.

### Costs
Deliberate processing generally requires more cognitive effort and time than highly automatic processing. It is therefore not continuously optimal.

### Quality
Deliberation can improve performance when the task benefits from explicit analysis, but additional thought does not guarantee better conclusions. Deliberation can also amplify a poor representation or invalid assumption.

### Moderators
Knowledge, cognitive resources, time, fatigue, task complexity, and motivation affect the effectiveness of deliberation.

### Related Concepts
Analytical, logical, reflective, and critical thinking can involve deliberate processing but refer to more specific properties.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Deliberate Thinking represents a major controlled mode of cognitive processing and provides an important contrast with automatic thinking.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The construct of intentional and relatively controlled cognitive processing.

### Defers
Specific forms of deliberation to later Topics and the broader theoretical distinction to 1.3.67.

## 6. Key Principles
- Deliberation involves intentional cognitive control.
- More deliberation does not automatically produce better thinking.
- Deliberation trades cognitive cost for greater opportunity for monitoring and revision.

# 1.3.11 Intuitive Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Intuitive Thinking explains judgments or thoughts that arise relatively quickly from integrated patterns, experience, associations, or cues without requiring explicit step-by-step reasoning.

## 2. Definition
Intuitive thinking is cognitive processing in which a conclusion, impression, or judgment emerges relatively quickly and with limited conscious access to the intermediate steps producing it.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Intuition can reflect learned pattern sensitivity, associative processing, tacit knowledge, or rapid integration of multiple cues. In skilled domains, extensive experience can make complex patterns rapidly recognizable.

### Quality
Intuition can be accurate when the environment contains stable, learnable regularities and the person has relevant experience. It can be unreliable when cues are weak, environments are unstable, or misleading associations dominate.

### Awareness
The person may experience the output as a “sense” or immediate impression without being able to articulate every contributing operation.

### Moderators
Expertise, feedback quality, environmental predictability, familiarity, emotional state, and time pressure can affect intuitive performance.

### Misconceptions
Intuition is not inherently irrational. It is also not automatically trustworthy merely because it feels immediate or certain.

### Related Concepts
Automatic Thinking concerns low-control processing more generally. Analytical Thinking emphasizes explicit decomposition and evaluation.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Intuitive Thinking is an important form of rapid cognitive output and helps explain why people can reach conclusions before explicit reasoning is articulated.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The construct of intuition as a mode of relatively rapid, non-explicitly articulated cognition.

### Defers
Automaticity to 1.3.9 and formal dual-process theory to 1.3.67.

## 6. Key Principles
- Intuition can reflect learned expertise.
- Intuitive accuracy depends strongly on environment and experience.
- Subjective confidence is not proof of intuitive accuracy.

# 1.3.12 Analytical Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Analytical Thinking explains deliberate decomposition and examination of information, relationships, and alternatives.

## 2. Definition
Analytical thinking is a mode of thinking that deliberately separates a complex problem, claim, or decision into components or relationships so they can be examined systematically.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Analysis may involve identifying variables, separating assumptions from evidence, comparing components, tracing relationships, calculating implications, and evaluating each part before integrating the results.

### Strengths
Analytical processing can make assumptions visible, support detailed comparison, and reduce dependence on an undifferentiated impression.

### Limitations
Analysis can become inefficient when the task is simple, when information is excessive, or when decomposition destroys important interactions among components.

### Moderators
Expertise, task structure, available data, time, cognitive resources, and the quality of the decomposition influence effectiveness.

### Related Concepts
Logical Thinking concerns coherence and valid relations. Critical Thinking adds evaluation of evidence and assumptions. Deliberate Thinking describes the control characteristics rather than the specific analytical style.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Analytical Thinking is one of the chapter's major forms of deliberate cognitive processing.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Deliberate decomposition and systematic examination as a thinking style.

### Defers
Formal reasoning rules to reasoning topics and evidence evaluation to Critical Thinking.

## 6. Key Principles
- Analysis separates a complex whole into examinable elements.
- Good analysis preserves important relationships rather than merely fragmenting information.
- Analytical thinking is a method, not a guarantee of correctness.

# 1.3.13 Logical Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Defines logical coherence as a characteristic of thinking.

## 2. Definition
Logical thinking is thinking organized according to relevant relations of coherence, consistency, implication, or contradiction.

## 3. Core Understanding
This Topic concerns the **organization and quality of thought**, not the inferential act itself. Everyday thought can be logically structured without formal symbolic proof.

Logical thinking is distinct from Logical Reasoning. Logical reasoning is the inferential process of deriving or evaluating conclusions from premises. Logical thinking is the broader characteristic in which logical relations are maintained.

Logical coherence and consistency as characteristics of thought.

## 6. Key Principles
- Logical thinking concerns coherence.
- Coherence does not guarantee truth.
- Logical thinking is broader than formal proof.

Logical Thinking describes thinking that maintains consistency among propositions and relationships and follows appropriate inferential structure.

Logical thinking is thinking organized according to valid or coherent relationships among propositions, assumptions, evidence, and conclusions.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Logical thinking involves identifying relationships among statements and ensuring that conclusions follow appropriately from the assumptions or evidence used.

# 1.3.14 Rational Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Rational Thinking explains cognition directed toward appropriate conclusions or actions given goals, evidence, probabilities, and constraints.

## 2. Definition
Rational thinking is thinking that uses available information and appropriate inferential or evaluative procedures in a way that is responsive to evidence, uncertainty, goals, and relevant constraints.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Rational thinking requires more than formal logic. It can involve probability, causal reasoning, evidence evaluation, comparison of expected consequences, and revision when new information becomes available.

### Goal Dependence
Whether a decision is rational depends partly on the objective being pursued. A choice can be instrumentally rational relative to one goal and not another.

### Boundedness
Human rationality is constrained by limited information, time, memory, computation, and attention. Rational thinking therefore often means making a defensible use of available resources rather than achieving mathematically perfect optimization.

### Misconceptions
Rational does not mean emotionless. Emotional information can be relevant to goals, values, and risk. Rationality also does not mean certainty.

### Related Concepts
Probabilistic reasoning, judgment, decision-making, and critical thinking each address components of rational cognition.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Rational Thinking connects the chapter's reasoning processes with evidence-sensitive evaluation and goal-directed choice.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The general construct of evidence- and goal-responsive cognition.

### Defers
Specific decision criteria, probability operations, and judgment mechanisms to their respective Topics.

## 6. Key Principles
- Rationality is context- and goal-sensitive.
- Rational thinking can operate under uncertainty.
- Rationality does not require emotional absence or perfect optimization.

# 1.3.15 Reflective Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Reflective Thinking explains deliberate reconsideration of one's interpretations, assumptions, experiences, and conclusions.

## 2. Definition
Reflective thinking is deliberate cognitive examination of one's own thoughts, experiences, assumptions, interpretations, or conclusions in order to understand, evaluate, or revise them.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Reflection can involve recalling an experience, identifying an initial interpretation, examining assumptions, considering alternatives, comparing outcomes with expectations, and revising understanding.

### Functions
Reflection can support learning, error correction, self-monitoring, planning, and integration of experience.

### Limits
Reflection does not automatically produce accurate insight. People can repeatedly analyze an inaccurate premise or construct persuasive explanations after the fact.

### Moderators
Feedback quality, metacognitive skill, time, emotional state, knowledge, and willingness to revise influence the value of reflection.

### Related Concepts
Critical Thinking emphasizes evaluation of claims and evidence. Metacognition concerns knowledge and monitoring of one's own cognition. Reflection can use both but is not identical to either.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Reflective Thinking provides a mechanism for cognitive review and revision within the dynamic system of thinking.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Deliberate reconsideration of one's cognitive activity and experience.

### Defers
Specific metacognitive mechanisms to their canonical topic if present elsewhere in the broader system.

## 6. Key Principles
- Reflection involves reconsideration, not mere recollection.
- Reflection can support learning and revision.
- Reflection can still be distorted by inaccurate assumptions.

# 1.3.16 Critical Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Critical Thinking explains disciplined evaluation of claims, evidence, reasoning, assumptions, and alternatives.

## 2. Definition
Critical thinking is deliberate, reasoned evaluation of information, claims, arguments, assumptions, evidence, and possible conclusions using relevant standards of accuracy, relevance, consistency, justification, and uncertainty.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components
Critical thinking may include clarifying the claim, identifying assumptions, examining evidence, evaluating reasoning, considering alternatives, checking uncertainty, and revising the conclusion when warranted.

### Mechanism
A critical thinker does not merely search for flaws. The process evaluates both supporting and disconfirming information and asks whether the conclusion is proportionate to the evidence.

### Boundaries
Critical thinking cannot eliminate uncertainty. It improves the quality of evaluation within the limits of available information and reasoning capacity.

### Misconceptions
Critical thinking is not automatic skepticism. Rejecting every claim without evidence is not more critical than accepting every claim.

### Evidence Status
Critical-thinking instruction has a substantial research literature, but transfer from training in one context to broad improvements across unrelated contexts is not guaranteed.

### Related Concepts
Logical Thinking concerns consistency and inferential structure; Reflective Thinking concerns reconsideration; Rational Thinking concerns evidence- and goal-responsive cognition.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Critical Thinking is a central quality-control process within human thinking.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The general process of disciplined evaluation of claims, evidence, reasoning, and assumptions.

### Defers
Formal reasoning types to 1.3.19–1.3.27 and judgment to 1.3.28.

## 6. Key Principles
- Critical thinking evaluates both evidence and reasoning.
- It is not synonymous with skepticism.
- Good critical thinking calibrates conclusions to evidence.

# 1.3.17 Abstract Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Abstract Thinking explains the ability to operate on concepts, properties, relationships, or principles that are not tied to one concrete instance.

## 2. Definition
Abstract thinking is the ability to represent and reason about general concepts, categories, relationships, principles, or possibilities beyond the immediate concrete instance.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Abstraction involves identifying common structure across instances, separating relevant properties from incidental details, and operating on the resulting general representation.

### Functions
Abstract thinking supports categorization, theory building, analogy, symbolic reasoning, planning, and understanding principles that apply across situations.

### Limits
Abstraction can remove useful detail. An abstract representation is useful when the omitted information is irrelevant to the task; it can mislead when omitted details determine the outcome.

### Development
Abstract reasoning abilities develop through interacting cognitive, educational, linguistic, and experiential processes. Development is not simply a single transition from concrete to abstract thought.

### Related Concepts
Concrete Thinking focuses more directly on observable or specific instances. Mental Representation is the broader representational foundation.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Abstract Thinking represents the level of representation at which many higher-order reasoning processes operate.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
General cognition involving concepts and generalized relations.

### Defers
Specific developmental claims and formal reasoning processes to their respective domains.

## 6. Key Principles
- Abstraction preserves some structure while omitting other details.
- Useful abstraction depends on task relevance.
- Abstract thinking is not simply detached from reality; it can model real systems.

# 1.3.18 Concrete Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Concrete Thinking explains cognition focused on specific, directly represented objects, events, examples, or observable relations.

## 2. Definition
Concrete thinking is thinking that operates primarily on specific instances, observable information, literal meanings, or directly represented situations rather than generalized abstractions.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Characteristics
Concrete thinking is often useful when details, procedures, physical properties, or immediate circumstances matter. It can support accurate handling of specific information without requiring extensive abstraction.

### Mechanism
The thinker retains or manipulates representations closely tied to particular examples or directly specified relations.

### Strengths
Concrete representations can reduce ambiguity and preserve operational detail.

### Limitations
Exclusive reliance on concrete representations can make it difficult to generalize principles or transfer knowledge across structurally similar situations.

### Misconceptions
Concrete thinking is not inherently inferior to abstract thinking. The appropriate representational level depends on the task.

### Related Concepts
Abstract Thinking differs by operating more strongly on generalized concepts and relations.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Concrete Thinking establishes one end of a representational continuum that helps explain differences in how information is mentally organized.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Thinking focused on specific and directly represented content.

### Defers
Broader representational theory to 1.3.8.

## 6. Key Principles
- Concrete thinking preserves specific detail.
- Concrete and abstract thinking serve different functions.
- Effective cognition often requires movement between levels of abstraction.

# 1.3.19 Logical Reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Defines inference governed by logical relations between premises and conclusions.

## 2. Definition
Logical reasoning is the inferential process of deriving, evaluating, or rejecting conclusions on the basis of logical relations among propositions, premises, and conclusions.

## 3. Core Understanding
Logical reasoning asks whether a conclusion follows from premises under the relevant logical structure. Deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning are distinct forms with different standards of support.

A valid inference can still yield a false conclusion when premises are false. A true conclusion can also be reached by invalid reasoning.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Logical Reasoning is the inferential counterpart to Logical Thinking and the parent process for the specialized reasoning forms that follow.

## 5. Boundaries
### Owns
Inference governed by logical relations among premises and conclusions.

### Defers
General coherence to 1.3.13; specific forms to 1.3.20–1.3.22; scientific inference to Chapter 1.2.

## 6. Key Principles
- Logical reasoning concerns premises-to-conclusion relations.
- Validity is distinct from truth.
- Logical reasoning is broader than formal symbolic proof.

## 1. Purpose
Logical Reasoning specifies reasoning in which conclusions are evaluated according to formal or structured inferential relationships.

## 2. Definition
Logical reasoning is reasoning that evaluates whether conclusions follow appropriately from stated premises according to logical relationships or rules.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
The thinker represents premises, identifies their logical relationships, applies an inferential rule, and evaluates whether the resulting conclusion follows.

### Validity
An argument is logically valid when its conclusion follows from its premises such that if the premises were true, the conclusion could not be false under the specified logical structure.

### Related Concepts
Deductive reasoning is one major form of logical reasoning. Conditional reasoning handles if–then relations. Critical Thinking can evaluate whether the premises and logical structure are justified.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Logical Reasoning is the formal-structure-focused member of the chapter's reasoning family.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The general construct of reasoning based on logical relationships.

### Defers
Specific forms to 1.3.20–1.3.27.

## 6. Key Principles

- Validity is distinct from factual truth.
- Correct reasoning requires attention to both premises and inferential form.

# 1.3.20 Deductive Reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Deductive Reasoning explains inference from general or stated premises to conclusions intended to follow necessarily.

## 2. Definition
Deductive reasoning is reasoning in which the conclusion is intended to follow necessarily from the premises under the applicable inferential rules.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
The reasoner starts with premises and applies a valid deductive rule. If the premises are true and the argument is valid, the conclusion must be true.

### Example
If all members of a defined set have property X, and object A is a member of that set, then A has property X.

### Strength
Deduction preserves truth under valid inference from true premises.

### Limitations
Deduction cannot establish the truth of its starting premises. It can also produce little new factual information when the conclusion simply makes explicit what the premises already imply.

### Misconceptions
Deduction is not identical to all forms of reasoning. Induction and abduction serve different purposes.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Deduction is the clearest example of rule-governed inference within the chapter's reasoning architecture.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Inference from premises to conclusions intended to follow necessarily.

### Defers
General logical reasoning to 1.3.19 and other inference forms to 1.3.21–1.3.27.

## 6. Key Principles
- Valid deduction preserves truth from true premises.
- Deduction cannot validate its own premises.
- Deductive certainty depends on the logical structure and premise assumptions.

# 1.3.21 Inductive Reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Inductive Reasoning explains how people generalize, estimate, or infer broader patterns from observations.

## 2. Definition
Inductive reasoning is reasoning in which observations or specific cases are used to support a broader generalization, expectation, or probability judgment without logical necessity.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
The reasoner examines observations, identifies a pattern, and extends that pattern beyond the observed cases.

### Strength
Induction is essential for learning from experience and making predictions about cases not yet observed.

### Uncertainty
Even a strong inductive argument does not guarantee its conclusion. The conclusion is supported to a degree rather than entailed.

### Moderators
Sample size, representativeness, variability, base rates, measurement quality, and environmental stability influence inductive quality.

### Misconceptions
Repeated observation does not automatically establish a universal law. The evidential strength depends on how informative the observations are.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Inductive Reasoning explains how thinking moves from observed instances toward general expectations.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Generalization and probabilistic support from observed cases.

### Defers
Probabilistic reasoning to 1.3.25 and prediction to 1.3.53.

## 6. Key Principles
- Induction supports rather than guarantees conclusions.
- Evidence quality depends on sampling and representativeness.
- Inductive conclusions should retain appropriate uncertainty.

# 1.3.22 Abductive Reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Abductive Reasoning explains inference toward plausible explanations of observations.

## 2. Definition
Abductive reasoning is reasoning that generates or evaluates a possible explanation for observations, typically seeking an explanation that accounts for the available evidence well.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
The reasoner begins with observations, identifies possible explanatory hypotheses, and evaluates how well each could account for the evidence.

### Nature
Abduction produces plausible explanations, not deductively guaranteed truths. Several explanations can sometimes fit the same observations.

### Evaluation
Useful criteria may include explanatory coverage, coherence, simplicity, consistency with established knowledge, and predictive consequences. No single criterion is sufficient in every domain.

### Misconceptions
The “best explanation” is not necessarily the true explanation. Abductive inference remains vulnerable to missing information and alternative hypotheses.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Abduction connects observed information to hypothesis generation and explanation.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Inference toward plausible explanations.

### Defers
Hypothesis Generation to 1.3.66 and Explanation to 1.3.65.

## 6. Key Principles
- Abduction seeks plausible explanations.
- Plausibility is not certainty.
- Alternative explanations must remain possible when evidence underdetermines the cause.

# 1.3.23 Analogical Reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Analogical Reasoning explains how people use structural similarities between situations to transfer knowledge or infer possibilities.

## 2. Definition
Analogical reasoning is reasoning in which relationships or structural patterns in one domain are mapped onto another domain to support understanding, inference, or problem solving.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
The thinker identifies a source situation, detects relevant relational structure, maps corresponding elements to a target situation, and transfers an inference or strategy.

### Quality
Analogy is strongest when the shared structure is causally or functionally relevant rather than merely superficial.

### Uses
Analogy can support learning, explanation, problem solving, and generation of hypotheses.

### Limits
Surface similarity can produce misleading mappings. A target may resemble the source in appearance while differing in the relations that matter.

### Related Concepts
Mental Representation supports mapping. Problem Solving can use analogy as a solution-generation strategy.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Analogical Reasoning demonstrates how stored representations can be transferred across contexts through relational structure.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Reasoning based on structural correspondence between domains.

### Defers
Problem-solving use to 1.3.39 and representation generally to 1.3.8.

## 6. Key Principles
- Good analogies preserve relevant relational structure.
- Surface resemblance is not sufficient.
- Analogy supports inference but does not guarantee correctness.

# 1.3.24 Causal Reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Causal Reasoning explains how people reason about causes, effects, mechanisms, interventions, and counterfactual consequences.

## 2. Definition
Causal reasoning is reasoning about whether, how, or under what conditions one factor produces, prevents, changes, or contributes to another outcome.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Causal reasoning may involve temporal order, covariation, mechanisms, interventions, background conditions, and alternative explanations.

### Forms
People may infer causes from observed patterns, reason about the effects of interventions, or use causal models to predict consequences.

### Limits
Association alone does not establish causation. Confounding, selection, reverse causation, measurement error, and unobserved variables can produce misleading causal impressions.

### Evidence Status
Causal conclusions require evidence appropriate to the claim. Experiments can provide strong causal identification under suitable conditions; observational designs can support causal inference under explicit assumptions but generally require greater caution.

### Related Concepts
Counterfactual Thinking examines alternative states. Prediction and forecasting may use causal models but are not themselves causal reasoning.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Causal Reasoning is essential for understanding how thinking moves from observed relationships toward explanations and intervention-related conclusions.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The cognitive construct of reasoning about causal structure.

### Defers
Formal scientific causal inference to the scientific methods system, while preserving the distinction between association and causation.

## 6. Key Principles
- Correlation is not sufficient for causal inference.
- Causal reasoning involves mechanisms, alternatives, and conditions.
- Causal conclusions should be calibrated to evidence.

# 1.3.25 Probabilistic Reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Probabilistic Reasoning explains how people reason when conclusions are uncertain and outcomes have different likelihoods.

## 2. Definition
Probabilistic reasoning is reasoning that represents, compares, or updates uncertainty about events, hypotheses, or outcomes using probabilities or probability-like judgments.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
The reasoner combines evidence with prior information to estimate how plausible different possibilities are. Formal Bayesian reasoning is one mathematically explicit framework, but human probabilistic reasoning is not limited to formal calculation.

### Uses
Probability supports diagnosis, forecasting, risk assessment, prediction, planning, and decisions under uncertainty.

### Errors
People may neglect base rates, misunderstand conditional probabilities, overweight salient evidence, or express more confidence than the evidence warrants.

### Moderators
Numeracy, domain knowledge, representation format, feedback, time pressure, and complexity affect probabilistic reasoning.

### Misconceptions
A probability is not a guarantee. A low-probability event can occur, and a high-probability event can fail.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Probabilistic Reasoning provides the uncertainty-sensitive foundation for prediction, forecasting, and decision-making.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Cognitive reasoning involving uncertain likelihoods.

### Defers
Formal Bayesian theory to the broader scientific/statistical architecture where appropriate.

## 6. Key Principles
- Probability represents uncertainty, not certainty.
- Base rates and conditional structure can materially change conclusions.
- Confidence should be calibrated to evidence.

# 1.3.26 Conditional Reasoning

## 1. Purpose
Conditional Reasoning explains thinking involving relationships of the form “if X, then Y” and related hypothetical conditions.

## 2. Definition
Conditional reasoning is reasoning about propositions whose truth or consequences depend on specified conditions.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
The thinker represents a conditional relationship, identifies whether the relevant condition is satisfied, and determines what follows or what would be relevant under alternative conditions.

### Importance
Conditional reasoning supports planning, diagnosis, rule application, hypothesis testing, and counterfactual thought.

### Errors
People can confuse sufficient and necessary conditions, reverse conditional relations, or infer consequences that do not logically follow.

### Relation to Counterfactuals
Conditional reasoning can concern actual conditions or hypothetical ones. Counterfactual Thinking specifically concerns alternatives to what actually occurred.

### Related Concepts
Logical Reasoning provides the broader inferential structure. Counterfactual Thinking extends conditional reasoning to alternative histories or states.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Conditional reasoning provides a basic structure for representing contingencies and conditional consequences.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Reasoning about condition-dependent propositions.

### Defers
Counterfactual cognition to 1.3.27.

## 6. Key Principles
- Conditional relations specify dependencies between conditions and outcomes.
- Necessary and sufficient conditions must not be confused.
- Conditional reasoning can describe actual, hypothetical, or counterfactual situations.

# 1.3.27 Counterfactual Thinking

## 1. Purpose
Counterfactual Thinking explains cognition about alternatives to events or states that actually occurred.

## 2. Definition
Counterfactual thinking is thinking about how events, outcomes, or states might have been different if one or more aspects of the actual situation had been different.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
The thinker constructs an alternative state while retaining enough of the actual situation to identify the changed condition. Counterfactuals can be used to understand causes, evaluate choices, imagine prevention, or plan future behavior.

### Forms
Counterfactuals can be upward, imagining a better outcome; downward, imagining a worse outcome; additive, introducing a missing action; or subtractive, removing an action or event.

### Functions
They can support learning and causal understanding but can also contribute to regret or rumination when repeatedly focused on uncontrollable alternatives.

### Boundary Conditions
Counterfactual quality depends on knowledge of causal structure and the plausibility of the altered condition.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Counterfactual Thinking connects conditional reasoning, causal reasoning, mental simulation, and future-oriented cognition.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Mental construction of alternatives to actual states or events.

### Defers
Mental Simulation to 1.3.49 and Future Thinking to 1.3.51.

## 6. Key Principles
- Counterfactuals modify aspects of an actual state.
- They can support learning and causal understanding.
- Plausibility depends on the underlying causal and situational model.

# 1.3.28 Judgment

## 1. Purpose
Judgment explains the cognitive process of evaluating information and arriving at an assessment, estimate, or conclusion about a person, event, claim, or state.

## 2. Definition
Judgment is the cognitive act of evaluating available information to form an assessment, estimate, belief, or conclusion about something.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Judgment can involve information selection, interpretation, comparison with standards, integration of cues, and expression of an evaluation or estimate.

### Forms
Judgments may concern likelihood, value, quality, causality, responsibility, trustworthiness, severity, or other attributes.

### Sources of Error
Judgments can be influenced by incomplete information, biased sampling, framing, prior beliefs, irrelevant cues, and cognitive shortcuts.

### Calibration
Good judgment requires correspondence between confidence and accuracy where accuracy can be meaningfully assessed.

### Related Concepts
Reasoning can support judgment, but judgment does not require a formal inferential chain. Decision-making differs because it involves selecting among actions or options.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns

### Defers
Decision selection to 1.3.29 and decision sequence to 1.3.30.

## 6. Key Principles
- Judgment produces an assessment or estimate.
- Judgment quality depends on information and evaluation processes.
- Confidence and accuracy are distinct.

# 1.3.29 Decision-Making

## 1. Purpose
Decision-Making explains how people select among possible actions, commitments, or alternatives.

## 2. Definition
Decision-making is the cognitive and behavioral process of selecting, committing to, or intentionally refraining from an available course of action or state among alternatives.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components
Decision-making can involve goals, options, predictions, preferences, values, constraints, risk assessment, comparison, choice, and commitment.

### Mechanism
A person identifies the decision problem, generates or recognizes options, evaluates them, forms or accesses preferences, selects an option, and may commit to action.

### Uncertainty
Decisions are often made with incomplete information. People may therefore rely on probabilities, heuristics, experience, values, and assumptions.

### Outcomes
A decision can be judged by the quality of the process or by the eventual outcome. A sound decision can produce an unfavorable outcome because uncertain events are not fully controllable.

### Related Concepts
Decision Process describes the temporal sequence. Choice is the selection event. Decision Confidence and Commitment concern subjective certainty and willingness to act.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Decision-making is a major endpoint at which multiple thinking processes converge.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The general construct of selecting or refraining among alternatives.

### Defers
Detailed stages to 1.3.30 and option/evaluation constructs to 1.3.31–1.3.38.

## 6. Key Principles
- Decisions concern action or commitment among alternatives.
- Decision quality must be distinguished from outcome quality.
- Uncertainty is intrinsic to many decisions.

# 1.3.30 Decision Process

## 1. Purpose
Decision Process explains the sequence and interaction of cognitive operations involved in reaching a decision.

## 2. Definition
A decision process is the temporally unfolding set of cognitive operations through which a person identifies a decision, considers alternatives, evaluates them, selects an option, and may commit or revise the choice.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components
Common components include problem recognition, option generation, evaluation, preference formation, choice, confidence, commitment, implementation, feedback, and revision.

### Dynamics
Decision processes are not always linear. New information can cause a person to return to earlier stages, redefine the problem, generate new alternatives, or change evaluation criteria.

### Moderators
Time pressure, uncertainty, complexity, expertise, stakes, information availability, and emotional or motivational context can alter the process.

### Misconceptions
A decision process cannot be inferred completely from the final choice. Different processes can produce the same decision.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
This Topic organizes the decision-related Topics that follow it.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The dynamic sequence and interaction of decision operations.

### Defers
Specific operations to 1.3.31–1.3.38.

## 6. Key Principles
- Decision-making is often iterative.
- New information can reopen earlier stages.
- Final choice alone does not reveal the complete decision process.

# 1.3.32 Alternative Generation

Alternative generation is the process of constructing distinct possible courses of action, solutions, or states that could serve as substitutes within a decision or problem.

### Mechanism

Generating alternatives can reduce premature commitment to the first plausible solution and reveal trade-offs.

Alternatives are limited by knowledge, imagination, representation of the problem, resources, and perceived feasibility.

### Misconceptions

# 1.3.34 Preference Formation

## 1. Purpose
Preference Formation explains how relative liking, valuation, priority, or desirability among alternatives develops or changes.

## 2. Definition
Preference formation is the cognitive process through which a person constructs, accesses, or changes relative evaluations of possible objects, outcomes, or actions.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Preferences can reflect goals, values, beliefs about consequences, prior experience, affective responses, social information, constraints, and comparisons among alternatives.

### Context Dependence
Preferences can be constructed during decisions rather than always existing as fixed, fully articulated rankings.

### Stability
Some preferences are stable and deeply learned; others are sensitive to framing, context, available options, or new information.

### Misconceptions
A stated preference is not necessarily immutable. Revision after relevant information is not automatically inconsistency; it can be appropriate updating.

### Related Concepts
Alternative Evaluation supplies comparative information; Choice converts preferences and other considerations into a selection.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Preference Formation explains how evaluations become organized into relative priorities within decision-making.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Formation and change of relative preferences.

### Defers
Choice to 1.3.35 and commitment to 1.3.37.

## 6. Key Principles
- Preferences can be stable or constructed in context.
- Preferences reflect more than objective features alone.
- Preference revision can be rational when information or goals change.

# 1.3.35 Choice

## 1. Purpose
Choice explains the selection event through which one available alternative is selected, retained, or rejected.

## 2. Definition
Choice is the act of selecting one option, course of action, or state from among available alternatives, including an intentional decision to defer or select none.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Choice integrates relevant evaluations, preferences, constraints, probabilities, values, and situational demands into a selection.

### Distinction
Choice is the selection outcome or act; the broader decision process includes the operations that lead to it.

### Constraints
The selected option may reflect limited information, time, opportunity, or feasibility rather than a complete comparison of all possible alternatives.

### Outcome
Choice does not guarantee the selected option will produce the desired outcome because external uncertainty remains.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Choice is a central output of the decision process.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The selection event among available alternatives.

### Defers
Confidence, commitment, and revision to 1.3.36–1.3.38.

## 6. Key Principles
- Choice is distinct from the process that produces it.
- A choice can be rational under uncertainty and still produce an unfavorable result.
- Not choosing can itself be a choice.

# 1.3.36 Decision Confidence

## 1. Purpose
Decision Confidence explains subjective certainty that a selected or contemplated decision is correct, appropriate, or likely to succeed.

## 2. Definition
Decision confidence is the person's subjective assessment of certainty in the correctness, adequacy, or expected success of a decision.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Calibration
Confidence is useful when it tracks actual accuracy or decision quality, but confidence and correctness can diverge.

### Mechanism
Confidence can reflect evidence strength, consistency of information, familiarity, perceived control, prior success, and subjective coherence.

### Dynamics
Confidence can rise or fall as information changes. It can also differ from confidence in the underlying facts or from confidence in eventual outcomes.

### Misconceptions
High confidence does not prove correctness. Low confidence does not prove that a decision is poor.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Decision Confidence is a metacognitive evaluation attached to the decision process.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Subjective certainty about a decision.

### Defers
Choice itself to 1.3.35 and commitment to 1.3.37.

## 6. Key Principles
- Confidence is a subjective state.
- Accuracy and confidence are separate dimensions.
- Good calibration requires confidence to correspond reasonably to evidence and performance.

# 1.3.37 Decision Commitment

## 1. Purpose
Decision Commitment explains the transition from selecting or intending an option to treating that decision as settled enough to guide action.

## 2. Definition
Decision commitment is the degree to which a person becomes psychologically and behaviorally committed to a selected decision or course of action.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Commitment can involve intention formation, resource allocation, behavioral initiation, and reduced openness to alternatives.

### Distinction
Commitment is not identical to confidence. A person can be uncertain yet committed because action is required.

### Dynamics
Commitment can strengthen through implementation, investment, identity, consistency, or successful progress. It can also weaken when new evidence or changing conditions make revision appropriate.

### Misconception
Persistence after a decision is not automatically evidence of commitment quality. Continuing a poor course merely because one has already invested in it can be maladaptive.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Decision Commitment is the bridge between cognitive selection and sustained action.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Psychological commitment to a decision.

### Defers
Behavioral execution mechanisms to broader systems and decision revision to 1.3.38.

## 6. Key Principles
- Commitment and confidence are distinct.
- Commitment can facilitate consistent action.
- Appropriate commitment includes openness to relevant corrective evidence.

# 1.3.38 Decision Revision

## 1. Purpose
Decision Revision explains how decisions are reconsidered and changed when information, goals, constraints, or expected consequences change.

## 2. Definition
Decision revision is the cognitive process of modifying, reversing, suspending, or replacing a previous decision in response to new information, changed conditions, or reconsidered priorities.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
Revision may involve detecting new evidence, reassessing the original assumptions, updating expectations, generating alternatives, and selecting a modified course.

### Adaptive Function
Revision allows decisions to remain responsive to changing reality rather than treating previous choices as permanently binding.

### Barriers
Commitment, consistency pressure, emotional investment, identity, sunk-cost concerns, or fear of admitting error can make revision more difficult.

### Misconception
Changing a decision is not inherently inconsistency. Revision can be rational when the information state has changed.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Decision Revision completes the decision cycle by allowing feedback to alter previous choices.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Cognitive reconsideration and modification of previous decisions.

### Defers
General feedback and self-regulation to their broader canonical owners.

## 6. Key Principles
- Good decision systems permit revision.
- New evidence can justify changing a previous decision.
- Consistency is not always more rational than updating.

# 1.3.39 Problem Solving

## 1. Purpose
Problem Solving explains how thinking transforms an initial state that is perceived as unsatisfactory or incomplete toward a desired state.

## 2. Definition
Problem solving is the cognitive process of identifying a problem, representing its structure, generating possible solutions, evaluating them, and implementing or selecting actions intended to reduce the gap between the current and desired states.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components
Problem solving commonly involves problem identification, representation, problem-space construction, solution generation, evaluation, strategy selection, and feedback.

### Mechanism
The solver constructs a representation of the current state, desired state, constraints, and available operations. Cognitive operations then search or transform the problem space.

### Forms
Problem solving can be heuristic, algorithmic, insight-based, analogical, iterative, or mixed.

### Moderators
Knowledge, representation quality, feedback, complexity, time, working memory, and environmental structure affect performance.

### Misconceptions
A failed solution does not necessarily mean the solver lacked ability. The problem may have been poorly represented or information may have been unavailable.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Problem Solving is the main problem-directed branch of the chapter's thinking architecture.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The general cognitive process of transforming problem states toward desired states.

### Defers
Specific stages to 1.3.40–1.3.48.

## 6. Key Principles
- Problem solving depends heavily on representation.
- Search occurs within a psychologically represented problem space.
- Solution quality depends on both strategy and problem definition.

# 1.3.40 Problem Identification

# 1.3.43 Solution Generation

Solution Generation explains how possible ways of resolving a problem are produced or retrieved.

## 3. Core Understanding

# 1.3.44 Solution Evaluation

## 1. Purpose
Solution Evaluation explains how candidate solutions are assessed for feasibility, effectiveness, cost, risk, and fit with the problem.

## 2. Definition
Solution evaluation is the cognitive assessment of candidate solutions against the problem's goals, constraints, criteria, expected consequences, and available evidence.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Components
Evaluation can include feasibility, effectiveness, efficiency, cost, risk, robustness, reversibility, and alignment with goals.

### Mechanism
The solver predicts or simulates consequences, compares them with criteria, identifies trade-offs, and selects or revises candidates.

### Uncertainty
Expected consequences are often uncertain. Evaluation therefore depends on assumptions and probability judgments.

### Feedback

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Evaluation of candidate solutions to a represented problem.

### Defers

## 6. Key Principles

- Feasibility and desirability are distinct.
- Uncertain consequences require calibrated expectations.

# 1.3.45 Means–End Analysis

## 1. Purpose
Means–End Analysis explains a problem-solving strategy that compares the current state with the goal and selects operations intended to reduce the difference.

## 2. Definition
Means–end analysis is a problem-solving strategy in which a solver identifies differences between the current and desired states and selects intermediate actions intended to reduce those differences.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
The solver identifies a goal, compares it with the current state, selects a subgoal or operation that reduces a relevant discrepancy, performs or mentally evaluates the operation, and repeats as needed.

### Strength
Means–end analysis can simplify complex problems by decomposing a distant goal into manageable intermediate states.

### Limitation
The strategy depends on the solver's representation of the difference and available operations. A misleading representation can produce ineffective subgoals.

### Related Concepts
Problem Space defines the represented states and transitions. Mental Simulation can help evaluate intermediate moves.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Means–End Analysis is a specific strategy within problem solving.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The difference-reduction strategy for navigating toward a goal.

### Defers
General problem solving to 1.3.39 and heuristic processing to 1.3.46.

## 6. Key Principles
- Means–end analysis works through goal-relevant differences.
- Subgoals can reduce cognitive complexity.
- The quality of the representation determines whether the identified differences are useful.

# 1.3.46 Heuristic Problem Solving

## 1. Purpose
Heuristic Problem Solving explains the use of practical shortcuts or rules of thumb to search for solutions without exhaustively examining every possibility.

## 2. Definition

A heuristic can systematically fail when its assumptions do not fit the problem environment.

### Misconception

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter

### Owns

### Defers

## 6. Key Principles

# 1.3.47 Algorithmic Problem Solving

## 1. Purpose
Algorithmic Problem Solving explains systematic procedures that specify operations for reaching a solution under defined conditions.

## 2. Definition
Algorithmic problem solving is problem solving through a specified sequence of operations or rules that, when applicable and correctly executed, systematically produces a solution or determines that none is reached within the defined procedure.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Mechanism
An algorithm specifies inputs, operations, decision rules, and termination conditions. The solver follows the procedure rather than relying primarily on open-ended search.

### Strengths
Algorithms can increase consistency and reduce dependence on momentary judgment when the problem is well specified.

### Limitations
An algorithm may be computationally expensive, unsuitable for an ill-defined problem, or dependent on assumptions that do not hold.

### Distinction
An algorithm can guarantee a result under its formal conditions; a heuristic generally cannot provide the same guarantee.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
Algorithmic Problem Solving represents rule-governed systematic search within the broader problem-solving architecture.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
Systematic problem solving through specified procedures.

### Defers
General reasoning and computational implementation details.

## 6. Key Principles
- Algorithms specify procedures rather than merely recommendations.
- Their guarantees depend on applicability and assumptions.
- Systematic procedures can still be inappropriate for poorly defined problems.

# 1.3.58 Cognitive Effort

## 2. Definition

## 3. Core Understanding

# 1.3.62 Functional Fixedness

# 1.3.67 Dual-Process Theory

## 1. Purpose
Dual-Process Theory explains a family of psychological models that distinguish relatively fast, automatic processing from relatively slow, controlled processing.

## 2. Definition
Dual-process theory refers to theoretical frameworks proposing that human cognition can involve at least two qualitatively different modes or processes, commonly characterized as relatively automatic and relatively controlled.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Theory / Model
Different dual-process models use different terminology, mechanisms, and claims. A common distinction contrasts rapid, associative, low-control processing with slower, effortful, rule-guided processing.

### What the distinction explains
The framework helps organize findings concerning intuition, deliberation, cognitive effort, reasoning, judgment, and error correction.

### Limitations
The two-process language should not be interpreted as proof that the brain contains exactly two discrete systems. Modern cognitive science often describes cognition as a network of interacting processes with varying degrees of automaticity and control.

### Misconceptions
“Fast equals bad” and “slow equals good” are not valid conclusions. Automatic processing can be highly skilled, and deliberate reasoning can still be biased or incorrect.

### Evidence Status
Dual-process approaches are influential theoretical frameworks, but the exact architecture and boundaries of the proposed processes remain subjects of theoretical debate.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
This theory integrates Automatic Thinking, Deliberate Thinking, Intuitive Thinking, Analytical Thinking, and Cognitive Effort.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The dual-process theoretical framing of human cognition.

### Defers
The underlying cognitive constructs to their individual Topics.

## 6. Key Principles
- Dual-process theory is a family of models, not one universally fixed mechanism.
- Automatic and controlled processing can interact.
- Processing speed does not determine correctness.

# 1.3.70 Cognitive Continuum Theory

## 1. Purpose
Cognitive Continuum Theory explains cognition as potentially varying along a continuum between intuitive and analytical processing rather than requiring a strict binary.

## 2. Definition
Cognitive Continuum Theory is a theoretical framework proposing that cognition can vary along a continuum from more intuitive, automatic, and holistic modes toward more analytical, controlled, and rule-based modes, with task structure influencing where processing tends to occur.

## 3. Core Understanding

### Theory / Model
The framework emphasizes that cognitive processing may occupy intermediate positions rather than always fitting cleanly into two discrete categories.

### Mechanism
Task characteristics such as structure, familiarity, ambiguity, time pressure, and available information can influence the degree to which cognition is more intuitive or analytical.

### Value
The continuum perspective can capture transitions and mixed processing that are difficult to describe using a strict two-category distinction.

### Limits
It is a theoretical framework rather than a universal law establishing one fixed cognitive continuum for all tasks and people. Its constructs and empirical implications should be evaluated against specific research designs.

### Related Concepts
It is closely related to Intuitive Thinking, Analytical Thinking, Automatic Thinking, Deliberate Thinking, and Dual-Process Theory.

### Misconceptions
A continuum does not mean every cognitive process lies on one single measurable dimension. Different processes may vary along different dimensions.

### Evidence Status
Cognitive Continuum Theory is a theoretical model whose usefulness depends on the task domain and operationalization. It should be distinguished from established descriptive findings about variation in processing speed, control, and effort.

## 4. Relationship to This Chapter
This theory provides a flexible integrative perspective for understanding variation between intuitive and analytical cognition across tasks.

## 5. Boundaries

### Owns
The cognitive-continuum theoretical framework.

### Defers
The underlying forms of thinking to their canonical Topics and broader dual-process theory to 1.3.67.

## 6. Key Principles
- Cognition can vary in degree rather than fitting a strict binary.
- Task characteristics influence processing mode.
- The continuum is a theoretical model, not proof of one universal cognitive dimension.
