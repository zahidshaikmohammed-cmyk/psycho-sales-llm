# SYSTEM 1 — HUMAN PSYCHOLOGY
## CHAPTER 1.1 — ARCHITECTURE OF THE HUMAN MIND
### CANONICAL ONTOLOGY MASTER INDEX — REBUILD v1.0

**Status:** ONTOLOGY UNDER AUDIT — NOT YET LOCKED

## Purpose of this chapter

Chapter 1.1 defines the human mind as an organized psychological system.

It does not attempt to teach every cognitive, emotional, motivational, perceptual, learning, memory, developmental, personality, or social process in full. Those mechanisms receive deeper canonical ownership in later System 1 chapters where appropriate.

The purpose here is architectural: to establish what the mind is made of at a useful level of abstraction, how its parts relate, how information moves through the system, how psychological functions are coordinated, how the mind relates to the brain and body, and what properties allow the system to remain both stable and adaptable.

This chapter is the structural foundation for the rest of System 1.

---

# PART I — FOUNDATIONAL ONTOLOGY OF THE MIND

### 1.1.1 Human Mind
**Scope:** The mind as the organized set of psychological processes, states, representations, capacities, and functions through which a person experiences, interprets, learns, evaluates, chooses, regulates, and acts.

### 1.1.2 Mind as an Organized Psychological System
**Scope:** The idea that mental activity is not a random collection of isolated events but an organized system whose components interact.

### 1.1.3 Mental Processes
**Scope:** Ongoing psychological operations such as perceiving, remembering, thinking, feeling, deciding, and regulating behavior. Detailed treatment belongs to later chapters.

### 1.1.4 Mental States
**Scope:** Temporarily existing configurations of psychological activity, such as an emotional, attentional, motivational, or cognitive state.

### 1.1.5 Mental Contents
**Scope:** The information, experiences, thoughts, images, beliefs, memories, goals, feelings, and other contents that can occupy or influence mental activity.

### 1.1.6 Mental Functions
**Scope:** What psychological systems do for the person, including representing information, selecting actions, regulating internal states, learning, adapting, and coordinating behavior.

### 1.1.7 Psychological Organization
**Scope:** The structured arrangement and coordination of psychological processes, representations, states, functions, and control mechanisms.

---

# PART II — AWARENESS AND CONSCIOUS ORGANIZATION

### 1.1.8 Consciousness
**Scope:** The person's subjective awareness of experience and mental activity. This topic establishes the architectural concept without attempting to resolve every scientific theory of consciousness.

### 1.1.9 Conscious Processing
**Scope:** Mental processing that is available to conscious awareness or deliberate report and control.

### 1.1.10 Unconscious Processing
**Scope:** Psychological processing that can occur without conscious awareness. This is a scientific description, not a claim that all unconscious processing follows a single hidden system.

### 1.1.11 Awareness
**Scope:** Detection or knowledge of internal or external information, including awareness of stimuli, thoughts, feelings, bodily states, and actions.

### 1.1.12 Metacognitive Awareness
**Scope:** Awareness of one's own knowledge, uncertainty, thinking, attention, memory, reasoning, or performance. Detailed metacognition may be developed elsewhere.

### 1.1.13 Forms and Levels of Awareness
**Scope:** Differences in what is consciously accessible, partially accessible, unattended, or outside awareness at a given moment.

### 1.1.14 Preconscious Processing as a Historical Concept
**Scope:** The historical psychoanalytic use of the term preconscious and its distinction from contemporary scientific descriptions of unconscious processing. It must not be presented as a universally accepted physical layer of the mind.

---

# PART III — ORGANIZATION OF PSYCHOLOGICAL SYSTEMS

### 1.1.15 Structural Organization of the Mind
**Scope:** How psychological components can be distinguished and related within an overall architecture.

### 1.1.16 Functional Organization of the Mind
**Scope:** Organization according to what different psychological processes accomplish.

### 1.1.17 Hierarchical Organization
**Scope:** Organization in which lower-level processes can support higher-level coordination and goals, without assuming that every psychological process forms a single strict hierarchy.

### 1.1.18 Distributed Organization
**Scope:** The principle that psychological functions can depend on activity distributed across interacting processes and neural systems rather than one isolated mental location.

### 1.1.19 Network Organization
**Scope:** Interconnected psychological and neural components whose relationships can change with task, state, learning, and context.

### 1.1.20 Temporal Organization
**Scope:** The fact that psychological activity unfolds over time and that earlier states and events can influence later processing.

### 1.1.21 Context-Dependent Organization
**Scope:** The way psychological activity changes according to goals, environment, social situation, prior experience, and current internal state.

---

# PART IV — CORE PSYCHOLOGICAL SYSTEMS

These topics define major functional systems at architectural level. Their detailed mechanisms remain owned by the appropriate later System 1 chapters.

### 1.1.22 Cognitive System
**Scope:** Systems involved in acquiring, representing, transforming, evaluating, and using information.

### 1.1.23 Perceptual System
**Scope:** Systems that transform sensory input into psychologically meaningful representations of the environment and body.

### 1.1.24 Memory System
**Scope:** Systems involved in encoding, retaining, maintaining, and retrieving information.

### 1.1.25 Learning System
**Scope:** Processes through which experience produces relatively lasting changes in knowledge, behavior, skills, or response tendencies.

### 1.1.26 Language System
**Scope:** Psychological capacities involved in understanding, producing, and representing language. Detailed language psychology remains a later topic owner.

### 1.1.27 Affective and Emotional System
**Scope:** Systems involved in affective states, emotional responses, appraisal, feeling, and emotion-related action tendencies.

### 1.1.28 Motivational System
**Scope:** Processes that energize, direct, maintain, or terminate goal-directed behavior.

### 1.1.29 Action and Behavioral System
**Scope:** Processes that translate psychological states, goals, and information into observable action and behavior.

### 1.1.30 Self System
**Scope:** Psychological processes involved in representing, evaluating, monitoring, and regulating the self.

### 1.1.31 Social-Cognitive System
**Scope:** Processes used to represent and interpret other people, social situations, and one's own position within a social environment.

### 1.1.32 Regulatory and Control System
**Scope:** Processes that monitor conditions, maintain goals, resolve competing demands, regulate behavior, and adjust ongoing activity.

### 1.1.33 Homeostatic and Allostatic Regulation
**Scope:** Regulation of internal conditions through mechanisms that maintain stability and mechanisms that anticipate or adjust to changing demands.

---

# PART V — INFORMATION-PROCESSING ARCHITECTURE

### 1.1.34 Information Processing
**Scope:** The transformation and use of information by psychological systems.

### 1.1.35 Input–Processing–Output Organization
**Scope:** A useful functional description linking incoming information, internal processing, and resulting responses. It is an organizing model, not a complete literal description of all mental activity.

### 1.1.36 Selection
**Scope:** Selection of information, representations, goals, responses, or actions from competing possibilities.

### 1.1.37 Encoding
**Scope:** Transformation of information into forms that can be represented or used by the psychological system.

### 1.1.38 Transformation
**Scope:** Changes made to information during psychological processing.

### 1.1.39 Integration
**Scope:** Combining information from different sources, processes, or time points into a coordinated state or representation.

### 1.1.40 Storage
**Scope:** Retention or maintenance of information within psychological systems. Detailed memory mechanisms belong to the memory chapter.

### 1.1.41 Retrieval
**Scope:** Re-accessing information or representations for current processing.

### 1.1.42 Updating
**Scope:** Revising active representations, beliefs, goals, or internal states in response to new information or changing conditions.

### 1.1.43 Monitoring
**Scope:** Tracking internal processes, external conditions, performance, errors, goals, or outcomes.

### 1.1.44 Evaluation
**Scope:** Assessing information, situations, actions, or outcomes against goals, values, expectations, or other standards.

### 1.1.45 Response Selection
**Scope:** Choosing among possible actions or responses.

### 1.1.46 Action Execution
**Scope:** Converting selected responses into coordinated behavior.

---

# PART VI — REPRESENTATION AND INTERNAL KNOWLEDGE STRUCTURE

### 1.1.47 Mental Representation
**Scope:** Internal psychological structures or states that stand for, encode, or carry information about objects, events, people, concepts, actions, or situations.

### 1.1.48 Internal Representation
**Scope:** Representation of information within the organism, distinguished from the external world or external information display.

### 1.1.49 External Representation and Its Psychological Interface
**Scope:** How written language, diagrams, symbols, numbers, tools, and other external structures interact with internal psychological processing.

### 1.1.50 Conceptual Representation
**Scope:** Internal representations used to organize and understand categories, concepts, relations, and abstract information.

### 1.1.51 Propositional Representation
**Scope:** Representational structures that encode relational or statement-like information.

### 1.1.52 Imagery and Analogical Representation
**Scope:** Representations that preserve useful structural or perceptual relationships through imagery or analogy.

### 1.1.53 Schemas
**Scope:** Organized knowledge structures that help interpret information, predict events, guide attention, and support action.

### 1.1.54 Scripts
**Scope:** Knowledge structures representing familiar sequences of events or actions.

### 1.1.55 Categories
**Scope:** Mental groupings used to organize objects, events, people, or ideas according to shared or functionally relevant properties.

### 1.1.56 Mental Models
**Scope:** Internal models used to represent how systems, situations, or causal relationships work.

### 1.1.57 Belief Representations
**Scope:** Internal representations treated by the person as describing what is true, likely, possible, or expected.

### 1.1.58 Self-Representations
**Scope:** Mental representations of one's characteristics, roles, abilities, history, goals, and identity.

### 1.1.59 Social Representations
**Scope:** Shared or socially shaped ways of representing people, groups, events, and social realities.

---

# PART VII — PSYCHOLOGICAL FUNCTIONS

### 1.1.60 Cognitive Functions
**Scope:** Functions involving information processing, reasoning, remembering, language, problem solving, and related cognition.

### 1.1.61 Affective Functions
**Scope:** Functions through which affective states influence perception, thought, motivation, learning, and action.

### 1.1.62 Motivational Functions
**Scope:** Functions that initiate, direct, sustain, and terminate goal-related activity.

### 1.1.63 Behavioral Functions
**Scope:** Functions involved in producing, coordinating, modifying, and terminating behavior.

### 1.1.64 Executive and Control Functions
**Scope:** Higher-order control processes that coordinate attention, goals, inhibition, monitoring, working information, and flexible behavior.

### 1.1.65 Adaptive Functions
**Scope:** Psychological functions that help a person respond effectively to changing demands and survive, function, learn, and pursue goals.

### 1.1.66 Integrative Functions
**Scope:** Functions that coordinate multiple psychological systems so that cognition, emotion, motivation, perception, memory, and action can operate together.

### 1.1.67 Social Functions
**Scope:** Psychological functions that support functioning within social environments, including interpreting others and coordinating behavior.

### 1.1.68 Communicative Functions
**Scope:** Psychological functions that support the production and interpretation of communicative signals and language.

---

# PART VIII — CONTROL, REGULATION, AND COORDINATION

### 1.1.69 Executive Control
**Scope:** Coordinating behavior in accordance with goals, rules, priorities, and changing conditions.

### 1.1.70 Inhibitory Control
**Scope:** Suppressing or withholding responses, impulses, or information when control requires it.

### 1.1.71 Cognitive Flexibility
**Scope:** Adjusting representations, strategies, attention, or behavior when conditions or goals change.

### 1.1.72 Goal Maintenance
**Scope:** Keeping relevant goals active enough to guide ongoing processing and behavior.

### 1.1.73 Conflict Monitoring and Resolution
**Scope:** Detecting competing goals, responses, rules, or representations and coordinating a response.

### 1.1.74 Error Monitoring and Correction
**Scope:** Detecting discrepancies between intended and actual performance and using them to modify behavior or processing.

### 1.1.75 Self-Regulation
**Scope:** Processes through which people influence their thoughts, emotions, motivation, and behavior in pursuit of goals.

### 1.1.76 Feedback
**Scope:** Information about current or previous states or outcomes that can modify future processing or behavior.

### 1.1.77 Feedforward Control
**Scope:** Adjustment based on anticipated conditions or expected consequences rather than only on detected errors after they occur.

### 1.1.78 Reciprocal Regulation
**Scope:** Mutual influence among psychological systems, where each can alter the operation of others.

---

# PART IX — MIND, BRAIN, BODY, AND BIOLOGICAL FOUNDATIONS

### 1.1.79 Mind–Brain Relationship
**Scope:** The relationship between psychological phenomena and brain activity, while avoiding the assumption that psychological concepts can always be reduced to single brain locations.

### 1.1.80 Neural Basis of Psychological Function
**Scope:** How neural systems support psychological processes.

### 1.1.81 Brain–Body Interaction
**Scope:** The two-way relationship between brain processes and bodily systems.

### 1.1.82 Embodied Psychological Function
**Scope:** How bodily states, actions, and bodily interaction with the environment can participate in psychological processes.

### 1.1.83 Biological Constraints on Psychological Function
**Scope:** Ways biological structure and physiology constrain, enable, or bias psychological processes.

### 1.1.84 Genetic Influences
**Scope:** How genetic variation can contribute to differences in psychological traits, processes, and behavior.

### 1.1.85 Environmental Influences
**Scope:** How physical, social, cultural, and experiential environments shape psychological development and functioning.

### 1.1.86 Developmental Influences
**Scope:** How psychological organization changes across development.

### 1.1.87 Gene–Environment Interaction
**Scope:** How genetic differences and environmental conditions jointly influence psychological outcomes.

### 1.1.88 Experience-Dependent Change
**Scope:** Changes in psychological and neural organization produced by experience and learning.

---

# PART X — FUNDAMENTAL PROPERTIES OF THE MIND

### 1.1.89 Integration
**Scope:** The capacity of different psychological processes to interact and contribute to coordinated functioning.

### 1.1.90 Differentiation
**Scope:** The organization of psychological functions into distinguishable processes or components.

### 1.1.91 Modularity
**Scope:** The degree to which functions can be described as partially specialized components. Modularity is treated as a scientific hypothesis/property, not as proof that the mind consists of completely isolated modules.

### 1.1.92 Distributed Processing
**Scope:** Processing carried by interacting systems rather than a single isolated mechanism.

### 1.1.93 Plasticity
**Scope:** The capacity of psychological and neural systems to change with development, learning, experience, and environmental demands.

### 1.1.94 Stability
**Scope:** Persistence of psychological organization, traits, representations, or patterns across time and conditions.

### 1.1.95 Flexibility
**Scope:** The capacity to change processing or behavior when goals, information, or environmental conditions change.

### 1.1.96 Rigidity
**Scope:** Reduced ability to modify established representations, strategies, or responses when change would be useful.

### 1.1.97 Automaticity
**Scope:** Processing or behavior that can occur with limited conscious control or attention after sufficient conditions, learning, or practice.

### 1.1.98 Controlled Processing
**Scope:** Processing that depends more heavily on deliberate attention, working resources, monitoring, or conscious regulation.

### 1.1.99 Parallel Processing
**Scope:** Simultaneous processing of different information streams or operations.

### 1.1.100 Serial Processing
**Scope:** Processing in which operations depend more strongly on an ordered sequence.

### 1.1.101 Emergence
**Scope:** The possibility that system-level psychological properties arise from interactions among simpler components rather than existing as isolated components themselves.

### 1.1.102 Self-Organization
**Scope:** The spontaneous development or stabilization of organized patterns through interactions within a complex system.

### 1.1.103 Redundancy and Degeneracy
**Scope:** Different mechanisms can sometimes support overlapping functions, while similar outcomes can sometimes arise from different underlying configurations.

### 1.1.104 Limited Capacity
**Scope:** The finite nature of many psychological processing resources and the consequences of competition for those resources.

### 1.1.105 Resource Dependence
**Scope:** The way psychological performance depends on available attention, working resources, energy, time, and other constraints.

### 1.1.106 State Dependence
**Scope:** The way psychological functioning changes according to current physiological, emotional, motivational, cognitive, and contextual state.

### 1.1.107 Context Sensitivity
**Scope:** The degree to which psychological functioning changes as the surrounding situation changes.

---

# PART XI — SYSTEM DYNAMICS

### 1.1.108 Dynamic Equilibrium
**Scope:** Maintenance of functional stability while internal and external conditions continue to change.

### 1.1.109 Adaptation
**Scope:** Changes in psychological functioning that improve fit between the person and changing demands, without assuming that every adaptation is beneficial.

### 1.1.110 Feedback Loops
**Scope:** Recurring causal cycles in which outputs or consequences influence later states or inputs.

### 1.1.111 Reciprocal Causation
**Scope:** Mutual causal influence among psychological processes, behavior, body, environment, and social conditions.

### 1.1.112 Cross-System Interaction
**Scope:** How cognitive, perceptual, emotional, motivational, memory, learning, self, social, and regulatory systems influence one another.

### 1.1.113 Person–Environment Interaction
**Scope:** Mutual influence between psychological characteristics and the environments in which people operate.

### 1.1.114 Psychological Coherence
**Scope:** The degree to which multiple psychological processes and representations form a sufficiently coordinated pattern for the person to function as an organized system.

### 1.1.115 Individual Variability
**Scope:** Stable and changing differences between people in psychological processes, states, capacities, and responses.

---

# PART XII — ARCHITECTURAL FRAMEWORKS

### 1.1.116 Systems Perspective
**Scope:** Viewing psychological functioning as an interacting system rather than as isolated variables.

### 1.1.117 Dynamic Systems Perspective
**Scope:** Viewing psychological organization as continuously changing through interactions among components, time, context, and feedback.

### 1.1.118 Biopsychosocial Perspective
**Scope:** Understanding psychological functioning through interacting biological, psychological, and social influences.

### 1.1.119 Levels of Analysis
**Scope:** Distinguishing explanatory levels such as neural, cognitive, psychological, behavioral, interpersonal, social, and environmental levels.

### 1.1.120 Micro-to-Macro Organization
**Scope:** How lower-level processes can combine into higher-level patterns and how higher-level conditions can constrain lower-level processes.

### 1.1.121 Person–Environment System
**Scope:** Treating the person and surrounding environment as interacting components of psychological functioning.

### 1.1.122 Human Functioning
**Scope:** The integrated operation of psychological systems that allows a person to perceive, think, feel, learn, regulate, decide, act, relate, and adapt.

# CANONICAL OWNERSHIP RULES FOR CHAPTER 1.1

1. Chapter 1.1 owns the **architecture and organization** of the human mind.
2. It does not become the detailed owner of every process merely because that process appears in the architecture.
3. Detailed perception belongs to the perception chapter.
4. Detailed attention belongs to the attention chapter.
5. Detailed memory belongs to the memory chapter.
6. Detailed learning belongs to the learning chapter.
7. Detailed judgment and decision-making belong to the appropriate cognition/decision chapters.
8. Detailed emotion belongs to the emotion chapter.
9. Detailed motivation belongs to the motivation chapter.
10. Detailed personality belongs to the personality chapter.
11. Detailed social psychology belongs to the social psychology chapter.
12. Formal named theories and models receive canonical ownership in System 10 unless they are being mentioned only as architectural context.
13. Historical theories must be clearly labeled as historical when they are not established contemporary scientific descriptions.
14. No topic is admitted merely because it sounds psychologically relevant. It must contribute to understanding the architecture, organization, operation, regulation, biological embedding, or system-level properties of the mind.
15. Synonyms and near-duplicates must be merged under one canonical owner unless there is a scientifically meaningful distinction.
16. Every topic must pass a duplicate-ownership audit against the remaining System 1 chapters and Systems 2–11.
